# Discrimination from Soft Signals: Dilemmas of Mimic Men

**Summary**

Anurag Srivastava · Riskcare Ltd., London · University of Reading

---

Social boundaries have persisted in spite of individual counter-examples, legal equality, and formal anti-discrimination instruments. Loury (2002) distinguishes "discrimination in contract" (formal, labour-market) from "discrimination in contact" (informal, social) and argues that the latter sustains racial inequality even after the former is removed. This paper extends Loury to explain how non-discriminatory social environments combined with profit incentives can together shape social discrimination in a market of soft signals.

When people encounter someone new, they read soft signals — accent, dress, phenotype, consumption patterns — and form beliefs about economic position. The paper argues that soft-signal inference is better represented by Jeffrey conditioning than by a Bayesian rule. Under Jeffrey conditioning, statistical updating does not revise a fixed group-level belief: a high-class member of a stigmatised group is reclassified as an exception, leaving the group stereotype untouched. The boundary re-asserts itself not through irrationality but through the inferential structure of soft-signal processing.

The discrimination mechanism has two sufficient statistics: marker salience (how visible group markers are) and the group prior (the sediment of economic history). Marker salience governs whether the revision fires; the group prior governs what it maps to. The instruments that affect each are structurally different and neither substitutes for the other. Profit-maximising platforms — restaurants, schools, rental markets, neighbourhoods — calibrate atmosphere to attract preferred compositions using legally invisible instruments. Anti-discrimination law addresses Loury's contracting channel directly, but cannot reach the contact channel of soft signals; the model formalises this distinction and shows that addressing the contact channel requires instruments operating on the intergenerational class distribution, not on individual acts of exclusion.

Raising intergenerational mobility above the critical threshold γ* permanently eliminates the closure trap through a saddle-node bifurcation; above γ*, the group converges from any starting position without requiring integration. Below γ*, integration can shift a cohort above the tipping point, but the trap regenerates for subsequent cohorts. The two instruments are complements in this sub-threshold regime: integration manages cohort positions while mobility investment pushes γ toward γ*. The model generates a power-law prediction for convergence dynamics near the bifurcation threshold, testable against multigenerational mobility data.
