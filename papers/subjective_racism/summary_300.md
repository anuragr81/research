# Discrimination in a Market of Soft Signals: The "Mimic Men" Dilemma

**Summary**

Anurag Srivastava · Riskcare Ltd., London · University of Reading

---

The persistence of social prejudices remains divisive and controversial. Despite decades of legal equality, group-level economic gaps remain persistent — the stagnation of racial earnings convergence since the 1970s despite continued individual advancement is a canonical example (Loury 2002, Bowles et al. 2014). The paper delves into the premise that while members of a stigmatised group may invest in host-group markers — credentials, accent, dress, consumption patterns — these investments may not change the group-level position significantly. We ask first why individual investments fail to deliver group-level mobility, and second under what structural conditions integration programmes or affirmative actions durably eliminate the economic gap.

The model extends Loury's (2002) distinction between "discrimination in contract" (formal, labour-market) and "discrimination in contact" (informal, social). The paper argues that a mechanism where agents read soft signals — accent, phenotype, neighbourhood, consumption — and update beliefs about economic position is better represented by a general Jeffrey conditioning than by standard Bayesian updating. This general case allows the observer to reclassify the individual without revising the group-level prior. The general equilibrium that emerges allows a high-class member of a stigmatised group to be absorbed as an exception and the group stereotype to persist. The goal of this paper is not to identify the psychological mechanism governing this behaviour but to investigate welfare outcomes using a normative approach with an updating rule operating on soft evidence.

The first question is answered by the rigidity condition: individual marker investment produces personal reclassification but cannot aggregate into group prior revision, regardless of volume. Thus the compliance route is not merely ineffective at the group level — it is actively harmful, channelling resources away from the collective mobility investment that could address the trap. The second question is answered by the bifurcation result: there exists a critical threshold of intergenerational mobility above which the closure trap is permanently eliminated. Prior threshold results for the contracting channel (Coate and Loury 1993, Chaudhuri and Sethi 2008, Bowles et al. 2014) do not deal with this contact channel. The paper finds that below the threshold, integration and mobility investment are complements — integration manages cohort positions while mobility investment pushes toward the threshold. Above it, mobility is sufficient on its own.
