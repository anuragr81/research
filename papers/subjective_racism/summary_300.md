# Discrimination from Soft Signals: Dilemmas of Mimic Men

**300-Word Summary**

Anurag Srivastava · Riskcare Ltd., London · University of Reading

---

The post-colonial literary tradition has identified a structural feature of discrimination that economics has not formalised: individual assimilation is individually rewarded and collectively futile. The mimic man invests in the host group's markers and is personally reclassified. His group is not. This paper provides the formal mechanism.

When people encounter someone they have not met before, they read soft signals — accent, dress, phenotype, consumption patterns — and form beliefs about that person's economic position. The inference rule governing this process is Jeffrey conditioning, which revises beliefs about group membership while holding group-level class distributions fixed. Individual counter-examples do not revise group beliefs. The stereotype persists as a theorem, not an assumption.

The model's two sufficient statistics correspond to the spatial and temporal dimensions of social observation. Marker salience (space) governs whether the Jeffrey revision fires: mutable markers (accent, dress) can converge to host norms, while immutable phenotypic markers sustain attribution indefinitely. The group prior (time) governs what the revision maps to: the sediment of the group's economic history. Sustained discrimination requires both channels to be active — spatial identifiability and temporal disadvantage. This explains why phenotypic proximity dominates conflict history in determining integration speed: the Irish in England integrated rapidly despite severe historical conflict because low marker salience meant the Jeffrey revision barely fired.

The critical intergenerational mobility threshold required to eliminate the closure trap is strictly increasing in marker salience. Individual compliance with host-group markers produces personal reclassification without group-level liberation — and for high-salience groups, compliance cannot modify immutable markers, making the promise doubly false. When anti-discrimination law changes suddenly, it addresses neither spatial observations nor temporal priors: discrimination redirects rather than disappears.

The welfare loss includes an irreducible component. The only durable formal remedy operates on intergenerational economic mobility itself.
