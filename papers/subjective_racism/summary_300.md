# Discrimination from Soft Signals: Dilemmas of Mimic Men

**300-Word Summary**

Anurag Srivastava · Riskcare Ltd., London · University of Reading

---

The post-colonial literary tradition has identified a structural feature of discrimination that economics has not formalised: individual assimilation is individually rewarded and collectively futile. The mimic man invests in the host group's markers and is personally reclassified. His group is not. This paper provides the formal mechanism.

When people encounter someone new, they read soft signals — accent, dress, phenotype, consumption patterns — and form beliefs about that person's economic position. The inference rule governing this process is Jeffrey conditioning, which revises beliefs about group membership while holding group-level class distributions fixed. Individual counter-examples do not revise group beliefs. The stereotype persists as a theorem.

The model's two sufficient statistics correspond to spatial and temporal dimensions of social observation. Marker salience (space) governs whether the Jeffrey revision fires: mutable markers can converge to host norms, while immutable phenotypic markers sustain attribution indefinitely. The group prior (time) governs what the revision maps to. Sustained discrimination requires both — spatial identifiability and temporal disadvantage. This explains why phenotypic proximity dominates conflict history in determining integration speed.

The model shows that individual compliance is worse than futile. Because the highest-class individuals are most able to "pass," their exit actively worsens the remaining group's prior through adverse selection. When authorities forcibly suppress markers — consumption signalling under communism, dress codes in conservative societies — discrimination reallocates to unsuppressed channels, and when suppression lifts, stratification snaps back immediately unless the group prior has crossed the tipping point during suppression.

The critical intergenerational mobility threshold is strictly increasing in marker salience. Spatial interventions (reducing marker visibility) and temporal interventions (raising mobility) are categorically different and cannot substitute for each other. The only durable formal remedy operates on intergenerational economic mobility itself.
