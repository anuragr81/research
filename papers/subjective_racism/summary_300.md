# Discrimination from Soft Signals: Dilemmas of Mimic Men

**Summary**

Anurag Srivastava · Riskcare Ltd., London · University of Reading

---

Social boundaries seem to have persisted in spite of individual counter-examples, legal equality, and formal anti-discrimination instruments. A prominent economic framework for understanding this persistence is Loury (2002), which distinguishes "discrimination in contract" (formal, labour-market) from "discrimination in contact" (informal, social) and argues that the latter sustains racial inequality even after the former is removed. The current paper extends this framework to explain how non-discriminatory social environments amidst profit incentives could together shape social discrimination in a market of soft signals.

When people encounter someone new, they read soft signals — accent, dress, phenotype, consumption patterns — and form beliefs about economic position. These judgments are not hard boundaries but are still relevant for social interaction. For this reason, the updating rule that governs soft-signal inference is better represented with Jeffrey conditioning rather than a Bayesian rule. Under this rule, the statistical updating does not revise a fixed group-level belief: a high-class member of a stigmatised group is reclassified as an exception, leaving the group stereotype untouched. The boundary re-asserts itself not through irrationality but through the inferential structure of soft-signal processing.

The discrimination mechanism in the model has two sufficient statistics: marker salience (how visible group markers are) and the group prior (the sediment of economic history). Marker salience governs whether the revision fires; the group prior governs what it maps to. Because they enter the mechanism at different points, the instruments that affect each are structurally different and neither can compensate for the other. Reducing marker visibility — whether through individual assimilation or collective policy such as anonymisation — leaves the group prior untouched. Raising intergenerational mobility shifts the group prior but does not affect marker visibility. These effects play into the behaviour of profit-maximising platforms — restaurants, schools, rental markets, neighbourhoods — which calibrate atmosphere to attract preferred compositions using legally invisible instruments. It is possible therefore that stricter anti-discrimination law may constrain formal channels while simultaneously raising the return to informal ones, intensifying a shadow market that makes boundaries self-reinforcing.

The limits of anti-discriminatory policies in areas where the market of soft signals dominates are thus relevant for policy. Anti-discrimination law alone may redirect discrimination without addressing the prior that generates it. Assimilation incentives may channel resources toward marker investment and away from the mobility investment that would eliminate the trap. Raising intergenerational mobility above the critical threshold γ* permanently eliminates the closure trap through saddle-node bifurcation; above γ*, the group converges from any starting position without requiring integration. Below γ*, integration can shift a cohort above the tipping point, but the trap regenerates for subsequent cohorts. The two instruments are complements in this sub-threshold regime: integration manages cohort positions while mobility investment pushes γ toward γ*. The model generates a power-law prediction for convergence dynamics near the bifurcation threshold, testable against multigenerational mobility data.
