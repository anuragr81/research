# Social Discrimination as Informationally Mediated Closure

**Executive Summary**

Anurag Srivastava · Riskcare Ltd., London · University of Reading

---

When people decide whether to engage socially with someone they have just encountered, they do not have access to that person's economic record. They have access to soft signals: accent, dress, name, neighbourhood, consumption patterns. These signals are ambiguous — they suggest economic position without determining it. The question this paper asks is: what is the correct formal inference rule for updating beliefs from soft social signals, and what does that rule imply for discrimination, social closure, and economic mobility across generations?

The answer is that the correct rule is not standard Bayesian updating. When a signal is soft, the appropriate framework is one developed by the philosopher Richard Jeffrey, known as probability kinematics. Jeffrey conditioning is the unique rule that revises the marginal distribution over social groups while holding fixed the group-level beliefs about economic class. The consequence is that individual counter-examples cannot update group stereotypes. A high-class member of a historically disadvantaged group does not revise what observers believe about the group; he is attributed to a different group, or treated as an exception to a rule that remains intact. This emerges as a theorem of the updating rule, not as an assumption about irrationality or prejudice. The normative urgency of addressing this mechanism — that discrimination in informal social contact causes serious and lasting harm — is not in question; what is in question is the formal structure of the mechanism.

The starting point of the model is an observation, not a result: economic class generates social clustering. The model proves something different and stronger. The Jeffrey updating rule generates a self-sustaining closure trap as a stable equilibrium outcome even in a society with no racial divisions. The trap is path-dependent, compounds across generations, and is resistant to individual counter-examples by construction. Racial discrimination enters as a perturbation of this already-operating equilibrium — a group whose economic history is encoded as a low prior — not as a separate phenomenon requiring separate explanation. The model takes no position on the historical causal ordering: whether class structures preceded racial categorisation or racial exploitation created class stratification. The formal mechanism operates identically under either account.

The model establishes a threshold level of intergenerational economic mobility below which the closure trap persists permanently and above which it is eliminated through a structural phase transition. Near this threshold, the number of generations required for integration diverges: a group improving its economic position but not yet above the critical threshold may require many generations to escape, and any temporary reversal restores the trap. This provides a formal explanation for why apparent progress is so often followed by reversion, and why the reversion is a structural property of the equilibrium, not a failure of will. There are two qualitatively different policy instruments and they are not substitutes. Temporary forced integration shifts one cohort above the tipping point but leaves the trap's structure intact. Sustained investments in intergenerational mobility raise the group's transition rate above the structural threshold and eliminate the trap permanently.

A central result concerns the relationship between individual compliance and group liberation. Every historical closure system has offered a compliance route — the promise that individuals who sufficiently adopt the host group's markers will be reclassified and eventually integrated. The colonial évolué, the converso, and the anglicised colonial subject all occupied this position. Translated into the model's language, the compliance promise is a claim that the operative updating rule is Bayesian: individual compliance updates the group prior, and enough counter-examples dissolve the trap. The model shows this promise is structurally false when the updating rule is Jeffrey. Individual compliance does produce personal reclassification — the observer shifts their assessment of this individual — but the rigidity condition prevents compliance from aggregating into group prior revision. No volume of individual compliance can drive the group prior across the tipping point.

This has two consequences that are difficult to reconcile without the formal framework but follow directly from it. The first is that individual compliance often did produce genuine personal mobility: the évolué did achieve a different social position. The second is that this personal mobility did not translate into group-level liberation: the group prior moved negligibly, the closure trap persisted, and subsequent generations began from the same baseline. The Jeffrey architecture resolves the paradox: both outcomes are immediate consequences of the rigidity condition. The compliant individual is reclassified; the group is not. The compliance route is not merely ineffective at the group level — it is structurally harmful, because it channels the highest-investing individuals into personal reclassification, depleting the group of those most capable of collective organisation and diverting effort from the structural mobility that would raise the group's transition rate above the critical threshold. The most diagnostically powerful historical test is the case of colonised soldiers who fought on behalf of the colonial power against their own people of origin — compliance at the limit — without achieving group prior revision.

The model addresses formal institutions — restaurants, schools, neighbourhoods — that are legally open to all but produce discriminatory compositions through legally invisible means. A profit-maximising institution optimises its atmosphere to attract the economic composition its clients prefer, using pricing calibrated by class, location creating transport barriers, and social codes that make certain guests uncomfortable. The racial correlation of the resulting clientele is a consequence of this optimisation, not an input. The institution is not discriminating by race; it is optimising a belief architecture that produces racial sorting because race and economic history are correlated in the ambient group prior.

Stricter anti-discrimination law does not resolve this and amplifies one dimension of it. The analysis shows formally that tighter legal constraints on formal admission instruments raise the return to informal discrimination networks: word of mouth among insiders, coded language in reviews, selective signals through social networks. The value of being connected to this informal information rises when formal channels are constrained, because the informed agent can still identify the institution producing the desired social composition while the uninformed agent increasingly cannot. This is not an argument against anti-discrimination law; it is an argument that the law alone is insufficient and must be paired with interventions that address economic mobility directly. As a stigmatised group's purchasing power grows, market forces generate voluntary partial integration — landing the group in a structural zone of platform access without social integration, visible inclusion without the underlying belief revision that integration would require. Market forces cannot push the group past the social tipping point; that requires the mobility programme.

The model also accounts for a range of discrimination patterns in everyday interaction that existing frameworks cannot explain. A delivery driver who credential-checks a recipient because a white-sounding name conflicts with an appearance associated with a disadvantaged group is performing an inference operation, not expressing prejudice. Restaurant staff who intervene when a white woman sits next to a black man are applying a group prior that makes voluntary cross-group proximity implausible in that setting. Fashion and beauty standards that incorporate or reject foreign influences are responding to the economic implications those influences carry in the ambient group prior, not to intrinsic aesthetic merit. All of these are instances of the same mechanism — a prior, a soft signal, and an updating rule that is rigid to individual revision — operating in contexts where adverse selection has no purchase because there is no repeated game and no self-selection dynamic.

When race and gender markers compound in institutional settings — harassment accusations, credibility assessments, disciplinary proceedings — the resulting distortion exceeds what either dimension produces alone. The observer simultaneously conducts two Jeffrey revisions: one over the accused's character using race-correlated markers, another over the accuser's credibility using gender-and-race-correlated markers. Both are governed by the rigidity condition, and both are independently anchored to group priors rather than case-specific evidence. The compounded distortion is non-additive: it is the joint product of two independently rigid revisions reinforcing each other. A Black woman reporting harassment by a white man faces a different compounding, with her race-correlated credibility prior lower and her gender-correlated credibility prior higher — precisely the non-additive interaction pattern that the intersectionality literature identifies empirically, here given a formal microfoundation. Anti-discrimination policy targeting one dimension alone leaves the interaction term untouched.

The welfare loss from discrimination decomposes into four components: blocked compatible transactions, rigid priors that never correct themselves, suppressed intergenerational mobility compounding across generations, and the shadow information market cost. Three respond to specific instruments. The informational loss is structurally irreducible within the class of policies that leave the soft-signal character of social interaction intact. This characterises the limits of formal instruments, not a verdict on the world. Social movements and long-run cultural shifts that alter the signal structure itself could reduce this floor.

The paper positions itself as a formal microfoundation for the programme outlined by Glenn Loury, showing that Loury's framework is a limiting special case. The key addition is the rigidity condition: in Loury's informal account, individual counter-examples do not erode stereotypes; here that property is derived as a theorem. The model does not advise the oppressed on how to conduct their struggles and does not pass normative judgement on whether the residual welfare loss is acceptable. Its contribution is positive-analytical: given that social interaction is soft-signal-mediated and Jeffrey conditioning is the theoretically natural updating rule, these are the structural properties of discrimination equilibria, these are the limits of specific formal instruments, and this is why the compliance route that every closure system has offered is structurally incapable of delivering what it promises.
