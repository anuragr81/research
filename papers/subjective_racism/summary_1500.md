# Discrimination from Soft Signals: Dilemmas of Mimic Men

**Executive Summary**

Anurag Srivastava · Riskcare Ltd., London · University of Reading

---

The post-colonial literary tradition has repeatedly identified a structural feature of discrimination that economics has not formalised. Naipaul's Ralph Singh, in *The Mimic Men*, invests everything in the markers of the colonial metropole and achieves personal admission to its institutions. He is reclassified. His group is not. Fanon's colonised subject discovers that the white mask does not revise the white gaze. Coetzee's post-apartheid figures navigate a world whose legal structure has changed overnight while its inferential architecture has not. The dilemma these writers identify is precise: individual assimilation is individually rewarded and collectively futile. This paper provides the formal mechanism.

When people decide whether to engage socially with someone they have just encountered, they do not have access to that person's economic record. They have access to soft signals: accent, dress, name, neighbourhood, consumption patterns, phenotype. These signals are ambiguous — they suggest economic position without determining it. The question this paper asks is: what is the correct formal inference rule for updating beliefs from soft social signals, and what does that rule imply for discrimination, social closure, and economic mobility across generations?

The answer is that the correct rule is not standard Bayesian updating. When a signal is soft, the appropriate framework is Jeffrey conditioning — probability kinematics developed by the philosopher Richard Jeffrey. Jeffrey conditioning revises the marginal distribution over social groups while holding fixed the group-level beliefs about economic class. The consequence is that individual counter-examples cannot update group stereotypes. A high-class member of a historically disadvantaged group does not revise what observers believe about the group; he is attributed to a different group, or treated as an exception to a rule that remains intact. This emerges as a theorem of the updating rule, not as an assumption about irrationality or prejudice.

The starting point of the model is an observation, not a result: economic class generates social clustering. The model proves something different and stronger. The Jeffrey updating rule generates a self-sustaining closure trap as a stable equilibrium outcome even in a society with no racial divisions. The trap is path-dependent, compounds across generations, and is resistant to individual counter-examples by construction. Racial discrimination enters as a perturbation of this already-operating equilibrium — a group whose economic history is encoded as a low prior — not as a separate phenomenon requiring separate explanation.

The model's two sufficient statistics correspond to the spatial and temporal dimensions of social observation. Marker salience (the spatial dimension) governs the strength of the Jeffrey revision — the probability that the observer attributes the encountered individual to the out-group upon observing their markers. Not all markers are equally visible or equally persistent. Accent and dress are mutable: they can converge to host-group norms within a generation. Phenotypic markers — skin colour, facial structure — are immutable. Marker salience is a property of the marker-observer interaction, not an intrinsic property of the group: the same individual has high salience in one host environment and zero in another. The group prior (the temporal dimension) is the sediment of the group's economic history across generations — what the Jeffrey revision maps to once it fires. Every soft signal that enters the model is either a spatial property observable at the encounter or encodes temporal information accumulated across history. The model's central result — that sustained discrimination requires both channels to be active — can therefore be restated: sustained discrimination requires both spatial identifiability and temporal disadvantage. Spatial interventions (reducing marker visibility) and temporal interventions (raising intergenerational mobility) are categorically different and cannot substitute for each other.

This resolves a puzzle that the base model, operating on economic history alone, cannot address. The Irish in England had a history of famine, colonial extraction, partition, and armed conflict — comparable to or worse than many Commonwealth immigrant groups. Yet Irish immigrants integrated within two generations, while Indian and Kenyan immigrants — from countries with which England's relationship was in some respects less conflict-ridden — remained in the closure trap under the same institutional environment. The marker salience framework explains why: the Irish had low phenotypic salience (visually similar to the host population, accent mutable), so the Jeffrey revision fired weakly and integration occurred despite poor economic history. Indian and Kenyan immigrants had high phenotypic salience (immutable markers), so the Jeffrey revision fired persistently regardless of individual economic position.

The model establishes a threshold level of intergenerational economic mobility below which the closure trap persists permanently and above which it is eliminated through a saddle-node bifurcation. Near this threshold, the number of generations required for integration diverges. The marker salience extension sharpens this: the threshold itself is strictly increasing in marker salience, so groups with immutable markers need more intergenerational mobility to achieve integration than groups with mutable markers. There are two qualitatively different policy instruments and they are not substitutes. Temporary forced integration shifts one cohort above the tipping point but leaves the trap's structure intact. Sustained investments in intergenerational mobility raise the group's transition rate above the structural threshold and eliminate the trap permanently.

A central result concerns the relationship between individual compliance and group liberation. Every historical closure system has offered a compliance route — the promise that individuals who sufficiently adopt the host group's markers will be reclassified and eventually integrated. The colonial évolué, the converso, and Naipaul's mimic man all occupied this position. The model shows this promise is structurally false when the updating rule is Jeffrey: individual compliance produces personal reclassification but the rigidity condition prevents compliance from aggregating into group prior revision. For low-salience groups, compliance can reduce the individual's marker visibility toward zero — the Irish immigrant who loses the accent ceases to be identified as out-group. For high-salience groups, compliance can modify mutable markers while leaving the immutable phenotypic component unchanged: the évolué who adopts French language, dress, and cultural practice is still identified as out-group on sight. The compliance promise was structurally false on both counts. Moreover, the compliance route is not merely ineffective at the group level — it is actively harmful, because it channels the highest-investing individuals into personal reclassification, depleting the group of those most capable of collective organisation.

The space-time sufficiency structure generates a cross-country prediction. The UK has a deep pre-existing class closure trap with a rich temporal marker space — accent, school, dress encode class history with high precision — so the temporal dimension does more of the discriminatory work. The US has a thinner temporal marker space, so the spatial (racial) dimension carries more of the informational load. A high-class foreigner from a racially stigmatised group should experience more racial friction in the US and more class friction in the UK. The shadow information market is organised around temporal signals in the UK (the "old boy network") and spatial signals in the US.

The model addresses formal institutions — restaurants, schools, neighbourhoods — that are legally open to all but produce discriminatory compositions through legally invisible means. When anti-discrimination law changes suddenly while the belief architecture remains fixed, it addresses neither the spatial observations nor the temporal priors on which the mechanism operates. The shadow market intensifies, discrimination redirects from formal to informal channels, and the result is the tokenism equilibrium: visible inclusion without belief revision. Without simultaneous investment in intergenerational mobility, sudden legal change produces an outcome that erodes political support for both instruments.

The welfare loss includes a component that no formal policy instrument can eliminate while social interaction relies on soft signals. The individually rational response to the closure trap — marker investment, compliance, personal reclassification — is collectively self-defeating. How a dispersed and disadvantaged group coordinates on collective mobility investment in the presence of this private incentive to defect into compliance is a coordination problem the model identifies but does not resolve — and one that connects naturally to the collective action literature. The mimic man is not a literary conceit but an equilibrium outcome: the Jeffrey architecture ensures that individual exceptions do not aggregate into group prior revision, and the compliance route that every closure system has offered is structurally incapable of delivering what it promises.
