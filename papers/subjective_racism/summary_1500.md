# Discrimination from Soft Signals: Dilemmas of Mimic Men

**Executive Summary**

Anurag Srivastava · Riskcare Ltd., London · University of Reading

---

The post-colonial literary tradition has repeatedly identified a structural feature of discrimination that economics has not formalised. Naipaul's Ralph Singh, in *The Mimic Men* (1967), invests everything in the markers of the colonial metropole and achieves personal admission to its institutions. He is reclassified. His group is not. Fanon's colonised subject discovers that the white mask does not revise the white gaze. Coetzee's post-apartheid figures navigate a world whose legal structure has changed overnight while its inferential architecture has not. The dilemma these writers identify is precise: individual assimilation is individually rewarded and collectively futile. This paper provides the formal mechanism.

When people decide whether to engage socially with someone they have just encountered, they do not have access to that person's economic record. They have access to soft signals: accent, dress, name, neighbourhood, consumption patterns, phenotype. The question this paper asks is: what is the correct formal inference rule for updating beliefs from soft social signals, and what does that rule imply for discrimination, social closure, and economic mobility across generations?

The answer is that the correct rule is not standard Bayesian updating. When a signal is soft, the appropriate framework is Jeffrey conditioning — probability kinematics developed by the philosopher Richard Jeffrey. Jeffrey conditioning revises the marginal distribution over social groups while holding fixed the group-level beliefs about economic class. The consequence is that individual counter-examples cannot update group stereotypes. A high-class member of a historically disadvantaged group does not revise what observers believe about the group; he is attributed to a different group, or treated as an exception to a rule that remains intact. This emerges as a theorem of the updating rule, not as an assumption about irrationality or prejudice.

The model's two sufficient statistics correspond to the spatial and temporal dimensions of social observation. Marker salience (the spatial dimension) governs the strength of the Jeffrey revision — the probability that the observer attributes the encountered individual to the out-group upon observing their markers. Not all markers are equally visible or persistent. Accent and dress are mutable: they can converge to host-group norms within a generation. Phenotypic markers — skin colour, facial structure — are immutable. Marker salience is a property of the marker-observer interaction, not an intrinsic property of the group. The group prior (the temporal dimension) is the sediment of the group's economic history across generations — what the Jeffrey revision maps to once it fires. Sustained discrimination requires both channels to be active: spatial identifiability and temporal disadvantage. Spatial interventions (reducing marker visibility) and temporal interventions (raising intergenerational mobility) are categorically different and cannot substitute for each other.

The model establishes a threshold level of intergenerational economic mobility below which the closure trap persists permanently and above which it is eliminated through a saddle-node bifurcation. The threshold is strictly increasing in marker salience, so groups with immutable markers need more intergenerational mobility to achieve integration than groups with mutable markers. This resolves a puzzle the base model cannot address: the Irish in England had a history of famine, colonial extraction, and armed conflict comparable to many Commonwealth immigrant groups, yet integrated within two generations. The marker salience framework explains why — low phenotypic salience meant the Jeffrey revision fired weakly, and integration occurred despite poor economic history. Indian and Kenyan immigrants, with high phenotypic salience, remained trapped under the same institutional environment.

The compliance analysis goes beyond the rigidity condition. The model shows that individual "passing" — reducing one's personal marker salience through name changes, accent modification, cultural adoption — is not merely futile at the group level but actively harmful. Because the individuals most able to pass are positively selected (highest class, lowest natural salience), their departure lowers the mean class of the remaining visible group through adverse selection. The mimic man's escape worsens the group prior for those he leaves behind. This sharpens the central dilemma: the individually rational compliance strategy is collectively destructive, not just collectively ineffective.

When authorities forcibly suppress markers — consumption signalling under communism, female appearance under conservative dress codes — the model predicts three consequences. First, discrimination reallocates to unsuppressed markers: party membership, family connections, and behavioural conformity substitute for the suppressed consumption signals. Partial suppression is marker reallocation, not elimination. Second, the mimic man's dilemma is unchanged: the operative updating rule is still Jeffrey, and compliance with the reduced marker set still fails to revise the group prior. Third, when suppression is lifted, the accumulated temporal pressure — the group prior has not converged during the suppression period — meets a suddenly expanded marker space. Stratification snaps back immediately unless the group prior has crossed the tipping point during suppression. The post-Soviet explosion of conspicuous consumption and the rapid re-stratification of Central European societies in the 1990s are consistent with this prediction.

The model addresses formal institutions — restaurants, schools, neighbourhoods — that are legally open to all but produce discriminatory compositions through legally invisible means. When anti-discrimination law changes suddenly, it addresses neither the spatial observations nor the temporal priors on which the mechanism operates. The shadow market intensifies, discrimination redirects from formal to informal channels, and the result is visible inclusion without belief revision. Gentrification illustrates this at the neighbourhood level: the platform's atmosphere vector is optimised to attract higher-class residents, existing residents face the tokenism equilibrium, and anti-displacement policies constrain formal instruments while shadow mechanisms intensify.

The space-time sufficiency structure illuminates several further phenomena. East Asian immigrant communities in the US and UK occupy a distinctive parameter region — high marker salience combined with high economic position — where the salience effect reverses sign: the group prior is non-threatening, producing economic integration without proportional social integration (the "bamboo ceiling"). Language revival movements present the converse puzzle: a suppressed group deliberately increasing its own marker salience. The model predicts this is rational only when the group's economic position is already above the tipping point, so the closure trap does not bind. Anti-colonial movements represent the most important historical examples of collective coordination on mobility investment rather than individual compliance. Movements that rejected the compliance route and invested in structural economic independence — Nehruvian import substitution, Nyerere's ujamaa — pursued the model's correct strategy. Movements that maintained the compliance channel produced the tokenism equilibrium at the national scale. Where the anti-colonial narrative persists (Iran after 1979), the model predicts it functions as a coordination device preventing individually rational defection into compliance, and will dissolve endogenously when the group prior crosses the tipping point.

The model generates a distinctive empirical prediction: escape time from the closure trap diverges as a power law near the bifurcation threshold, producing non-linear convergence dynamics observationally distinguishable from the exponential convergence predicted by standard linear mobility models. The welfare loss includes a component that no formal policy instrument can eliminate while social interaction relies on soft signals. How a dispersed and disadvantaged group coordinates on collective mobility investment in the presence of a private incentive to defect into compliance is a coordination problem the model identifies but does not resolve — and one that connects naturally to the collective action literature. The mimic man is not a literary conceit but an equilibrium outcome.
