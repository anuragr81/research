\begin{abstract}
The effect on individual judgment of the sequence in which evidence arrives has been of interest in many experimental studies. The current paper studies evaluators who read two correlated soft
cues in either sequence, updating coherently by Jeffrey conditioning, and compares their beliefs and decisions against the sequence-free Bayesian benchmark. It then asks which summaries of those
beliefs and decisions register the resulting sequence effects in the population aggregate. We argue that since the two cue-sequences displace a belief only within a two-dimensional plane, an arbitrary statistic is an order of magnitude closer to the benchmark in aggregate exactly when its sensitivity with respect to belief is flat along the plane. Thus while measures of the believed association and the average value lost through decisions differing from the benchmark stay close, two quantities---the average marginal probability and the share of the population whose decision the reading sequence changes---differ from the benchmark both for the individual and in the aggregate. The implication of these findings for audits is that an aggregate conforming to the benchmark is no evidence that reading sequence did not affect the decisions taken.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

That the sequence in which evidence arrives can move the final judgment is among the oldest findings in the study of impression formation \citep{Asch1946} but the mechanics of how sequence dependence may carry over in aggregate statistics are poorly understood. While the question does seem to have been addressed one statistic and one setting at a time, there seems no general account for whether sequence dependence matters in the population aggregate or not. The current paper follows the belief-updating literature since \citet{Asch1946} that accounts for complex judgment behaviour using simple processing models \citep{HogarthEinhorn1992} and explores how the sequence of evidence for two cues matters for average beliefs about correlated attributes in the population as well as the decision that arise from them. Using Jeffrey conditioning as a coherent updating mechanism for impression-formation, the paper finds that while aggregate believed marginal probabilities deviate from the benchmark at the same first order as individual beliefs do, the aggregate believed association between attributes stays close to the benchmark. The paper also pins down what really makes an arbitrary smooth statistic remain first-order in the aggregate and what makes it remain closer to the sequence-independent Bayesian updating mechanism.

We use Jeffrey updating mechanism in the paper as a coherent mechanism with sequence-dependence properties that is pertinent for situations where evidence is soft \citep{Jeffrey1983}. A typical scenario that we use to explain the aggregation problem is that of a hiring panel working through a candidate's file on the basis of two pieces of evidence that bear on the decision---i) a credential that speaks to competence, and ii) the tone of a reference letter that speaks
to trustworthiness. Both are soft evidence in the sense of \citet{Jeffrey1983} so that neither is decisive but each leaves an impression. While the mechanism can extend to higher number of cues, we focus on the sequence dependence in a situation where one panel may read
the credential first and the letter second and an otherwise identical panel may read them in reverse. If the two attributes are believed to go together at all, the two panels finish with different beliefs about the same candidate---and, if the candidate sits near the panel's
threshold, with different decisions. In the context of soft evidence the stake is real for the individual in the sense that identical evidence can license
different decisions, with the difference set by arrival sequence rather than by anything the evidence contains. Whether sequence matters or not in the aggregate is the question that the current paper answers. The answer turns out to depend on which aggregate is taken. Four quantities are natural for an outside observer to compute: the average believed association between the two attributes, the average believed marginal probability for one attribute alone, the share of evaluators whose decision the reading sequence changes, and the surplus foregone when those decisions go the other way. As the rest of the paper elaborates, these do not all share a common fate.

There are two caveats worth stating in the setup that follows. First, the sequence of cues is not relevant when the evidence is hard and the updating is Bayesian. The sequence-relevance arises when the evidence is soft and when it can be assumed that impressions arrive as revised credences, not as likelihoods. The panelist comes to feel the candidate competent, without that feeling arising from any known
likelihood ratio---indicating how many more times the credential is more probable under competence than in its absence. For input of that kind, strict conditionalisation is simply not available, i.e., there is no certifiable proposition to condition on. As a mechanism that holds conditionals fixed, Jeffrey updating is the unique coherent revision for credence delivered on cue's partition and is equivalent to making the minimal change of the prior consistent with the delivered marginal \citep{DiaconisZabell1982}. If the strength with which the two traits are believed to go together were represented with a prior covariance $c$, then reversal or sequence shifts the posterior as well as any threshold decision it drives before any cue arrives in proportion to $c$ (see  \citet{Field1978} for how Jeffrey updates are asymmetric in the delivered-credence parametrisation and \citet{Wagner2002} for how they commute when their inputs are held fixed in Bayes-factor form). Second, the sequence effect is not forced by delivered-credence inputs as such. What produces the sequence effect is the sequential, myopic structure that makes Jeffrey conditioning the coherent response to a delivered credence in the first place. Holding one attribute's conditional fixed while resetting the other's marginal is what is licensed by the delivered credence \citep{Domotor1980}. 

The answer provided by the paper to the question of how individual sequence-dependence passes to the population is twofold. First, it establishes that while the individual diverges from the benchmark at first order in $c$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:A2}), the average believed association between attributes
(Proposition~\ref{prop:C1}) and the surplus-weighted loss of the population's decisions
(Theorem~\ref{thm:B2}) are second order. Second, it characterises exactly which
aggregate statistics can register the phenomenon at its own order. For every interior mixture of reading sequences and across an open set of priors, a smooth statistic of the average belief is
second order if and only if its differential at independence is proportional to the differential of the believed cross-attribute association (Proposition~\ref{prop:C3}). The condition is necessary as well as sufficient, so the protected
statistics (i.e.\ those that become second order) are the local dependence measures and surplus-weighted loss (for the threshold decision). The clear implication of the finding is that what an audit can answer depends on which statistic is aggregated rather than on how many evaluators are averaged over \footnote{What we consider as association in the paper is one that is held by an evaluator rather than one that is exhibited by the world. In other words, the
prior covariance $c$ is a feature of a panel's belief about a single candidate, not a frequency among candidates that the panels are estimating.}.

The paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:setup} sets up the model and the benchmark.
Section~\ref{sec:literature} relates the paper to the literature.
Section~\ref{sec:individual} establishes the individual-level results, exact immunity on a
single cue and first-order divergence on two. Section~\ref{sec:aggregation} asks which of these carry over into the population, namely the
second-order surplus-weighted loss, the decoupling of the believed cross-attribute association, the
characterisation of the protected class, and the criterion split.
Section~\ref{sec:scope} states the scope of results and limitations that would break the results. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} discusses the key takeaways from the results. Proofs not discussed in the main text are provided in \ref{app:proofs}.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Decision problem and surplus criterion}
\label{sec:decision}

Two binary attributes $(A,B)\in\{0,1\}^{2}$---competence and trustworthiness, say---are each
present or absent, so a belief assigns probability to the four configurations at once. It is thus a
\emph{joint law}, a single distribution over the pairs $(i,j)$---a point in the probability
simplex $\Delta^{3}$---rather than a pair of one-attribute distributions \anchor{Setup}. The two marginal distributions of the joint law record only how likely each attribute is on its own. The fixed marginals---$P(A{=}0)=\alpha$ and $P(B{=}0)=\beta$---then leave a whole family of joint laws
open---with its members differing precisely in how the attributes are believed to co-move. A 
\textbf{cross-attribute association} in the sense of this paper---for a joint law $D$, $\assoc(D):=D_{00}D_{11}-D_{01}D_{10}$---therefore exists only on the joint law, never on its two marginals separately; evaluated at the prior, $\assoc(P)=c$. Thus the prior $P$ is the evaluator's subjective credence, before any cue arrives, over the joint attributes of a randomly drawn candidate. We coordinatise $\Delta^{3}$ by the marginals
$P(A{=}0)=\alpha$, $P(B{=}0)=\beta$ and the prior covariance $c$ between the attributes---a change of coordinates on the simplex:
\[
  P=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\beta+c & \alpha(1-\beta)-c\\[2pt](1-\alpha)\beta-c & (1-\alpha)(1-\beta)+c\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(A,B)=c,
\]
so $c=0$ is independence and $c$ is feasibility-bounded by nonnegativity of the four
cells---the Fr\'echet--Hoeffding bounds for the $2\times2$ table \anchor{Setup}.

The evaluator receives two attribute-local \emph{soft cues} so that a cue on $A$
yields an impression (a target marginal) $q=(q_{0},q_{1})$ on $A$'s partition and a cue on $B$
yields an impression $r=(r_{0},r_{1})$ on $B$'s partition---with no cue bearing on a joint event. There is no cue that bears on both attributes jointly and the two cues in the model are never fused into one joint event (see Assumption~\ref{as:local}). Each impression then is a delivered credence, not a likelihood, and the product of the two impressions can be written $q\otimes r$, with $(q\otimes r)_{ij}=q_{i}r_{j}$ \anchor{Setup}.

The engagement decision for the evaluator is defined in terms of a desirability vector $\vv=(v_{ij})_{i,j\in\{0,1\}}$---one entry per state-space cell (the same shape as $P$). With the score $s(P)=\langle\vv,P\rangle=\sum_{ij}v_{ij}P(i,j)$, the evaluator engages if and only if
$s(P)\ge\tau$ \anchor{Setup}. This is expected-utility maximisation at the belief $P$---so that $s(P)$ is the expected desirability of engaging, and $\tau$ is the value of the outside option one forgoes by not engaging. Whether sequence-dependence changes what evaluators do or not is driven by the expected desirability an evaluator forgoes when their sequence-dependent belief leads them to a different action than a benchmark belief. 

As we further detail in Section \ref{sec:jeffrey}, the Bayes-factor benchmark $\PB$ is used as a sequence-free reference \citep{Wagner2002} to define a surplus. Writing $u:=s(\PB)-\tau$ for the net surplus of engaging at the benchmark belief, the cost of taking the action that differs from the benchmark action is $|u|$. More specifically, if $u>0$, the benchmark says engage but the evaluator doesn't and they forgo a gain of $u$---so that the cost is $|u|$. Likewise, when $u<0$, the benchmark says not to engage, but the evaluator does, with the cost again being $|u|$. Whether engagement deviates from the benchmark is what feeds the aggregation picture, with
$\tau$ entering the results only through $u=s(\PB)-\tau$ and never appearing on its own.

The surplus thus defined is what is of interest as the notion of loss in the population aggregate. With $\delta_\sigma$ as the first-order coefficient with which an evaluator's own score departs from the benchmark's under reading sequence $\sigma$ (see Section~\ref{sec:individual}), $g$ as the joint density of $(u,\delta_\sigma)$ across the population and the sequence distribution, the expected foregone surplus is defined as 
\[
  L(c)=\iint_{\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta)\}}|u|\,g(u,\delta)\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}\delta,
\]
the cost $|u|$ borne by exactly those evaluators whose decision the sequence changes, the integration variable $\delta$ ranging over the values taken by $\delta_\sigma$.


\subsection{The Bayes-factor benchmark}
\label{sec:jeffrey}

Jeffrey's rule updates on an impression by resetting the cue's marginal to the delivered
credence and holding the conditionals fixed---the invariance condition
\citep[ch.~11]{Jeffrey1983}. For an impression $q$ on $A$,
\[
  (\PJ_{A}P)(i,j)=q_{i}\,P(i,j)/P(A{=}i),
\]
and symmetrically for an impression $r$ on $B$ \anchor{Setup}. The axiomatic grounding is
twofold. The partition satisfying the invariance condition is the minimal sufficient statistic
for revising the prior to any candidate posterior on that partition
\citep{DiaconisZabell1982}, and the rule is the minimal revision of the prior
consistent with the delivered marginal, in the $I$-divergence sense
\citep{DiaconisZabell1982}. The
two-cue Jeffrey posteriors are the two sequences
\[
  \PJ_{AB}=\PJ_{B}\!\circ\PJ_{A}\,P,\qquad \PJ_{BA}=\PJ_{A}\!\circ\PJ_{B}\,P .
\]

\begin{definition}[sequence effect]\label{def:seqeffect}
The \emph{sequence effect} is the difference $\PJ_{AB}-\PJ_{BA}$ between the beliefs two
evaluators reach from the same prior and the same two impressions, differing only in which cue
they met first. It is the object of study at the individual level.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[individual gap]\label{def:indgap}
The \emph{individual gap} is the difference between either sequence-posterior and the benchmark,
$\PJ_\sigma-\PB$ for $\sigma\in\{AB,BA\}$ \anchor{Setup}.
\end{definition}

The benchmark $\PB$ is not a rival updating rule but the same two impressions
re-expressed---each read as a multiplicative factor relative to the prior marginal. Each impression's evidential content can be written as a Bayes factor---a likelihood
matched to the cue given the prior, $\ell^{A}_{i}\propto q_{i}/P(A{=}i)$ and
$\ell^{B}_{j}\propto r_{j}/P(B{=}j)$---and, unlike a delivered credence, a Bayes factor carries
the same content whatever prior it meets. Combining the two cues' Bayes-factor content is
sequence-invariant \citep{Wagner2002}, and $\PB$ is the single update on that combined content,
\[
  \PB(i,j)\propto P(i,j)\,\ell^{A}_{i}\,\ell^{B}_{j},
\]
well-defined regardless of which cue arrived first \anchor{Setup}. As we later show, it coincides
on any one cue with the matched Jeffrey step (Proposition~\ref{prop:A1}). We compare sequential updating on delivered credences (which does not commute) against the one-shot updating on Bayes-factor content (which does). 

\subsection{Assumptions}
\label{sec:assumptions}

With the above setup, we now state four key assumptions that limit the scope of our discussion---fixing which reading sequences, which cues, and which criterion the results apply to. The assumptions are revisited again in Section~\ref{sec:scope} to discuss how relaxing one or more of these assumptions would affect our key conclusions.

\begin{assumption}[Locality in $c$]\label{as:localc}
The prior covariance $c$ between the two correlated attributes is treated as small, and results
are derived as expansions near $c=0$ rather than as statements holding for arbitrary $c$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Soft cues]\label{as:soft}
The cues are bundled, correlated, and soft, each delivering a credence on an attribute's partition,
not a decisive cue.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Attribute-locality]\label{as:local}
Each impression is a credence on a single attribute's partition, never on a joint event.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Surplus criterion]\label{as:surplus}
Loss is measured by foregone surplus (cost $|u|$), with a threshold density that is at least
integrable and with the population's desirability vectors uniformly bounded.
\end{assumption}

Before we detail what the above assumptions imply for sequence-dependent decisions in a population aggregate, we discuss how the model relates with other approaches to non-commutativity of sequence in the literature.

\section{Related literature}
\label{sec:literature}

While sequence-dependence has been of interest to empirical psychology at least since
\citet{Asch1946}, the problem seems to have received interest in economics primarily through the
observational learning literature. \citet{BHW1992} and \citet{Banerjee1992}, for
example, consider herd-behaviour and informational cascades in which sequence matters. In
\citet{Banerjee1992}, the sequence persists because a coarse public action fails to be a
sufficient statistic for private information. In \citet{BHW1992}, on the other hand, a
cascade is an action that conveys no private signal. The context of the paper differs significantly
from that of existing Bayesian approaches in several ways. First, the object
of interest in the current paper is a literal permutation non-commutativity of a fixed
pair of inputs read by a single agent. Second, instead of the sequencing occurring across different agents (the sequence in
which predecessors act), the current paper considers two cues read by the same agent
(i.e.\ the sequence in which one evaluator meets two cues). Finally, the agents are assumed to be independent of each other in their actions. Instead of coupling being a feed-forward
informational externality therefore---with agent $n$'s action entering agent $n{+}1$'s information set---the agents in the current model do not have any feedback. One similarity with the literature, however,
is that the sequence effect in \citep{BHW1992, Banerjee1992} lives on the analogue of the
marginals---which remain so in the current paper as well.


The purview of the current paper is positive rather than normative. The paper merely takes the mean belief as the object an observer computes and characterises which of its
statistics conceal the members' sequence-dependence. A related normative literature evaluates group belief formation by whether aggregation
commutes with updating---a design criterion for the pooling rule \citep{Dietrich2021}.
While geometric pooling \citep[Def.~1]{Dietrich2021} satisfies the criterion, the population mean studied in the
current paper (i.e.\ the average posterior across evaluators who differ only in the sequence they meet the two cues) is the outcome of linear pooling and generically fails the criterion. As further sections elaborate, this is consistent with the first-order divergence of every statistic outside the protected class of Proposition~\ref{prop:C3}.

Several alternatives to Bayesian updating have been used in the decision theory literature to explain whether sequential conditioning is sequence-independent or not. For example, \citet{Epstein2006} makes the updating rule subjective, and \citet{Ortoleva2012} axiomatises departures triggered by unexpected news. \citet{Cripps2021} shows that symmetry and divisibility jointly force sequence-independence,
so the correlated two-cue composite of Proposition~\ref{prop:A2} fails divisibility---and, of the four axioms, only divisibility.\footnote{Cripps's axioms govern rules on fixed-likelihood experiments; the statement here is behavioural---the map from prior and delivered credences to final belief, held against the sequence-invariance that his Symmetry and Divisibility axioms jointly force.} While the literature restores sequence-invariance for sequential Jeffrey updating by changing how successive inputs are pooled \citep{PettigrewWeisberg2025}, the current paper instead accepts the non-commutativity and characterises what remains of it in aggregate.


The paper borrows the evaluator-with-binary-attributes frame from the statistical-discrimination lineage \citep{Phelps1972, Arrow1973}. However, the object of interest for the current paper remains
kinematic rather than an equilibrium fixed point. Unlike \citet{CoateLoury1993}, for example, in which the stereotype is a self-confirming
steady state of best responses, the current paper merely characterises the channel-free baseline. In this baseline, the differences are keyed to encounter sequence rather than to
any group-marker, are individually first order, and yet remain invisible in the protected dependence statistics. It is worth highlighting that, instead of stereotypes as representativeness-distortion or selective recall \citep{BCGS2016}, the sense of stereotype in the current paper is that of a believed cross-attribute association produced by coherent updating on impoverished input, not by a distortion of memory or sampling. The lineage distinction the
concluding section trades on---protection intrinsic to the arithmetic of aggregation, rather than imposed by an enforced constraint on behaviour---goes back at least as far as \citet{Becker1962}.

It is worth contrasting the phenomenon explored in the current paper against the amalgamation paradox \citep{GoodMittal1987}, where a real effect present in every subpopulation is erased or
reversed by a confound in how the subpopulations are weighted together. Whereas the amalgamation paradox erases a real effect by combining subpopulations under confounded weights,
our decision-space result shows the same kind of erasure can occur within a single population -- where a first-order share of individuals who are individually affected contributes only a second-order loss in aggregate
(Theorem~\ref{thm:B2}).

\section{Sequence effects at the individual level}
\label{sec:individual}

Since Bayesian conditioning is sequence-independent by definition, we measure sequence effects primarily in terms of two \textbf{primitive} statistics -- that we refer to as gap and score. The \emph{individual gap} is a distance from a Bayes-factor benchmark $\PB$ for the evaluator (see Definition \ref{def:indgap}). The second primitive is a \emph{score-gap} $s(P)=\langle\vv,P\rangle$ defined against the sequence-free reference $\PB$ alongside with a threshold $\tau$. The rest of the belief-statistics and decision-statistics rely on these two primitives. Recall Section~\ref{sec:setup} that $\Pbar$ is the mean posterior, $u:=s(\PB)-\tau$ the net surplus, and $|u|$ the cost of taking the action that differs from the benchmark action. 

With these, the two \textbf{belief-statistics} that we are interested in are the \emph{marginal probability} and \emph{believed cross-attribute association}. The \textbf{decision-statistics} that we discuss for the individual evaluator and the population aggregate are \emph{surplus-weighted loss} $L(c)$ and a decision-\emph{flip}. The decision-flip is counted in the population to determine the \emph{share} of those evaluators whose decision is changed by the sequence. The loss is then simply an aggregation of $|u|$ over this share. How all these quantities are carried over in aggregate are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:aggregation}. 

\subsection{Primitives for the evaluator}
Starting with the \emph{individual gap}, we first state the rather trivial assertion that the difference between Jeffrey and Bayes is absent on a single cue or on two cues on independent attributes. It is worth highlighting here that since we focus on soft, credence-delivering evidence in the current paper, the fact that two hard cues---delivered as a likelihood--- already commute, remains out of scope (Assumption~\ref{as:soft}).

\renewcommand{\theproposition}{A1}%
\begin{proposition}[exact immunity]\label{prop:A1}
On a single cue, the Jeffrey posterior equals the matched Bayesian posterior identically, for
every prior and every impression. With two cues but $c=0$ (independent attributes),
$\PJ_{AB}=\PJ_{BA}=\PB$ exactly. \anchor{Prop.~A1}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The single cue on $A$ means the matched likelihood $\ell^A_i=q_i Z/P(A{=}i)$, with $Z$ the normaliser
fixing the posterior $A$-marginal to $q$---which gives
$\PB(i,j)=P(i,j)\ell^A_i/Z=q_i\,P(i,j)/P(A{=}i)=(\PJ_A P)(i,j)$. For $c=0$ the prior factorises,
$P(i,j)=P(A{=}i)P(B{=}j)$. Each Jeffrey step and the Bayesian update then act on independent
coordinates, so both sequences and the Bayesian posterior equal the product $q\otimes r$.
\end{proof}

In other words, a decision turning on \emph{one} cue is immune. Next we specify the sequence effect at the individual level---which is first order in our setup and serves as a building block for the rest of the propositions. 

\renewcommand{\theproposition}{A2}%
\begin{proposition}[micro divergence]\label{prop:A2}
Let $Z:=\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)>0$. At $c=0$ both reading sequences coincide with the
benchmark, $\PJ_{AB}=\PJ_{BA}=\PB=q\otimes r$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] \textup{(Individual gap.)} For $c\neq0$,
\[
  \PJ_{AB}-\PB=c\,\kappa R_1+\bigO(c^{2}),
  \qquad
  R_1=q\otimes(1,-1),
  \qquad
  \kappa=\frac{(\alpha-q_0)\,r_0(1-r_0)}{Z},
\]
\[
  \PJ_{BA}-\PB=c\,\kappa' R_2+\bigO(c^{2}),
  \qquad
  R_2=(1,-1)\otimes r,
  \qquad
  \kappa'=\frac{(\beta-r_0)\,q_0(1-q_0)}{Z},
\]
with $\kappa=0\iff q_0=\alpha$ or $r_0\in\{0,1\}$, and symmetrically for $\kappa'$; hence
$D\not\equiv0$.
\item[(ii)] \textup{(Sequence effect.)} $\PJ_{AB}-\PJ_{BA}=c\,\Delta_{\mathrm{seq}}+\bigO(c^{2})$
with $\Delta_{\mathrm{seq}}=\kappa R_1-\kappa' R_2$. Since $R_1,R_2$ are linearly independent,
$\Delta_{\mathrm{seq}}\equiv0$ if and only if $\kappa=\kappa'=0$; hence
$\Delta_{\mathrm{seq}}\not\equiv0$.
\end{enumerate}
\anchor{Prop.~A2}
\end{proposition}

The proof is provided in Appendix \ref{app:proofs}. The mechanism behind it is rather straightforward. Since prior covariance $c$ is the panel's belief about how strongly competence and trustworthiness go together, learning about one attribute is informative about the other (in the panel's own prior). A Jeffrey update on the $A$-cue rescales the joint distribution row by row, preserving the conditional distribution of $B$ given $A$. Thus when the attributes are independent, preserving that conditional leaves $B$'s marginal untouched, so the two sequences trivially commute and coincide with the benchmark exactly (Proposition~\ref{prop:A1}). On the other hand, when $c\neq0$, rescaling rows changes $B$'s marginal and the update reaches the second attribute through the covariance, so the two sequences no longer agree at first order in $c$. 

The scalar $\kappa$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:A2} shows the two ways in which the \emph{individual gap} $P^J_{AB}-P^B$ can vanish. Since $\kappa\propto(\alpha-q_0)$, the gap can vanish when the $A$-impression coincides with the prior $A$-marginal. Similarly, since $\kappa\propto r_0(1-r_0)$, the gap can also vanish when the $B$-impression is degenerate. In other words, the individual gap has a nonzero leading coefficient only when the credential's impression genuinely surprises ($q_{0}\neq\alpha$, the impression departing from the prior marginal probability) and the letter leaves a genuinely soft impression ($r_{0}(1-r_{0})>0$, neither glowing nor damning enough to be decisive).


The sequence effect (Definition~\ref{def:seqeffect})---the difference $\PJ_{AB}-\PJ_{BA}$ between the two reading sequences---is also first order for the individual, and it is worth pausing on why before turning to the next quantity. The sequence effect is irreducible to either single-route gap. The $A$-first gap $\PJ_{AB}-\PB$ has leading coefficient $\kappa\,R_1$ with $R_1=q\otimes(1,-1)$ (
which holds the $A$-marginal fixed and moves only the $B$-conditional) and the $B$-first gap $\PJ_{BA}-\PB$ has $\kappa'\,R_2$ with $R_2=(1,-1)\otimes r$ (holding the $B$-marginal
fixed). The sequence effect, being their difference, has the form $\PJ_{AB}-\PJ_{BA}=c\,(\kappa R_1-\kappa' R_2)+\bigO(c^2)$. Because $R_1$ and $R_2$ preserve different marginals, the sequence effect is two-dimensional and does not collapse to either gap or reduce to a rescaling of one of them. This demonstrates how ``first impressions stick''---once the attributes covary, the earlier cue leaves a residue in a direction the later cue does not reach. 

The second primitive, \textbf{score gap}  of a panel (given a fixed weight $\vv$) is the first-order coefficient with which its own score $s(P)=\langle\vv,P\rangle$ departs from the benchmark's. Given a particular route $\sigma$, we have
\[
  s(\PJ_\sigma)-s(\PB)=c\,\delta_\sigma+\bigO(c^{2}), \qquad \delta_\sigma:=\big\langle\vv,\ \partial_c(\PJ_\sigma-\PB)|_{c=0}\big\rangle.
\]
Given that $\delta_{AB}=\langle\vv,\kappa R_1\rangle$ and $\delta_{BA}=\langle\vv,\kappa'R_2\rangle$, the score gap is essentially the sequence effect seen through the lens of $\vv$.

\subsection{Belief Statistics}

The two belief-statistics we're concerned with are the \emph{believed association} between the two attributes and the believed \emph{marginal probability} of each attribute. The evaluator panel's \emph{believed cross-attribute association} is the association carried by its the evaluator's own posterior, $\assoc(\PJ_\sigma)$, set against the benchmark's $\assoc(\PB)$. In the example, it measures only how strongly the panel takes competence and trustworthiness to go together once the file is read (not how attributes are observed in the candidate pool). Unlike the gap and the score gap, it does not inherit the first order of Proposition~\ref{prop:A2}.

\renewcommand{\thelemma}{A3}%
\begin{lemma}[individual-level association immunity]\label{lem:A3}
For each reading sequence $\sigma\in\{AB,BA\}$ the believed association departs from the
benchmark's only at second order,
\[
  \assoc(\PJ_\sigma)-\assoc(\PB)=\bigO(c^{2}),
\]
identically in $(\alpha,\beta,q_{0},r_{0})$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $\assoc$ is a polynomial in the cells and hence smooth, and
$\PJ_\sigma-\PB=c\,D_\sigma+\bigO(c^{2})$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:A2}), the chain rule gives
\[
  \assoc(\PJ_\sigma)-\assoc(\PB)
   =c\,\big\langle\nabla\assoc(q\otimes r),\,D_\sigma\big\rangle+\bigO(c^{2}),
\]
\[
  \nabla\assoc\big|_{q\otimes r}
   =\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}r_{1} & -q_{1}r_{0}\\ -q_{0}r_{1} & q_{0}r_{0}\end{pmatrix}.
\]
With $D_{AB}=\kappa R_1$ and $D_{BA}=\kappa'R_2$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:A2}), both inner
products vanish termwise:
\[
  \big\langle\nabla\assoc(q\otimes r),\,R_1\big\rangle
   = q_0q_1r_1 + q_0q_1r_0 - q_0q_1r_1 - q_0q_1r_0
   = 0,
\]
\[
  \big\langle\nabla\assoc(q\otimes r),\,R_2\big\rangle
   = q_1r_0r_1 - q_1r_0r_1 + q_0r_0r_1 - q_0r_0r_1
   = 0,
\]
so the first-order term is zero on each route.
\end{proof}

It worth highlighting that the immunity is exact rather than generic and it is complete for a single evaluator (before any
averaging takes place). \anchor{Lem.~A3}

The second belief-statistic of interest is the panel's \emph{marginal probability} for an attribute i.e. the belief about that attribute alone, $\PJ_\sigma(A{=}1)$. This
is a linear read-out of the individual gap, the relevant row or column sum of $\PJ_\sigma-\PB$. Since $D_{AB}=\kappa R_1$ with $R_1=q\otimes(1,-1)$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:A2}), its rows sum to zero and its columns to $\pm\kappa$:
\[
  \textstyle\sum_j (R_1)_{ij}=0 \ \text{for each } i,
  \qquad
  \sum_i (R_1)_{i0}=1,
  \quad
  \sum_i (R_1)_{i1}=-1.
\]
On route $AB$ the competence marginal---the attribute read \emph{first}---therefore carries no
first-order term at all, while the trustworthiness marginal---read \emph{last}---moves at
$c\kappa$. Similarly, $D_{BA}=\kappa'R_2$ with $R_2=(1,-1)\otimes r$ gives the
opposite split, rows summing to $\pm\kappa'$ and columns to zero:
\[
  \textstyle\sum_i (R_2)_{ij}=0 \ \text{for each } j,
  \qquad
  \sum_j (R_2)_{0j}=1,
  \quad
  \sum_j (R_2)_{1j}=-1.
\]
The marginal probability of whichever attribute is read first is
$\bigO(c^{2})$ exactly, and of whichever is read last is $\Theta(c)$ generically.

\subsection{Decision Statistics}

Panels differ not only in what they believe but also in the effects of how they act on their beliefs. The two decision statistics we're concerned with in this regard are the decision-\emph{flip} (which becomes sequence-affected-share in the aggregate) and sequence-affected \emph{loss}.

The panel's \emph{flip} indicator, $\mathds{1}\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}$, records whether the first-order
perturbation $c\delta_\sigma$ of the score carries the net surplus across the threshold. At the individual level, a flip requires two things together---the perturbation
$c\delta_\sigma$ must point toward the threshold, in the opposite direction from $u$ itself,
and it must be large enough to actually reach it. Formally, $\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)$
holds exactly when $\sgn(c\delta_\sigma)=-\sgn(u)$ and $|c\delta_\sigma|>|u|$---equivalently,
when $u$ lies strictly between $0$ and $-c\delta_\sigma$. A perturbation $c\delta_\sigma$ pushing $u$ further from the threshold never flips it, however large $c$ grows.

Writing $c^{*}:=|u/\delta_\sigma|$ for the crossing point, a panel with $u\neq0$ and
$\delta_\sigma\neq0$ satisfies
\begin{align*}
  \mathds{1}\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}= 
  \begin{cases}
    1, & |c|>c^{*} \ \wedge\ \sgn(c\delta_\sigma)=-\sgn(u),\\[2pt]
    0, & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  \equiv 0 \ \text{ on } (-c^{*},c^{*}),
\end{align*}

and therefore
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\mathrm{d}c^{k}}
  \mathds{1}\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}\bigg|_{c=0}=0
  \quad \forall\, k\ge 0,
  \qquad\text{while}\qquad
  \mathds{1}\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}\not\equiv 0.
\end{align*}

Every Taylor coefficient at the origin vanishes even though the quantity itself does not, so it is a step function of $c$, not a power of $c$, and it leaves no leading coefficient for
aggregation to preserve or cancel. As we describe in the next subsection, what the
population acquires comes instead from the measure of the flip set,
\[
  \Pr\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}
   =\int_{\{|u|<|c\delta_\sigma|,\ \sgn(u)=-\sgn(c\delta_\sigma)\}} f(u)\,\mathrm{d}u
   =f(0)\cdot\bigO(c),
\]
a band of width $\bigO(c)$ at the threshold rather than an average of individual coefficients,
which is why the sequence-affected share is $\Theta(c)$ under a density hypothesis rather than
exactly or generically so (Proposition~\ref{prop:C2}).


The second decision statistic is the panel's \textbf{foregone surplus} which measures costs of a flip $|u|$ i.e. in the event that the decision flips. It weights the flip indicator by the stake at issue, so that panels barely past the threshold contribute little and panels far from it contribute much. Writing
$\ell_\sigma(c):=|u|\cdot\mathds{1}\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}$ for the foregone
surplus, both decision quantities inherit the same threshold structure established above for
$u\neq0,\ \delta_\sigma\neq0$:
\[
  \mathds{1}\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}\equiv 0,
  \qquad
  \ell_\sigma(c)\equiv 0,
  \qquad\text{on } (-c^{*},c^{*}),
\]
\[
  \frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\mathrm{d}c^{k}}\,\mathds{1}\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}
  \bigg|_{c=0}
  =
  \frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\mathrm{d}c^{k}}\,\ell_\sigma(c)\bigg|_{c=0}
  =0
  \quad \forall\, k\ge 0,
\]
so neither is smooth in $c$ at the individual level, and neither carries a first-order
coefficient of its own into the aggregate \anchor{Thm.~B2; Prop.~C2}.

\section{Aggregated Effects of Cue-Sequence}
\label{sec:aggregation}

We now describe how the sequence-effect on the statistics discussed in the previous section fares in the population aggregate. Throughout this section we consider a population mixing the two encounter sequences: a fraction $\lambda\in[0,1]$ of evaluators meet the $A$-first (the credential cue in the example) and the
rest $B$-first (the letter), so the mean belief under Jeffrey conditioning is
$\Pbar_\lambda=\lambda\,\PJ_{AB}+(1-\lambda)\,\PJ_{BA}$. As it turns out, what makes a statistic second order or first order is largely the nature of the statistic itself rather than the mechanism of aggregation.

Two definitions are worth pinning down before we move further. First, we define a \emph{distortion} of an arbitrary smooth scalar statistic $F$ as the aggregate departure from the benchmark, $F(\Pbar)-F(\PB)$. This is the aggregate counterpart of the individual \emph{gap} and is always a population-average object by definition. Second, we define the notion of a \emph{protection}, which classifies that distortion by its order in $c$.

\begin{definition}[protection]\label{def:protection}
$F$ is \emph{protected} if its distortion is second order in the prior covariance,
$F(\Pbar)-F(\PB)=\bigO(c^{2})$, and \emph{unprotected} if that distortion is first order, $\bigO(c)$.
\end{definition}

A particularly surprising finding is that for a smooth statistic of the belief, the answer is settled before any averaging takes place. The aggregation confines the average posterior to a two-dimensional plane, spanned by the leading directions $R_1$ and $R_2$ of the two reading sequences, so whether a statistic is protected turns only on whether its differential at independence annihilates that plane. The attribute-locality assumption and Lemma~\ref{lem:A3} ensure that the annihilator is exactly $\mathrm{span}\{d\,\assoc\}$. As we summarise in Table~\ref{tab:order-summary}, the average believed cross-attribute association
(Proposition~\ref{prop:C1}) and the surplus-weighted loss $L(c)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:B2}) are
second order, whereas the aggregate gap, score gaps on unprotected weights, the marginal
probabilities, and the sequence-affected share remain first order.

The obvious consequence of this property is that detectability is a property of the instrument, not of the phenomenon. In the panel example, two panels reading the same file in opposite sequences differ in what they believe about the candidate's trustworthiness, and near the threshold in whom they
hire. A reviewer who computes the panels' average believed association between competence and trustworthiness finds it within $\bigO(c^{2})$ of the benchmark and sees nothing (Lemma~\ref{lem:A3}, Proposition~\ref{prop:C1}) -- whereas a reviewer computing the share of files whose decision the reading sequence changes finds a departure at
$\bigO(c)$ instead. The rest of the section discusses these  findings that are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:order-summary}.

\subsection{Aggregate Primitive Statistics}
That both individual gap and score-gap remain $\Theta(c)$ in the population aggregate is somewhat trivial. This is because the aggregate simply mixes the two routes' own shifts together in proportion to 
$\lambda$ and generically retains the sequence effects that were applicable at the individual level.

\subsubsection{Gap distortion}
\label{sec:distortion}

The proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:C3} gives $\Pbar_\lambda-\PB=c\,M_\lambda+\bigO(c^{2})$ directly, and because $R_1$ and $R_2$ are linearly independent, $M_\lambda$ vanishes at interior $\lambda$ only where the coefficients of both reading sequences vanish. The aggregate gap is therefore first order on the same generic set as the individual gap of Proposition~\ref{prop:A2}. In other words, averaging over reading sequences reweights the leading coefficient without removing it. \anchor{Prop.~C3}

\subsubsection{Score gap}
\label{sec:scoregap}

The average \emph{score gap} is a straightforward measure of the weighted score of the belief and its first-order in aggregate follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:A2}.

\renewcommand{\thelemma}{B1}%
\begin{lemma}[first-order score gap]\label{lem:B1}
$s(\PJ_\sigma)-s(\PB)=c\,\delta_\sigma+\bigO(c^{2})$, with $\delta_\sigma$ as defined in
Section~\ref{sec:individual}; generically
$\delta_{AB}\neq\delta_{BA}$, and $\delta_\sigma=0$ if $\vv$ is constant. \anchor{Lem.~B1}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Apply the linear functional $s=\langle\vv,\cdot\rangle$ to
$\PJ_\sigma-\PB=cD_\sigma+\bigO(c^2)$ from Proposition~\ref{prop:A2}. Constancy of $\vv$ makes
$\delta_\sigma$ telescope to $0$ since both posteriors are normalised.
\end{proof}


\subsection{Aggregate Belief Statistics}
The aggregation of belief statistics also shows that aggregation doesn't really change the sequence-effects from the individual case. As Lemma~\ref{lem:A3} shows, a particular route doesn't shift association in first-order. Mixing them does not change that. On the other hand, the case for marginal probability is not symmetrical by definition. Whichever attribute a route reads first has its marginal pinned exactly to the impression, so that route's shift in that marginal is zero whereas the shift due the other route reading the attribute last is generically nonzero. Mixing the two does simply zero out the first-order effect. Once again, it's not the averaging that changes the sequence, but the nature of statistic itself determines the order.

\subsubsection{Aggregate cross-attribute association}
\label{sec:association}

Recall that the sense of stereotype in the paper depends on the cross-attribute association. With $\assoc(D)=D_{00}D_{11}-D_{01}D_{10}$ as the association of a joint law $D$, the \emph{stereotype} is the believed association absent from---or in excess of---the benchmark, a
nonzero $\assoc(\Pbar)-\assoc(\PB)$. By definition, stereotype is absent when the belief matches the Bayesian benchmark.

We now explain how the average believed cross-attribute association is second order because of a separable reweighting
(Lemma~\ref{lem:sep})---rather than the Jeffrey conditioning or the presence of two cues. More particularly, every updating route in the model composes to a separable
reweighting---which can only rescale an association, never shift it---so the average posterior
moves along a single fixed direction. Since separability holds at every $N$ and for any rule whose steps read one attribute at a time, the protection is structural rather than a cancellation of terms and shows up as a property of attribute-locality (holding for any such rule, not Jeffrey
conditioning in particular). The lemma is stated and proved for arbitrary $N$.

\renewcommand{\thelemma}{SEP}%
\begin{lemma}[separability of attribute-local composites]\label{lem:sep}
For any number $N$ of binary attributes and a joint prior $P$ over $\{0,1\}^{N}$, any finite
sequence of attribute-local updates produces a \emph{separable} reweighting
\[
  P'(x)=P(x)\prod_{a=1}^{N}g_{a}(x_{a})
\]
for some single-attribute functions $g_{a}$. Equivalently, $\log(P'/P)$ contains no interaction
term of any order. \anchor{Lem.~SEP}
\end{lemma}

The proof is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. It proceeds by induction on the update
sequence: each attribute-local step folds into that attribute's own multiplicative factor
without ever coupling two coordinates, so the product form survives any finite sequence of such
steps; an equivalent log-linear characterisation---no interaction term of any order---follows
from the same argument.

The Jeffrey-specific content is therefore confined to the \emph{sequence effect}
itself (Proposition~\ref{prop:A2})---as a property of probability kinematics. The first-order
protection established below thus rests only on separability---possessed by any attribute-local
multiplicative update.


Proposition~\ref{prop:C1} now shows how the second-order behaviour of the distortion of the average believed association depends on the separability lemma. More specifically, Lemma~\ref{lem:sep} forces every updating route to act
on the association by the pure multiplier $\assoc(D')=(g_0g_1h_0h_1/Z^{2})\,\assoc(D)$, so all of them share the same leading factor at independence and the $\bigO(c)$ terms in $\assoc(\Pbar)-\assoc(\PB)$ cancel identically. On the other hand, a non-separable updating route obeys no such
identity and can shift $\assoc$ additively rather than merely rescale it, leaving a first-order distortion.

\renewcommand{\theproposition}{C1}%
\begin{proposition}[belief-level decoupling]\label{prop:C1}
The distortion of the average believed association is second order---its first-order coefficient is
identically zero:
\[
  \assoc(\Pbar)-\assoc(\PB)=\bigO(c^{2}).
\]
\anchor{Prop.~C1}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Association---the $\bigO(c)$ terms coincide by separable covariance.} For any rank-one
(separable) reweighting $D'_{ij}=g_i h_j D_{ij}/Z$ with $Z=\sum_{ij}g_i h_j D_{ij}$,
\[
  \assoc(D')=\frac{g_0 g_1 h_0 h_1}{Z^{2}}\,\assoc(D),
\]
since the cross terms in $D'_{00}D'_{11}-D'_{01}D'_{10}$ carry the identical factor
$g_0g_1h_0h_1$. Explicitly,
\begin{align*}
  D'_{00}D'_{11}-D'_{01}D'_{10}
  &=\frac{(g_0h_0D_{00})(g_1h_1D_{11})-(g_0h_1D_{01})(g_1h_0D_{10})}{Z^{2}}\\
  &=\frac{g_0g_1h_0h_1}{Z^{2}}\bigl(D_{00}D_{11}-D_{01}D_{10}\bigr).
\end{align*}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:sep}, every Jeffrey route composes to exactly such a
separable reweighting (at $N=2$, a rank-one form $g_ih_j$), and the Bayes update is one
directly ($g_i=q_i/\alpha_i,\ h_j=r_j/\beta_j$, writing $\alpha_0=\alpha,\ \alpha_1=1-\alpha$ and
similarly for $\beta$). Every route therefore
maps the prior association $\assoc(P)=c$ to a \emph{positive multiple} of $c$; none can shift it
additively. Evaluating the multiplier at $c=0$---where all intermediate marginals are
$(\alpha,1-\alpha)$ and $(\beta,1-\beta)$ regardless of route---gives the same leading factor
\[
  \assoc(\PJ_{AB})=\assoc(\PJ_{BA})=\assoc(\PB)=K\,c+\bigO(c^2),
  \qquad K=\frac{q_0 q_1\, r_0 r_1}{\alpha(1-\alpha)\beta(1-\beta)} .
\]
Explicitly, at $c=0$ one has $g_i=q_i/\alpha_i$, $h_j=r_j/\beta_j$ and $Z=1$, so the
multiplier equals $\bigl(q_0q_1/\alpha(1-\alpha)\bigr)\bigl(r_0r_1/\beta(1-\beta)\bigr)=K$.
Hence $\assoc(\Pbar)=\lambda\,\assoc(\PJ_{AB})+(1-\lambda)\,\assoc(\PJ_{BA})+\bigO(c^2)
=Kc+\bigO(c^2)$ (the mixture identity holds to leading order; $\assoc$ is quadratic, and the
cross terms are $\bigO(c^2)$) and
$\assoc(\Pbar)-\assoc(\PB)=\bigO(c^2)$, the $\bigO(c)$ coefficients cancelling since Jeffrey and Bayes are both separable, so both can only rescale the prior
association by the same leading factor.

\end{proof}

The proposition's implications for the example vignette are quite revealing. In a population of panels evaluating a common pool---where each receives the same two impressions, one bearing on competence and one on trustworthiness---each panel's posterior differs from the benchmark at $\bigO(c)$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:A2}). Yet the average believed association---whether the evaluators
collectively take the two attributes to go together more or less than the benchmark
warrants---carries no first-order trace of the sequence (Proposition~\ref{prop:C1}). In other words, reading sequence cannot, to first order, build or erode the perceived relationship between the attributes,
because both updates reweight separably, and a separable reweighting only rescales the
association already present. Thus, sequence effects, in this model, do not create stereotypes to first
order. In fact, no route and no mixture of routes moves the believed association. What the
reading sequence does move is the subject of the next subsection.

\subsubsection{Aggregate Marginal-probability}
\label{sec:marginal}

The current section shows that the reading sequence does move the marginal probability in the aggregate. In other words, marginal probability does not behave like a protected statistic. Proposition \ref{prop:C1m} formalises this.

\renewcommand{\theproposition}{C1$'$}%
\begin{proposition}[marginal-probability drift]\label{prop:C1m}
The aggregate \emph{marginal-probability} distortion is first order, with
\[
  \big[\Pbar(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)\big]
   = c\cdot(1-\lambda)\,\frac{q_0(1-q_0)\,(r_0-\beta)}{\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)}+\bigO(c^2),
\]
so for $\lambda<1$ its sign equals $\sgn(r_0-\beta)$, sign-indefinite across
cue--marginal-probability configurations. At $\lambda=1$ this drift vanishes at first order and
the $B$-marginal drifts with coefficient
$\lambda\,r_0(1-r_0)(q_0-\alpha)/\big(\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)\big)$ with sign
$\sgn(q_0-\alpha)$. For every $\lambda\in[0,1]$, therefore, at least one marginal drifts at first order.
\anchor{Prop.~C1$'$}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In the $BA$ sequence, the $A$-cue is processed last, and a Jeffrey step pins its own marginal ($\PJ_{BA}(A{=}1)=q_{1}$ identically
in $c$). Similarly, in the $AB$ sequence the later $B$-step pulls the $A$-marginal off the delivered value
through the covariance so that we have
\[
  \PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)=q_{1}-c\,\frac{q_{0}(1-q_{0})(r_{0}-\beta)}{\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)}
  +\bigO(c^{2}),
\]

Writing $K:=\dfrac{q_0(1-q_0)(r_0-\beta)}{\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)}$, the two routes give
\[
  \PJ_{BA}(A{=}1)=q_1,
  \qquad
  \PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)=q_1-cK+\bigO(c^2),
  \qquad
  \PB(A{=}1)=q_1-cK+\bigO(c^2),
\]
Hence,
\[
  \PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)=\bigO(c^2),
  \qquad
  \PJ_{BA}(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)=cK+\bigO(c^2).
\]
Averaging over routes (Figure~\ref{fig:routes}),
\begin{align*}
  \Pbar(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)
   &=\lambda\bigl[\PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)\bigr]+(1-\lambda)\bigl[\PJ_{BA}(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)\bigr]\\
   &=\lambda\cdot\bigO(c^2)+(1-\lambda)\bigl(cK+\bigO(c^2)\bigr)
    =c\,(1-\lambda)K+\bigO(c^2).
\end{align*}
Since $q_0(1-q_0)>0$ and $\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)>0$ on the open cube, $\sgn K=\sgn(r_0-\beta)$, so
\[
  \lambda<1
  \ \Longrightarrow\
  \sgn\bigl[\Pbar(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)\bigr]=\sgn(r_0-\beta),
  \]
  When $\lambda = 1$, we have
 \[
  \lambda=1
  \ \Longrightarrow\
  \Pbar(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)=\bigO(c^2).
\]

It's worth highlighting a key result that follows from the sequence affecting marginal probability at first order. The pool's average perceived competence drifts at $\bigO(c)$. In terms of the terminology we've used, the marginal probability lies outside the protected class. As a result, what an outside observer surveying the panels' joint beliefs and comparing them against the benchmark would find is that along the association axis, the average believed relationship between competence and trustworthiness would sit at essentially its benchmark value (order $c^{2}$). On the other hand, along the marginal-probability axis the average perceived marginal probability of either attribute would depart from the benchmark already at order $c$. It is only in the marginal probabilities that the observer could read off any trace of the sequence at all. The \emph{footprint of sequencing thus resides in marginal probabilities, not believed association}.

\subsection{Aggregate Decision Statistics}

Both decision-statistics look at evaluators inside the threshold band who may flip their decision when the sequence changes their score. However, \textbf{Share} depends on only on the band's mass, $f(0)\cdot\bigO(c)$ -- thus leaving it first order. On the other hand , \textbf{Loss} weights each one by their stake $|u|$, and is itself bounded by $\bigO(c)$ from the threshold inside the
band $|u|$ -- leaving it second-order.

\subsubsection{Surplus-weighted loss}
\label{sec:welfare}

We now show that a fixed evaluator with net surplus $u\neq0$ is  unaffected by the reading sequence until $|c\delta_\sigma|$ reaches $|u|$, so their foregone surplus
is identically zero for small $c$ rather than second order in it. Whatever sequence the population
aggregate has is therefore not inherited from any member. Taking the score gap of
Lemma~\ref{lem:B1} as given, the higher-order behaviour of the expected surplus loss $L(c)$ is
now stated as a theorem.

\renewcommand{\thetheorem}{B2}%
\begin{theorem}[surplus-weighted loss]\label{thm:B2}
Let $u=s(\PB)-\tau$ have, across the population and the sequence distribution, a joint density
whose $u$-marginal is integrable (Assumption~\ref{as:surplus}). Then the expected surplus loss $L(c)$ of the Jeffrey
population relative to the benchmark is $o(c)$---strictly higher order than the individual
divergence. If the $u$-marginal is bounded and continuous at $u=0$ with value
$f(0)>0$, the loss is exactly second order, with constant named:
\[
  L(c)=\frac{f(0)}{2}\,\E\!\big[\delta_\sigma^{2}\mid u=0\big]\,c^{2}+\bigO(c^{3}).
\]
\anchor{Thm.~B2}
\end{theorem}

The proof, given in full in \ref{app:proofs}, can be viewed as a band picture shown in Figure~\ref{fig:band}. The Jeffrey and benchmark
actions differ only when the perturbation $c\delta_\sigma$ flips the sign of the surplus $u$, so
the flip set is confined to a band of width $\bigO(c)$ at the threshold. On that band the cost
$|u|$ is itself $\bigO(c)$. An $\bigO(c)$ mass of individuals, each costing $\bigO(c)$, yields an
$\bigO(c^{2})$ loss. More particularly, a candidate is mis-decided only when the first-order perturbation flips the sign of the surplus, $\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)$---which in turn requires
$|u|\le|c\delta_\sigma|=\bigO(c)$. The affected evaluators are exactly those lying within $\bigO(c)$ of the threshold---the thin strip around
$u=0$ that we call the \emph{band}. Its mass, the fraction of the population sitting in it, is
$\Pr\{|u|\le|c\delta_\sigma|\}=f(0)\cdot\bigO(c)$, since a strip of width $\bigO(c)$ carries that
much probability when $f$ is continuous at $u=0$. The foregone surplus $|u|$ on the band is itself $\bigO(c)$ and the loss must obey
\[
  L(c)\;\le\;\underbrace{\bigO(c)}_{\text{cost }|u|}\cdot\underbrace{\bigO(c)}_{\text{band mass}}
       \;=\;\bigO(c^{2}).
\]

While the mechanism through which the surplus-weighted loss becomes second order may seem unremarkable \footnote{A few other candidate explanations for why the surplus-weighted loss may become second order are worth ruling out. First, the rate is not an
artefact of the \emph{threshold density} itself. Consider three instances of when the population's threshold density is not well-behaved at the margin: i) an \emph{atom at threshold} situation, where a spike of ``mass'' sits exactly at the threshold; ii) an \emph{integrable singularity} at the threshold;
or iii) a \emph{vanishing} density, with no mass at the threshold. None of the three destroys the separation of scales, though two of them shift the exponent away from exactly $2$. The \emph{atom at the threshold} results in $|u|=0$, so the integrand vanishes. The \emph{integrable singularity}
$f(u)\sim|u|^{-\gamma}$ with $0<\gamma<1$ degrades the rate but not the conclusion---the density
is $\bigO(c^{-\gamma})$ and the cost $|u|$ is $\bigO(c)$, so the loss is
$\bigO(c)\cdot\bigO(c)\cdot\bigO(c^{-\gamma})=\bigO(c^{2-\gamma})$, which is still $o(c)$ since
$\gamma<1$. A \emph{vanishing} density $f(0)=0$ takes the loss below $\bigO(c^{2})$---continuity carries $f(0)=0$ onto the shrinking band, so $f=o(1)$ there while the band still contributes $\bigO(c^{2})$, giving $L(c)=o(c^{2})$. Second, within that same regime, the exponent is not affected by the \emph{sequence distribution} either. This is because $\delta_\sigma$ enters \emph{squared} and so cannot average away even when sequencing is one-directional. Third, the \emph{smoothness of the density} $f$ of $u=s(\PB)-\tau$ is not a requirement for the separation of scales. Smoothness is used above only to name the constant in the exact $c^{2}$ expression for the expected surplus loss. The decoupling of scales---i.e.\ the aggregate scale being strictly above the individual scale---relies in fact only on the integrability of $f$.}, it is not the only mechanism by which a statistic can be second order. The believed cross-attribute association---which we explore in the next subsection---reaches second order by a completely different route. The necessary and sufficient condition for protection is given in Proposition~\ref{prop:C3}.


\subsubsection{Sequence-affected share---incidence versus intensity}
\label{sec:criterion}


The mechanism for the sequence-affected share is largely the same as that for surplus-weighted loss. Both
integrate over the same set---the band of evaluators whose decisions the perturbation flips, of
width $\bigO(c)$ at the threshold---and differ only in what they weight each member by. The surplus criterion weights by the stake $|u|$, which the band itself holds to $\bigO(c)$ whereas the $0$--$1$ criterion weights by $1$ and returns the band's mass alone.

Membership in that band is forced by a comparison of two lengths. An evaluator's distance from
the threshold is $|u|$, and the reading sequence moves their score by $c\delta_\sigma$; a flip
requires the second to be at least the first, so $|u|>|c\delta_\sigma|$ leaves the action
unchanged whichever sequence the cues may be in. As $\delta_\sigma$ is bounded across the population,
only evaluators within $\bigO(c)$ of the threshold can be affected at all. The condition is
necessary but not sufficient---the perturbation must also point towards the threshold rather
than away from it---so the affected form a subset of the band, and it is the band's mass,
$f(0)\cdot\bigO(c)$, that sets the first-order rate.

\renewcommand{\theproposition}{C2}%
\begin{proposition}[sequence-affected share]\label{prop:C2}
Suppose the $u$-marginal is bounded and continuous at $u=0$ with $f(0)>0$ (the same density
hypothesis as Theorem~\ref{thm:B2}). Then under the $0$--$1$ criterion---weight $1$ for each
individual whose Jeffrey and benchmark actions differ, $0$ for everyone else, so that the
aggregate is the \emph{incidence} of sequence-affected individuals, their share of the
population---the aggregate is genuinely $\bigO(c)$, not merely $o(1)$, since the band mass
itself scales as $f(0)\cdot\bigO(c)$. \anchor{Prop.~C2}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Both criteria integrate over the same set, differing only in the weight attached to a member.

\emph{Step 1: the affected set.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:B1} the Jeffrey score perturbs the benchmark
score by $c\delta_\sigma+\bigO(c^{2})$, so
\[
  \text{affected}
  \iff
  \sgn(u)\neq\sgn\bigl(u+c\delta_\sigma+\bigO(c^{2})\bigr)
  \ \Longrightarrow\
  u\in S_c:=\bigl\{\,|u|\le|c\,\delta_\sigma|+\bigO(c^{2})\,\bigr\},
\]
a band of width $\bigO(c)$ about the threshold $u=0$, where $\sgn(u)$ turns over.

\emph{Step 2: the band mass.} With $f$ bounded, continuous at $0$, and $f(0)>0$,
\[
  \mu(S_c)=\int_{|u|\le|c\delta_\sigma|}\!\! f(u)\,du=f(0)\cdot\bigO(c),
\]
first order rather than merely bounded by it, since $f(0)>0$ and $\delta_\sigma\neq0$
generically (Lemma~\ref{lem:B1}).

\emph{Step 3: the two criteria.} On $S_c$ the stake obeys $|u|\le|c\delta_\sigma|=\bigO(c)$,
whence
\[
  \underbrace{\textstyle\int_{S_c} 1\,d\mu}_{\text{incidence}}=\mu(S_c)=\bigO(c),
  \qquad
  \underbrace{\textstyle\int_{S_c} |u|\,d\mu}_{\text{surplus}}
   \le \bigO(c)\cdot\mu(S_c)=\bigO(c^{2})
  \quad (\text{Theorem~\ref{thm:B2}}).
\]
The extra factor of $c$ is the weight $|u|$, which the band forces to first order.
\end{proof}

What follows from the above proposition has strong implications for what an audit can measure. A first-order \emph{number} of individuals are genuinely affected, each near-marginal. The second-order quantities are the surplus-weighted loss and the believed association. Therefore, the audit result cannot ever be phrased as ``few are affected,'' because the incidence is first order \anchor{Prop.~C2}. Whatever significance attaches to those individuals---their decisions turned on the sequence in which two impressions happened to arrive---attaches to this first-order count, not to the second-order aggregates. In other words, the separation of scales is not a reassurance that little is at stake but a statement that the aggregate an observer computes is not a sufficient statistic for what is at stake. That the arbitrarily-decided are individually real yet aggregately invisible is a fact about the resolution of the measuring statistic, carrying whatever normative weight the affected decisions do, and it is exactly the incidence---the unprotected criterion---that carries it. The two questions ``how many are affected?'' and ``what do the aggregate dependence and surplus statistics show?'' thus decouple by one order in $c$. The criterion, not the phenomenon, selects which answer an aggregate instrument returns
(Propositions~\ref{prop:C2} and~\ref{prop:C3}).



\subsection{The protected class of aggregate statistics}
\label{sec:protected}

We now arrive at the essence of the findings of sequence relevance in aggregate -- the necessary and sufficient conditions under which an arbitrary smooth statistic becomes second order. We define a differential condition for a smooth statistic of the average belief---a condition whose fulfilment leaves no first-order trace of the sequence effects. More specifically, this differential condition on $F$ is $\nabla F(q\otimes r)\in\mathrm{span}\{J,\ \nabla\assoc(q\otimes r)\}$ (where $J$ is
the all-ones matrix and $R_1=q\otimes(1,-1)$, $R_2=(1,-1)\otimes r$ are the two route
directions).

Recall that \emph{protection} means that the aggregate's first-order coefficient vanishes identically, so the statistic sits within $\bigO(c^{2})$ of its benchmark value (even
though every member of the population is $\bigO(c)$ away). What we now prove is that a smooth statistic is \emph{protected} exactly when its differential at the independent posterior $q\otimes r$ points along $d\,\assoc$, the differential of the association there, so that to first order the statistic moves only as the association moves; every other smooth statistic
departs at first order at all but exceptional priors. The following proposition formalises this.


\renewcommand{\theproposition}{C3}%
\begin{proposition}[uniqueness of the protected statistic]\label{prop:C3}
Fix $(q_0,r_0)\in(0,1)^2$ and let $F$ be $C^1$ in a neighbourhood of $q\otimes r$ in the
probability simplex, and recall the mean belief $\Pbar_\lambda$ of
Section~\ref{sec:aggregation}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] \textup{(Protection.)} If the differential of $F$ at $q\otimes r$, restricted to the
tangent space of the simplex, is a scalar multiple of $d\,\assoc$, then
$F(\Pbar_\lambda)-F(\PB)=\bigO(c^2)$ for every prior $(\alpha,\beta)$ and every $\lambda$.
\item[(ii)] \textup{(Uniqueness.)} Conversely, fix $\lambda\in(0,1)$. If
$F(\Pbar_\lambda)-F(\PB)=o(c)$ for all $(\alpha,\beta)$ in some open set, then $dF$ at
$q\otimes r$, restricted to the simplex, is a multiple of $d\,\assoc$.
\end{enumerate}
\anchor{Prop.~C3}
\end{proposition}

The proof is provided in Appendix \ref{app:proofs}. What drives it is that each sequence's leading gap is a scalar
multiple of a single rank-one direction---$\kappa\,q\otimes(1,-1)$ for one sequence and, exchanging
the roles of the cues, $\kappa'\,(1,-1)\otimes r$ with $\kappa'=(\beta-r_0)\,q_0(1-q_0)/Z$ for
the other (Proposition~\ref{prop:A2} and its mirror)---so the annihilator of the two directions
is two-dimensional and spanned by the constants and $\nabla\assoc$, and the converse follows
from the nonconstancy of $\kappa/\kappa'$ on any open set of priors.

The smooth statistics protected to second order are exactly the \emph{local dependence
measures}, those that, to first order at independence, depend on the posterior only through its
association. This includes the association itself (Proposition~\ref{prop:C1} is the case
$F=\assoc$), the odds ratio, its logarithm, and Yule's $Q$; among expectations
$\langle v,P\rangle$ it admits exactly those whose $v$ lies in the two-dimensional span of the
constants and $\nabla\assoc$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:B1}). At interior $\lambda$ it excludes every
marginal (the marginal-probability drift of Proposition~\ref{prop:C1m}) except at the priors
where that drift's coefficient vanishes, $\beta=r_{0}$ for the $A$-marginal and $\alpha=q_{0}$
for the $B$-marginal; more generally, no statistic outside the class is second order on an open
set of priors. Together with Theorem~\ref{thm:B2} and Proposition~\ref{prop:C2}, which settle
the two threshold criteria on a common set---counting each sequence-affected individual once
returns the band's $\bigO(c)$ mass, while adding up what each forgoes brings a second factor of
$c$, since everyone in the band is within $\bigO(c)$ of the threshold---this yields a complete
answer for the aggregates at issue.

The quantification over $\lambda$ in part~(i) is what makes the class a classification of
statistics rather than of populations. The aggregate first-order coefficient is
$\lambda\langle\nabla F,\kappa R_1\rangle+(1-\lambda)\langle\nabla F,\kappa' R_2\rangle$, so it
can vanish in two quite different ways. Either both inner products vanish, in which case $F$ does
not respond to either reading sequence and is second order whatever the composition of the
population; or both are nonzero and of opposite sign, in which case the single mixture
$\lambda=\langle\nabla F,\kappa' R_2\rangle/(\langle\nabla F,\kappa' R_2\rangle-\langle\nabla
F,\kappa R_1\rangle)$, when it lies in $(0,1)$, cancels them against each other. A statistic of
the second kind is first order along each reading sequence separately and first order in every other
population, yet indistinguishable from a protected one in the population that happens to cancel
it. Requiring $\bigO(c^2)$ at every $\lambda$ admits only the first kind. Protection in the sense
of Definition~\ref{def:protection} is thus annihilation of the route directions, not cancellation
between them, and the converse holding at every interior $\lambda$ confirms the classification is
not an artefact of any particular composition of encounter sequences. \anchor{Prop.~C3}

In the vignette the classification says which audit questions can register the sequencing, and
the boundary falls in an instructive place. Ask how likely the panels believe a
competent-seeming candidate to be trustworthy, $\Pbar(B{=}1\mid A{=}1)$, and the distortion is
first order, since this conditional probability carries a component along the marginal
probabilities and the marginal probabilities drift at $\bigO(c)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:C1m}).
Ask how much \emph{likelier} they believe a competent-seeming candidate to be trustworthy than
an incompetent-seeming one, $\Pbar(B{=}1\mid A{=}1)-\Pbar(B{=}1\mid A{=}0)$, and the distortion
is second order, since the subtraction removes the component along the marginal probabilities
and leaves a differential at independence that is a positive multiple of $d\,\assoc$, placing
this difference in the protected class. A single subtraction, within one family of summaries,
crosses the boundary. Nor is it the subtracting that does the work---the gap between the two
marginal probabilities, $\Pbar(A{=}1)-\Pbar(B{=}1)$, departs at first order all the same,
because differencing two drifting marginal probabilities removes neither drift. What decides
the sequence of an audit question is the differential test of Proposition~\ref{prop:C3}, not the
family, the units, or the arithmetic shape of the summary that asks it.
\anchor{Prop.~C3; Prop.~C1}


\section{Scope and robustness}
\label{sec:scope}

The domain fixed in Section~\ref{sec:assumptions} by Assumptions~\ref{as:localc}--\ref{as:surplus}
is deliberately narrow, and its boundary is part of the content. Each assumption is load-bearing,
and dropping it reopens a specific failure mode, located exactly below.

The binary two-attribute structure is not an analytical convenience but the canonical object of
the evaluation setting the paper addresses---a threshold decision over latent present-or-absent
attributes is the architecture of the statistical-discrimination models this framework engages
\citep{Phelps1972, Arrow1973, CoateLoury1993}.

Attribute-locality (Assumption~\ref{as:local}) is load-bearing rather than decorative---it is exactly what makes every
Jeffrey update a separable reweighting (Lemma~\ref{lem:sep})---the engine of
Proposition~\ref{prop:C1}. A gestalt cue
bearing jointly on both attributes is out of scope, and, being non-separable, could itself
manufacture association from independence, so the immunity of Proposition~\ref{prop:A1} would
not apply.

Stated affirmatively, three things would break the results, and each is located exactly. A
non-surplus criterion breaks the surplus decoupling (Assumption~\ref{as:surplus}). Under a fixed
per-error cost the aggregate loss \emph{is} the incidence and is first order
(Proposition~\ref{prop:C2})---the criterion-dependence is the content of the paper, not a
weakness of it, and Proposition~\ref{prop:C3} delimits precisely which criteria are protected.

A prior covariance outside the leading-order regime voids the separation
(Assumption~\ref{as:localc}). Assumption~\ref{as:localc} states the results as $c\to0$: they
compare $\bigO(c)$ to $\bigO(c^{2})$, a comparison that is informative only when the prior
covariance $c$ is small relative to its feasibility bound, since $\bigO(c^{2})$ is then
genuinely smaller than $\bigO(c)$. When $c$ is instead a sizable fraction of that bound, the two
orders are no longer numerically distinguishable, and the local-in-$c$ results do not apply.
This is not a claim that the aggregates' distortion changes character at large $c$; it is that
the hypothesis under which $\bigO(c)$ was compared to $\bigO(c^{2})$---$c$ small---no longer
holds, so the theorems make no claim, favourable or unfavourable, about the aggregates'
distortion in that regime.

The population modelled throughout is homogeneous in one further respect---every evaluator
shares the same prior $(\alpha,\beta,c)$, and only the encounter sequence ---and, for the surplus-weighted loss, the stakes $u$---varies across the pool. A population whose members differ in the prior
covariance $c$ itself is a different aggregation problem, outside the stated results.

And a single cue, taken alone, produces no phenomenon at all---on one cue the Jeffrey and
benchmark posteriors coincide exactly (Proposition~\ref{prop:A1}), so sequence-dependence needs a
second, independently arriving cue to act on---exactly the two-cue bundle
Assumption~\ref{as:soft} requires.

\section{Concluding remarks}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The paper's central contribution is a characterisation that delineates the conditions under
which an arbitrary smooth statistic of the average belief is protected, at first order, from the
non-commutativity of coherent sequential updating. This characterisation is built up
from the fundamental observation that the individual gap is not an arbitrary perturbation. Each
reading sequence contributes one route direction ($R_1$ and $R_2$ of
Section~\ref{sec:protected}), so the leading-order departures of the average posterior from the
benchmark are confined to the plane those two directions span. A smooth statistic responds to
that departure only through its differential at independence. Whether a statistic is protected
therefore turns on one geometric question---whether its differential annihilates the route
plane---and Proposition~\ref{prop:C3} shows the annihilating statistics are exactly those whose
differential is a multiple of $d\,\assoc$.

This answer has definite implications for soft-cue updating, which is non-commutative. An aggregate matching the Bayesian
benchmark does not license the inference that its members are Bayesian---the population's mean
believed association and its surplus-weighted loss sit within $\bigO(c^{2})$ of the benchmark
while every member's individual gap diverges at $\bigO(c)$. The characterisation clarifies that this is not because of a
cancellation across the population. Instead, the differential condition annihilates each route direction
separately, so each panel's own believed association is already $\bigO(c^{2})$ from the
benchmark (proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:C1}) and averaging adds nothing. The protected
statistics are, by construction, insensitive at first order to the directions in which coherent
sequential updating moves
beliefs, and aggregate conformity is a property of the statistic doing the measuring, not
evidence that the individual distortions are absent.

The instrumental reading also follows from the characterisation. More particularly, detection
turns on the criterion an instrument aggregates relative to the phenomenon's scale rather than
on sample size---disaggregating a protected aggregate does not change its sequence, and only a
change of criterion, from the surplus-weighted loss or a dependence measure to a statistic
outside the protected class such as the incidence, does. A null reading on an aggregate-built
instrument is therefore not evidence that the underlying phenomenon is null; the instrument has
one order less resolution than the thing it would detect.

The aggregate results are, in this sense, reassuring exactly insofar as they are cautionary.
The same separability that keeps the surplus-weighted loss second order and the mean believed
association first-order immune is what leaves the protected statistics silent about a
first-order incidence of sequence-decided outcomes---the dilution and the concealment are one fact,
and which face an observer meets is set by the criterion, not by the phenomenon.

There are two interpretive limits worth highlighting. First, the model exhibits a configuration in which coherent updating produces sequence-dependent decisions---but it asserts nothing about how often that configuration is realised in actual evaluation settings. Second, the result is
essentially one-directional: it refutes the inference from a null aggregate to a null phenomenon, without validating anything in the other direction. In other words, a null audit is uninformative both ways.

In summary, the main contribution of the paper is that the differential condition is necessary
as well as sufficient (Proposition~\ref{prop:C3}) for smooth statistics of the average belief,
at any interior mixture of reading sequences and across an open set of priors. This establishes
that, for the two-cue setting studied here, there are no protected statistics beyond the local
dependence measures and, for the threshold decision, the surplus-weighted loss. The observational consequence that connects to
the belief-aggregation criterion of Section~\ref{sec:literature} is that at leading order, a
population's aggregate beliefs can look externally Bayesian---sequence-free, benchmark-conforming
on the protected statistics---even when no member's belief is.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:A2}}

\emph{Step 1: coincidence at $c=0$.} The prior factorises, $P(i,j)=P(A{=}i)P(B{=}j)$, and each
Jeffrey step resets one coordinate's marginal while leaving the other untouched, as does the
matched Bayesian update. Hence
\[
  \PJ_{AB}\big|_{c=0}=\PJ_{BA}\big|_{c=0}=\PB\big|_{c=0}=q\otimes r,
  \qquad
  \bigl(\PJ_{AB}-\PB\bigr)\big|_{c=0}
   =\bigl(\PJ_{AB}-\PJ_{BA}\bigr)\big|_{c=0}=0 .
\]

\emph{Step 2: Taylor expansion.} Each posterior is a rational function of $c$ whose denominator
is nonvanishing near $0$, so both differences are analytic there and, having no constant term by
Step 1,
\[
  \PJ_{AB}-\PB=c\,D+\bigO(c^{2}),
  \qquad
  \PJ_{AB}-\PJ_{BA}=c\,\Delta_{\mathrm{seq}}+\bigO(c^{2}) .
\]

\emph{Step 3: the leading gap.} Differentiating at $c=0$, with
$Z=\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)>0$,
\[
  D_{00}=-D_{01}=\frac{q_0 r_0\,(q_0-\alpha)(r_0-1)}{Z},
  \qquad
  D_{10}=-D_{11}=\frac{-\,r_0\,(q_0-\alpha)(q_0-1)(r_0-1)}{Z},
\]
\[
  \text{equivalently}\qquad
  D=\kappa\,q\otimes(1,-1),
  \qquad
  \kappa=\frac{(\alpha-q_0)\,r_0(1-r_0)}{Z} .
\]
The $(1,-1)$ contrast makes the $A$-marginal preservation manifest, $D_{i0}=-D_{i1}$.

\emph{Step 4: $D\not\equiv0$.} The scalar $\kappa$ factors into the surprise in the
$A$-impression and the spread of the $B$-impression,
\[
  \kappa\propto(\alpha-q_0),
  \qquad
  \kappa\propto r_0(1-r_0),
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad
  \kappa=0
  \iff
  q_0=\alpha \ \ \text{or}\ \ r_0\in\{0,1\},
\]
and hence $D\not\equiv0$ for any $q_0\neq\alpha$ in $(0,1)$ and $r_0\in(0,1)$.

\emph{Step 5: the sequence effect.} For the diagonal,
\[
  \Delta_{\mathrm{seq},\,00}
   =\frac{q_0 r_0\bigl[(\alpha-\beta)+r_0(1-\alpha)-q_0(1-\beta)\bigr]}{Z},
\]
whose bracket vanishes only on a single hypersurface, so $\Delta_{\mathrm{seq},\,00}$ is nonzero
generically. On the symmetry locus $\{\alpha=\beta,\ q_0=r_0\}$ the bracket vanishes and the
effect is confined to the antisymmetric off-diagonal part,
\[
  \Delta_{\mathrm{seq},\,01}=-\Delta_{\mathrm{seq},\,10}
   =\frac{q_0(1-q_0)\,(q_0-\alpha)}{\alpha^{2}(1-\alpha)^{2}}
  \ \neq 0
  \quad\text{unless } q_0=\alpha .
\]
Both coefficients satisfy $\sum_k D_k=\sum_k\Delta_{\mathrm{seq},\,k}=0$, as a difference of
probability distributions must.
\qed

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:sep}}

Write the update sequence as $P_{0}=P$ and, for $t=1,\dots,T$,
\[
  P_{t}(x)=P_{t-1}(x)\,h_{t}(x_{a_{t}}),
\]
where $a_{t}$ is the attribute the $t$-th update reads and $h_{t}$ is a nonnegative function
of that single coordinate. Jeffrey updates take exactly this form:
\[
  h(x_{a})=\frac{q_{x_{a}}}{P_{t-1}(A_{a}{=}x_{a})},
\]
state-dependent only through its normaliser $P_{t-1}(A_a{=}x_a)$---a function of $x_{a}$ alone,
which is all the induction below uses.

We claim, by induction on $t$, that
$P_{t}(x)=P(x)\prod_{a=1}^{N}g^{(t)}_{a}(x_{a})$ for some single-attribute functions
$g^{(t)}_{a}$. The base case $t=0$ holds with $g^{(0)}_{a}\equiv1$. For the inductive step,
suppose the claim at $t-1$; then
\[
\begin{gathered}
  P_{t}(x)
  =P(x)\Big[\prod_{a}g^{(t-1)}_{a}(x_{a})\Big]h_{t}(x_{a_{t}})
  =P(x)\prod_{a}g^{(t)}_{a}(x_{a}),
  \\[4pt]
  g^{(t)}_{a}:=
  \begin{cases}
    g^{(t-1)}_{a}\cdot h_{t}, & a=a_{t},\\[2pt]
    g^{(t-1)}_{a}, & a\neq a_{t}
  \end{cases}
\end{gathered}
\]
the new factor folds into $g^{(t)}_{a_t}$ alone; no step couples two coordinates. Taking
$g_{a}:=g^{(T)}_{a}$ gives the displayed form.

For the equivalent formulation, on the common support (cells with $P(x)>0$ and every
accumulated factor positive) take logarithms:
\[
  \log\frac{P'(x)}{P(x)}=\sum_{a=1}^{N}\log g_{a}(x_{a}),
\]
an additive function of the individual coordinates; write $\varphi:=\log(P'/P)$. For any pair
$a<b$, define the pairwise contrast
\[
  \Delta_{ab}\varphi(x)\;:=\;\varphi(x)\big|_{x_{a}=1,x_{b}=1}-\varphi(x)\big|_{x_{a}=1,x_{b}=0}
  -\varphi(x)\big|_{x_{a}=0,x_{b}=1}+\varphi(x)\big|_{x_{a}=0,x_{b}=0},
\]
holding every coordinate other than $a,b$ fixed. Writing $R:=\sum_{c\neq a,b}\log g_{c}(x_{c})$
for the (fixed) contribution of the remaining coordinates,
\begin{align*}
  \Delta_{ab}\varphi
  &=\bigl[\log g_{a}(1)+\log g_{b}(1)+R\bigr]-\bigl[\log g_{a}(1)+\log g_{b}(0)+R\bigr]\\
  &\quad-\bigl[\log g_{a}(0)+\log g_{b}(1)+R\bigr]+\bigl[\log g_{a}(0)+\log g_{b}(0)+R\bigr]
  \;=\;0,
\end{align*}
valid for every pair $a<b$ and every fixing of the other $N-2$ coordinates. Higher-order
contrasts telescope into this one: for any three coordinates $a,b,c$, and any $\varphi$
whatsoever (additive or not),
\[
  \Delta_{abc}\varphi \;=\; \Delta_{ab}\varphi\big|_{x_{c}=1}-\Delta_{ab}\varphi\big|_{x_{c}=0},
\]
and analogously at every order above three, so $\Delta_{ab}\varphi\equiv0$ for all pairs forces
every $\Delta_{S}\varphi\equiv0$, $|S|\ge2$---exactly the interaction coefficients $\lambda_{S}$
of the log-linear (M\"obius) decomposition
$\varphi=\lambda_{\emptyset}+\sum_{a}\lambda_{a}(x_{a})+\sum_{a<b}\lambda_{ab}(x_{a},x_{b})
+\cdots$. Conversely,
\[
  \lambda_{\emptyset}:=\varphi(0),\qquad
  \lambda_{a}(1):=\varphi(x_{a}{=}1,x_{-a}{=}0)-\varphi(0),\qquad \lambda_{a}(0):=0
\]
reproduces $\varphi(x)$ exactly at every $x$ once every pairwise contrast vanishes, by the same
telescope run backward. Separability of $P'/P$ and vanishing interaction of order $\ge2$ are
thus the same condition both ways. Nothing above is specific to Jeffrey updating, $N=2$, or
sequence length.
\qed

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:B2}}

\emph{Step 1: bounded coefficient.} The two actions differ iff
$\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)$. At the fixed prior and impressions
$\|\partial_c(\PJ_\sigma-\PB)|_{0}\|<\infty$, and $\|\vv\|\le V<\infty$ across the population
(Assumption~\ref{as:surplus}), so
\[
  |\delta_\sigma|
   =\bigl|\langle\vv,\partial_c(\PJ_\sigma-\PB)|_{0}\rangle\bigr|
   \le M<\infty,
  \qquad \sigma\in\{AB,BA\}.
\]

\emph{Step 2: the flip set lies in a thin band.} For fixed $\delta$ the flip set in $u$ is the
open interval strictly between $0$ and $-c\delta$, whence
\[
  \{\mathrm{flip}\}\ \subseteq\ B_c:=\{\,u:\ 0<|u|\le cM\,\},
  \qquad
  u\in B_c\ \Longrightarrow\ |u|\le cM.
\]
That the band excludes $u=0$ does matter in the later Step~4.

\emph{Step 3: the bound.} With $\mu$ the marginal law of $u$, Tonelli's theorem
\citep[Corollary~1.7.23]{tao2011measure} gives
\[
  L(c)=\iint_{\mathrm{flip}}|u|\,g(u,\delta)\,du\,d\delta
   \;\le\; cM\!\iint_{u\in B_c}\! g(u,\delta)\,du\,d\delta
   \;=\; cM\,\mu(B_c).
\]

\emph{Step 4: the band is null in the limit.} The bands nest and exhaust,
\[
  c'<c\ \Longrightarrow\ B_{c'}\subseteq B_c,
  \qquad
  \bigcap_{c>0}B_c=\varnothing,
\]
since $u\neq0$ exits once $cM<|u|$ and $u=0$ lies in no $B_c$. As $\mu$ is finite, continuity
from above \citep[\S1.4]{tao2011measure} yields
\[
  \mu(B_c)\downarrow\mu(\varnothing)=0,
  \qquad
  \frac{L(c)}{c}\le M\,\mu(B_c)\longrightarrow0,
  \qquad
  L(c)=o(c),
\]
for \emph{any} integrable density, and irrespective of an atom at $u=0$, where $|u|=0$.

\emph{Step 5: named constant available under continuity.} Let the $u$-marginal be continuous at $0$ with
$f(0)>0$. For fixed $\delta$,
\[
  \int_0^{|c\delta|}t\,g(0,\delta)\,dt=\tfrac{1}{2}\,g(0,\delta)\,\delta^{2}c^{2}+\bigO(c^3),
\]
and with
\[
  f(0)=\int g(0,\delta)\,d\delta,
  \qquad
  \E[\delta^2\mid u=0]=\frac{1}{f(0)}\int\delta^2 g(0,\delta)\,d\delta,
\]
integration over $\delta$ gives
\[
  L(c)=\frac{f(0)}{2}\,\E[\delta_\sigma^2\mid u=0]\,c^{2}+\bigO(c^3).
\]
\qed

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:C3}}

\emph{Step 1: the aggregate moves in a plane.} By Proposition~\ref{prop:A2} and its mirror,
\[
  \PJ_{AB}-\PB=c\,\kappa R_1+\bigO(c^2),
  \qquad
  \PJ_{BA}-\PB=c\,\kappa' R_2+\bigO(c^2),
\]
\[
  R_1=q\otimes(1,-1),\quad \kappa=\frac{(\alpha-q_0)r_0(1-r_0)}{Z},
  \qquad
  R_2=(1,-1)\otimes r,\quad \kappa'=\frac{(\beta-r_0)q_0(1-q_0)}{Z},
\]
so with $M_\lambda:=\lambda\kappa R_1+(1-\lambda)\kappa' R_2$ the chain rule gives
\[
  \Pbar_\lambda-\PB=c\,M_\lambda+\bigO(c^2),
  \qquad
  F(\Pbar_\lambda)-F(\PB)=c\,\bigl\langle\nabla F(q\otimes r),M_\lambda\bigr\rangle+\bigO(c^2).
\]

\emph{Step 2: the annihilator is $\mathrm{span}\{J,\nabla\assoc\}$.} The directions are
independent---$aR_1+bR_2=0$ gives $b(r_0+r_1)=b=0$ on adding the top-row entries, then $aq_0=0$
and $a=0$---so
\[
  \dim\bigl\{V\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times2}:\ \langle V,R_1\rangle=\langle V,R_2\rangle=0\bigr\}=4-2=2 .
\]
Both $J$ (all entries $1$) and $\nabla\assoc(q\otimes r)$ lie in this space,
\[
  \langle J,R_1\rangle=\langle J,R_2\rangle=0,
  \qquad
  \langle\nabla\assoc,R_1\rangle=\langle\nabla\assoc,R_2\rangle=0
  \quad(\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:A3}}),
\]
and are linearly independent, hence span it.

\emph{Step 3: part (i).} If $dF|_{q\otimes r}$ restricted to the simplex is a multiple of
$d\,\assoc$, then
\[
  \nabla F(q\otimes r)=xJ+y\,\nabla\assoc(q\otimes r)
  \ \Longrightarrow\
  \bigl\langle\nabla F,M_\lambda\bigr\rangle=0
  \quad\text{identically in }(\alpha,\beta,\lambda).
\]

\emph{Step 4: part (ii).} Put $x=\langle\nabla F,R_1\rangle$ and $y=\langle\nabla F,R_2\rangle$,
so the first-order coefficient $\lambda\kappa\,x+(1-\lambda)\kappa'\,y$ vanishes on an open set
$U$ of priors. Suppose $y\neq0$. If $x=0$ then $\kappa'y\equiv0$ on $U$, and $\kappa'$ vanishes
only on $\{\beta=r_0\}$, forcing $y=0$; so $x\neq0$ and, on $U\setminus\{\beta=r_0\}$,
\[
  \frac{\kappa}{\kappa'}=-\frac{(1-\lambda)y}{\lambda x}=\text{const},
  \qquad\text{while}\qquad
  \frac{\kappa}{\kappa'}=\frac{(\alpha-q_0)\,r_0(1-r_0)}{(\beta-r_0)\,q_0(1-q_0)},
\]
\[
  \frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\frac{\kappa}{\kappa'}
   =\frac{r_0(1-r_0)}{(\beta-r_0)\,q_0(1-q_0)}\neq0
  \quad\text{wherever }\beta\neq r_0,
\]
a contradiction. Hence $y=0$, then $\kappa x\equiv0$ on $U$ forces $x=0$, so
$\nabla F(q\otimes r)\in\mathrm{span}\{J,\nabla\assoc\}$. On the tangent space of the simplex,
whose vectors have coordinates summing to zero, $J$ acts as zero, so $dF$ is a multiple of
$d\,\assoc$.
\qed

The interior-$\lambda$ hypothesis in \emph{(ii)} is essential rather than technical. At
$\lambda=0$ or $\lambda=1$ only one route direction enters the first-order coefficient, and
the annihilator of a single direction is three-dimensional rather than two, so vanishing on
an open set of priors pins $\nabla F$ only to that larger space, not to the span of the
constants and $\nabla\assoc$.
