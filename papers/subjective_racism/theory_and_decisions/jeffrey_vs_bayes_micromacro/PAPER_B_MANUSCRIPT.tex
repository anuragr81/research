\begin{abstract}
The effect of the order in which evidence arrives on an individual judgment has sparked much interest in both experimental and theoretical studies. Considering a case where evaluators make decisions using two correlated soft cues, the current papers asks which of their beliefs and decision statistics register a resulting effect of arrival sequence with respective to a sequence-free benchmark. With a view of implications for empirical measurement, the paper shows that an arbitrary statistic is an order of magnitude closer to the benchmark when it is insensitive to small shifts in the marginal probabilities. The paper shows how the collective marginal probabilities and the share of evaluators whose decision the sequence changes depart from the sequence-free benchmark at first order, whereas the believed association departs only at second order.  Two implications follow for measurement of sequence effects through audits. First, an aggregate conforming to the benchmark is no evidence that reading sequence does not affect evaluator beliefs. Second, whether the effect of order is detectable or not depends on the kind of question being asked rather than on the number of evaluators considered.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

That the sequence in which evidence arrives can move the final judgment is among the oldest findings in the study of impression formation \citep{Asch1946,HogarthEinhorn1992} yet the measurement of sequence dependence has rarely gathered attention. That arrival sequence of cues does effect judgment is rarely under doubt -- since if individual judgments did not depend on the arrival sequence, then each cue must move belief by a factor fixed before it is received and lead to the unlikely situation where experience does not change how later cues are read at all. The sequence-dependence of Jeffrey conditioning for soft evidence in particular has attracted much debate \citep{DiaconisZabell1982,Hawthorne2004}. However, the question of how that dependence registers in the statistics an observer reads has received little attention. The current paper uses a two-attribute setting under Jeffrey conditioning to explore how certain statistics respond better to sequence-affected beliefs than others. It finds that the share of population affected by evidence sequence deviates from a sequence-invariant benchmark -- but the believed association between attributes and the total loss due to sequence effects do not. The paper then pins down what makes an arbitrary smooth statistic become different from a sequence-free benchmark at first-order. Given that a difference from the benchmark below the observer's precision is invisible in the statistic read,  the paper discusses the implications for identification of sequence effects. Despite its simplicity the two-cue setting  demonstrates the central mechanism of how an attribute-local updating leaves the interactions among attributes untouched remains valid for any number of cues and attributes.

The discussions on how Jeffrey\citep{Jeffrey1983} conditioning  depends on arrival sequence have focused more often on whether the commutation criterion is exactly satisfied or not but not enough on how measurable its failure is in observed data. The belief-adjustment literature often models an impression  as a single score \citep{Hawthorne2004} or considers traits one attribute at a time \citep{Asch1946}. The characterisation of when two sequences lead to the same belief does consider a joint belief over attributes\citet{DiaconisZabell1982}, but the order dependence is more often treated as a defect to repair \footnote{\citet{Hawthorne2004} repairs this by letting a cue multiply the old belief instead of replacing it, and under his order-free variants the sequence effect disappears.} rather than as something to measure. While changing the input from a credence that replaces the prior to a factor that multiplies it -- does settle whether beliefs depend on sequence, it hardly addresses the empirical issue that an observer cannot tell whether impressions replace prior belief or adjust it.

Whether impressions replace prior belief or adjust is a question which \citet{Hawthorne2004}, writing as a logician rather than a psychologist, leave open. To set the scope of its conclusions, the paper considers that an arrival-sequence dependence can enter through two channels, an \textbf{association} channel, in which one cue changes what the other implies and which exists only when the attributes are believed related, and a \textbf{position} channel, in which the observer weights the later cue less whatever the attributes are \citep{HogarthEinhorn1992,Asch1946}. In the position channel, the later cue is adopted only in part and every marginal registers the sequence even when the attributes are believed unrelated. On the other hand, when each impression is adopted in full, as in the successive updating of \citet{DiaconisZabell1982}, the position channel is disabled with only the association channel remaining. In the two-attribute setting with full-adoption, the paper thus considers three coordinates of a belief  -- two marginal probabilities and the cross-attribute association -- and explains how a difference from the sequence-free benchmark below the observer's precision is invisible in the statistic read by the observer. The question is therefore not simply whether the two sequences agree but by how much they disagree in an arbitrary statistic. The paper finds that the marginal probabilities and decisions from them carry the difference at first order in the prior covariance, while the believed association and statistics carry it only at second order. This carries clear implications for what an audit can or cannot measure about sequence dependence -- an observation which may elucidate the formational mechanisms biases and prejudice.

The vignette used to discuss this setting consists of a hiring panel working through a candidate's file on the basis of two pieces of evidence that bear on the decision---i) a credential that speaks to competence, and ii) the tone of a reference letter that speaks to trustworthiness. Both are soft evidence in the sense of \citet{Jeffrey1983} so that neither is decisive but each leaves an impression. While the mechanism can extend to higher number of cues, we focus on the sequence dependence in a situation where one panel may read the credential first (the letter second) and an otherwise identical panel may read them in reverse. If the two attributes are believed to go together at all, the two panels finish with different beliefs about the same candidate. In the context of soft evidence the stake is real for the individual in the sense that identical evidence can license different decisions, with the difference set by arrival sequence.  Apart from showing how the sequence dependence of some statistics can be invisible to an observer, the panel example also shows why that invisibility needs each impression to be adopted in full. When each impression is adopted in full, the letter fixes the belief about trustworthiness whatever the credential implied, so the sequence acts only through the believed link between the traits. As the paper shows, this means that the believed association differs between the two sequences only at second order. On the other hand, a letter adopted only in part moves that belief from wherever the credential left it (and the believed association differs at first order - see Proposition LAD). The paper therefore restricts attention to situations in which unrelated traits exhibit no sequence dependence through the position channel, and studies the measurement issues under the association channel.

% What remains untouched at every degree of adoption is the odds ratio, since each impression rescales rows or columns of the belief and never the pairing within them (Lemma SEP). Since the odds ratio between the two traits remains the same under Jeffrey conditioning, it is only the size of the differences in panels' ratings where the extent of overwriting of impressions can matter.

The conclusions of the paper apply only when evidence is soft and when it can be assumed that impressions arrive as revised credences, not as
likelihoods. Read as a level, the credential leaves the panelist in the example feeling the candidate competent, and what it delivers is a credence on the attribute's partition into competent and not-competent categories. Read as a Bayes factor, the credential carries a likelihood ratio stating how much more probable it is under competence than in its absence, and that ratio multiplies whatever belief it meets. A Bayes factor, however, requires the probability of the same credential for a candidate who is not competent, and an impression does not supply that counterfactual. As a mechanism that holds conditionals fixed, Jeffrey updating is the unique coherent revision for credence delivered on the cue's partition and is equivalent to making the minimal change of the prior consistent with the delivered marginal \citep{DiaconisZabell1982}\footnote{See \citet{Field1978} for how Jeffrey updates are asymmetric in the delivered-credence parametrisation and \citet{Wagner2002} for how they commute when their inputs are held fixed in Bayes-factor form.}.

To avoid a clash in terminology we use ``order'' to describe proximity with a sequence-free Bayesian bechmark and ``sequence`` to denote the sequence in which evidence is read. Thus sequence effect is a sequential, myopic structure that makes Jeffrey conditioning the coherent response to a delivered credence. The distinction between first and second order on the other hand, is what an observer can or cannot see. More specifically, if the strength with which the two traits are believed to go together were represented with a prior covariance $c$, a departure from sequence-free benchmark is resolvable only when its size exceeds the observer's measurement precision $\varepsilon$. For small enough $c$ the two sequences separate, since $\varepsilon$ can sit between them, $c^{2}\ll\varepsilon\ll c$. A first-order departure is then resolvable and a second-order one is not, so the same population can present a visible effect on one statistic and none on another. The sequence-dependence of marginals follows easily -- since the likelihood a delivered credence implies must be read against the marginal in force when the cue arrives \citep{Domotor1980}. What is less obvious is the finding that some aggregate statistics are structurally blind to arrival order. 

The key finding is presented in two parts. First, we establish that while evaluator diverges from the benchmark at first order in $c$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV}), the average believed association between attributes (Proposition~\ref{prop:DEC}) and the surplus-weighted loss of the population's decisions (Theorem~\ref{thm:LOS}) are second order. Second, we characterise exactly which statistics can register the phenomenon at its own order. More specifically, for every interior mixture of reading sequences and across an open set of priors, a smooth statistic of the average belief is second order if and only if its differential at independence is proportional to the differential of the believed cross-attribute association (Proposition~\ref{prop:PRO}). The implication for evaluation is that and audit's answer depends on which statistic is aggregated rather than on how many evaluators are averaged over. One consequence, is that the instruments most naturally trusted for detecting stereotype---belief audits asking how attributes are thought to go together---may not even be detectable.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Decision problem and surplus criterion}
\label{sec:decision}

Two binary attributes $(A,B)\in\{0,1\}^{2}$---competence and trustworthiness, say---are each present or absent, so a belief assigns probability to the four configurations at once. It is thus a \emph{joint law}, a single distribution over the pairs $(i,j)$---a point in the probability simplex $\Delta^{3}$---rather than a pair of one-attribute distributions \anchor{Setup}. The two marginal distributions of the joint law record only how likely each attribute is on its own. The fixed marginals---$P(A{=}0)=\alpha$ and $P(B{=}0)=\beta$---then leave a whole family of joint laws
open---with its members differing precisely in how the attributes are believed to co-move. 

\begin{definition}[cross-attribute association]\label{def:assoc}
For a joint law $Q$ on $\{0,1\}^2$, the \emph{cross-attribute association} is
\[
  \assoc(Q):=Q_{00}Q_{11}-Q_{01}Q_{10},
\]
which exists only on the joint law, never on its two marginals separately. Evaluated at the prior, $\assoc(P)=c$.
\end{definition}

The prior $P$ is the evaluator's subjective credence, before any cue arrives, over the joint attributes of a randomly drawn candidate. It constitutes what a panelist believes about an applicant drawn at random before the file is opened, a probability for each of the four combinations $(i,j)$ of competence and trustworthiness. It concerns the applicant's traits alone, and no outcome of the hire enters it. The prior is a point of $\Delta^{3}$ fixed by three numbers, the marginals
$P(A{=}0)=\alpha$ and $P(B{=}0)=\beta$ and the prior covariance $c$ between the
attributes,
\[
  P=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha\beta+c & \alpha(1-\beta)-c\\[2pt](1-\alpha)\beta-c & (1-\alpha)(1-\beta)+c\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad \operatorname{Cov}(A,B)=c,
\]

The prior marginals $\alpha$ and $\beta$ and the prior covariance $c$ are the evaluator's own beliefs about a candidate drawn at random, not frequencies in
the real-world. In other words, the evaluator is not estimating them and no observed rate enters. When $c=0$ the two attributes are believed independent. The range of $c$ is
fixed by the requirement that all four cells of $P$ stay nonnegative, which
bounds it above by the smaller of $\alpha(1-\beta)$ and $(1-\alpha)\beta$ and
below by minus the smaller of $\alpha\beta$ and $(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)$. These
are the Fr\'echet--Hoeffding bounds for a $2\times2$ table. Simply put, the marginals $\alpha=P(A{=}0)$ and $\beta=P(B{=}0)$ denote the shares
of applicants the panelist believes to lack competence and to lack trustworthiness,
and the prior covariance $c=\assoc(P)$ denotes how strongly the panelist believes
the
two traits to go together among applicants. A positive $c$ means a competent
applicant is believed the more likely to be trustworthy, and $c=0$ means the
panelist believes the traits unrelated. It is elicited by a question about both
traits at once, since neither marginal carries it.

The evaluator receives two attribute-local \emph{soft cues} so that a cue on $A$
yields an impression (a target marginal) $q=(q_{0},q_{1})$ on $A$'s partition,
with $q_i$ the credence that $A=i$ and $q_0+q_1=1$, and a cue on $B$ yields an
impression $r=(r_{0},r_{1})$ on $B$'s partition, with $r_j$ the credence that $B=j$
and $r_0+r_1=1$, and no cue bears on a joint event. An impression is thus a
probability distribution on one attribute, on the same scale as the prior marginal
it replaces, $(\alpha,1-\alpha)$ for $q$ and $(\beta,1-\beta)$ for $r$. In other words, there is no cue that bears on both attributes jointly and the two cues in the model are never fused into one joint event (see Assumption~\ref{as:local}). Each impression then is a delivered credence, not a likelihood, and the product of the two impressions can be written $q\otimes r$, with $(q\otimes r)_{ij}=q_{i}r_{j}$ \anchor{Setup}. For the two-cues setting, a sequence $\sigma\in\{AB,BA\}$ is whether a panel read the credential or the letter first, which an observer records or an experimenter assigns, and $\lambda\in[0,1]$ is the share of panels that read the credential first, which is counted where the sequence is recorded and unknown in a pooled audit. To compare full adoption with the belief-adjustment model of
\citet{HogarthEinhorn1992}, in which a later impression is adopted only in part, we
also consider an adoption weight $\omega\in[0,1]$ (Section~\ref{sec:robust}), which
denotes how far the letter
displaces what the credential had already implied about trustworthiness. At
$\omega=1$ the letter sets the rating outright, and at $\omega=0$ the credential's
implication is never moved.

The engagement decision for the evaluator is defined in terms of a desirability vector $\vv=(v_{ij})_{i,j\in\{0,1\}}$---one entry per state-space cell (the same shape as $P$). With the score $s(P)=\langle\vv,P\rangle=\sum_{ij}v_{ij}P(i,j)$, the evaluator engages if and only if
$s(P)\ge\tau$ \anchor{Setup}. This is expected-utility maximisation at the belief $P$---so that $s(P)$ is the expected desirability of engaging, and $\tau$ is the value of the outside option one forgoes by not engaging. Whether sequence-dependence changes what evaluators do or not is driven by the expected desirability an evaluator forgoes when their sequence-dependent belief leads them to a different action than a benchmark belief. 

As we further detail in Section \ref{sec:jeffrey}, the Bayes-factor benchmark $\PB$ is used as a sequence-free reference \citep{Wagner2002} to define a surplus. Writing $u:=s(\PB)-\tau$ for the net surplus of engaging at the benchmark belief, the cost of taking the action that differs from the benchmark action is $|u|$. More specifically, if $u>0$, the benchmark says engage but the evaluator doesn't and they forgo a gain of $u$---so that the cost is $|u|$. Likewise, when $u<0$, the benchmark says not to engage, but the evaluator does, with the cost again being $|u|$. Whether engagement deviates from the benchmark is what feeds the aggregation picture, with
$\tau$ entering the results only through $u=s(\PB)-\tau$ and never appearing on its own.

The surplus thus defined is what is of interest as the notion of loss in the population aggregate. With $\delta_\sigma$ as the first-order coefficient with which an evaluator's own score departs from the benchmark's under reading sequence $\sigma$ (see Section~\ref{sec:individual}), $g$ as the joint density of $(u,\delta_\sigma)$ across the population and the sequence distribution, the expected foregone surplus is defined as 
\[
  L(c)=\iint_{\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta)\}}|u|\,g(u,\delta)\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}\delta,
\]
the cost $|u|$ borne by exactly those evaluators whose decision the sequence changes, the integration variable $\delta$ ranging over the values taken by $\delta_\sigma$.

\subsection{The Bayes-factor benchmark}
\label{sec:jeffrey}

Jeffrey's rule updates on an impression by resetting the cue's marginal to the delivered
credence and holding the conditionals given the cue's partition fixed, the invariance
condition \citep[ch.~3]{Jeffrey2004}, which \citet[ch.~11]{Jeffrey1983} expresses by
saying that the change originates in the partition. For an impression $q$ on $A$,
\[
  (\PJ_{A}P)(i,j)=q_{i}\,P(i,j)/P(A{=}i),
\]
and symmetrically for an impression $r$ on $B$ \anchor{Setup}. Two results of \citet{DiaconisZabell1982} support the rule. The invariance
condition makes the cue's partition sufficient for the prior and posterior, and
minimal whenever the delivered credence differs from the prior marginal (their
Theorem~2.2). The rule is also the unique minimiser of the $I$-divergence from the
prior among all beliefs with the delivered marginal (their Theorem~5.1). The
two-cue Jeffrey posteriors are the two sequences
\[
  \PJ_{AB}=\PJ_{B}\!\circ\PJ_{A}\,P,\qquad \PJ_{BA}=\PJ_{A}\!\circ\PJ_{B}\,P .
\]

\begin{definition}[sequence effect]\label{def:seqeffect}
The \emph{sequence effect} is the difference $\PJ_{AB}-\PJ_{BA}$ between the beliefs two
evaluators reach from the same prior and the same two impressions, differing only in which cue
they met first. It is the object of study at the individual level.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[gap]\label{def:indgap}
The \emph{gap} is the difference between either sequence-posterior and the benchmark,
$\PJ_\sigma-\PB$ for $\sigma\in\{AB,BA\}$ \anchor{Setup}.
\end{definition}

The benchmark $\PB$ is not a rival updating rule but the same two impressions
re-expressed---each read as a multiplicative factor relative to the prior marginal. Each impression's evidential content can be written as a Bayes factor---a likelihood
matched to the cue given the prior, $\ell^{A}_{i}\propto q_{i}/P(A{=}i)$ and
$\ell^{B}_{j}\propto r_{j}/P(B{=}j)$---and, unlike a delivered credence, a Bayes factor carries
the same content whatever prior it meets. Combining the two cues' Bayes-factor content is
sequence-invariant \citep{Wagner2002}, and $\PB$ is the single update on that combined content,
\[
  \PB(i,j)\propto P(i,j)\,\ell^{A}_{i}\,\ell^{B}_{j},
\]
well-defined regardless of which cue arrived first \anchor{Setup}. As we later show, it coincides
on any one cue with the matched Jeffrey step (Proposition~\ref{prop:IMM}). We compare sequential updating on delivered credences (which does not commute) against the one-shot updating on Bayes-factor content (which does). 

\par\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.4pt}
\begin{example}[the hiring panel]\label{ex:panel}
Let $\alpha=\beta=\tfrac12$ and $c=\tfrac1{20}$,
so that the prior table is
$P=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}.30&.20\\.20&.30\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, each trait
judged as likely absent as present and the two believed to go together. The
credential delivers $q_0=\tfrac15$ and the letter $r_0=\tfrac7{10}$. After the
credential alone the table is
$\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}.12&.08\\.32&.48\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. The
$A$-marginal is $(.20,.80)$, as delivered, and the $B$-marginal has moved from
$(.50,.50)$ to $(.44,.56)$ although no cue about $B$ has been read. Reading the
letter next resets the $B$-marginal to $(.70,.30)$ and moves the $A$-marginal
from $.20$ to $\PJ_{AB}(A{=}0)=.234$. In the reverse sequence the $A$-marginal
ends at $.20$, as delivered, and the $B$-marginal at $\PJ_{BA}(B{=}0)=.643$ in
place of $.70$. The benchmark gives $\PB(A{=}0)=.227$ and $\PB(B{=}0)=.647$. The
sequence effect is therefore $.034$ on the $A$-marginal and $.057$ on the
$B$-marginal. On the believed association it is $.0002$, since
$\assoc(\PJ_{AB})=.0273$ and $\assoc(\PJ_{BA})=.0271$, smaller than either
marginal effect by a factor above $150$. The odds ratio equals $9/4$ for the
prior, for both sequences and for the benchmark. Against the benchmark, sequence
$AB$ departs by $.053$ on the marginal read last, by $.007$ on the marginal read
first, and by $.002$ on the association.
\par
\emph{Why a soft cue differs from a hard one.} Suppose instead that the letter
were a hard cue, a proposition $F$ the panel becomes certain of, with a likelihood
fixed before any belief is formed, say $P(F\mid B{=}0)/P(F\mid B{=}1)=7/3$, and the
credential likewise a proposition $E$ with ratio $1/4$. Conditioning on $E$ and then
on $F$ is conditioning on $E\cap F$, so the two sequences give the same belief, which
is the benchmark $\PB$, with $A$-marginal $.227$ in either sequence. The soft letter
delivers the credence $r_0=\tfrac7{10}$ instead. The factor that credence implies is
the ratio of the odds it delivers to the odds it meets, $7/3$ when the letter is
read first against the prior marginal $\tfrac12$, but $98/33$ when it is read after
the credential, against the marginal $.44$ that the credential has already moved.
The panel knows the credence the letter left, not what that credence would have
been at another prior, and so cannot extract a portable factor from it. That
dependence of the implied factor on the belief it meets is the whole of the
sequence effect, and it vanishes at $c=0$, where the credential leaves the
$B$-marginal where it was and the two implied factors coincide.
\end{example}

\subsection{Assumptions}
\label{sec:assumptions}

With the above setup, we now state four key assumptions that limit the scope of our discussion---fixing which reading sequences, which cues, and which criterion the results apply to. The assumptions are revisited again in Section~\ref{sec:scope} to discuss how relaxing one or more of these assumptions would affect our key conclusions.

\begin{assumption}[Locality in $c$]\label{as:localc}
The prior covariance $c$ between the two correlated attributes is treated as small, and results
are derived as expansions near $c=0$ rather than as statements holding for arbitrary $c$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Soft cues]\label{as:soft}
The cues are bundled, correlated, and soft, each delivering a credence on an attribute's partition,
not a decisive cue.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Attribute-locality]\label{as:local}
Each impression is a credence on a single attribute's partition, never on a joint event.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Surplus criterion]\label{as:surplus}
Loss is measured by foregone surplus (cost $|u|$), with a threshold density that is at least
integrable and with the population's desirability vectors uniformly bounded.
\end{assumption}

Before we detail what the above assumptions imply for sequence-dependent decisions in a population aggregate, we discuss how the model relates with other approaches to non-commutativity of sequence in the literature.

\section{Related literature}
\label{sec:literature}

Whether the sequence dependence of Jeffrey's probability kinematics is a problem has long been debated \citep{DiaconisZabell1982}. The dependence has been questioned on two grounds. \citet{Doring1999} objects on a normative ground -- arguing that it is unjustified in an account of rational belief change when nothing essential turns on the sequence in which experiences arrive. \citet{Hawthorne2004} compares the usual extension of Jeffrey updating, whose full-adoption premise he calls the Amnestic Update-Factor Thesis and which the present paper adopts, with two factor models, and objects to it on psychological grounds. Judging amnestic updating to be the less plausible alternative, he turns to the normative properties of the models -- declining to fully settle the objection\footnote{ ``Which extension of Basic Jeffrey Updating is the more plausible model of human agents? I'm a logician, not a psychologist'' \citep{Hawthorne2004}}.

Two responses have been made to the sequence dependence that full adoption brings. One treats it as a defect of the framework. \citet{Doring1999} argues that the sequence effect can
be pronounced enough to call for an adjustment that successive Jeffrey steps cannot supply, though a single Jeffrey step from the original prior can. The other
re-describes the input so that the effect disappears. \citet{Field1978} reparametrises the update so that its input is a portable factor rather than a
delivered credence, and \citet{Wagner2002} shows that when identical learning is represented by identical Bayes factors, sequential revisions commute. The present paper takes neither of the two routes. It accepts the sequence dependence that the delivered-credence reading entails, and asks how far the two
sequences disagree in each statistic of the resulting belief. Instead of being the property of the observer (position channel), sequence dependence is treated as a property of believed association between traits (association channel). The disagreement is uneven across statistics, first order in the marginals and the
share of decisions changed and second order in the believed association and the
surplus-weighted loss, so whether the sequence dependence is a problem depends on
which statistic an observer reads.

\par

The ground on which the current paper adopts the premise is information-theoretic, since Jeffrey's rule is the revision that moves the prior least in $I$-divergence while taking on the delivered credence \citep{DiaconisZabell1982}. Since sequence dependence of cues is indeed observed in practice, the only way for sequence dependence to exist under partial-adoption (position channel) is for it to be there for unrelated attributes as well. The current paper thus explores full-adoption only to focus on how sequence dependence may arise from believed association between attributes. For the purposes of this paper, an objection of Hawthorne's form is a reason to measure the degree of adoption, not a reason to discard the premise. 

\par
Further afield, sequence dependence has been of interest to empirical psychology
at least since \citet{Asch1946}, and in economics has been taken up chiefly through
the observational learning literature, though it also arises in the dynamics of
discrimination \citep{Bohren2019}. \citet{BHW1992} and \citet{Banerjee1992}, for
example, consider herd-behaviour and informational cascades in which sequence matters. In
\citet{Banerjee1992}, the sequence persists because a coarse public action fails to be a
sufficient statistic for private information. In \citet{BHW1992}, on the other hand, a
cascade is an action that conveys no private signal. The context of the paper differs significantly
from that of existing Bayesian approaches in several ways. First, the object
of interest in the current paper is a literal permutation non-commutativity of a fixed
pair of inputs read by a single agent. Second, instead of the sequencing occurring across different agents (the sequence in
which predecessors act), the current paper considers two cues read by the same agent
(i.e.\ the sequence in which one evaluator meets two cues). Finally, the agents are assumed to be independent of each other in their actions. Instead of coupling being a feed-forward
informational externality therefore---with agent $n$'s action entering agent $n{+}1$'s information set---the agents in the current model do not have any feedback. One similarity with the literature, however,
is that the sequence effect in \citep{BHW1992, Banerjee1992} lives on the analogue of the
marginals---which remain so in the current paper as well.

Decision theory has axiomatised several alternatives to Bayesian updating, each
for a single piece of news. \citet{Epstein2006} makes the updating rule, and not only
the prior, subjective, and \citet{Ortoleva2012} axiomatises departures triggered by
unexpected news. \citet{Cripps2021} treats the sequence of signals, and his
Symmetry and Divisibility axioms imply that two conditionally independent signals
give the same posterior in either sequence. Read with each cue as its likelihood matched to the prior
(Proposition~\ref{prop:IMM}), the composite of Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV} is a
sequence of Bayes updates satisfying all four of his axioms, and it depends on the
sequence because the second likelihood is matched to the intermediate belief, so
the two sequences process different experiments. While \citet{PettigrewWeisberg2025} restore sequence-invariance by pooling the
prior with each new input multiplicatively before the Jeffrey step, the current paper instead accepts the non-commutativity and characterises what remains of it in aggregate. The characterisation discussed in the paper offers some clarity to the observations noted by \citet{HogarthEinhorn1992}. While Bayes-factor updating predicts no sequence effect anywhere \citep{Wagner2002}, the belief-adjustment accounts of order effects \citep{HogarthEinhorn1992} focus on a single evaluative anchor. What the paper highlights is that such experimental analyses must also factor in the cross-attribute association -- thus requiring the elicitation of a full posterior table rather than a single rating produced by step-by-step or end-of-sequence response modes. 

The paper borrows the evaluator-with-binary-attribute frame from the
statistical-discrimination lineage \citep{Arrow1973,CoateLoury1993}. However, the object of interest for the current paper remains
kinematic rather than an equilibrium fixed point. Unlike \citet{CoateLoury1993}, for example, in which the stereotype is a self-confirming
steady state of best responses, the current paper merely characterises the channel-free baseline. In this baseline, the differences are keyed to encounter sequence rather than to
any group-marker, are individually first order, and yet remain invisible in the protected dependence statistics. It is worth highlighting that, instead of stereotypes as the selective recall of representative types
\citep{BCGS2016}, whose exaggerated associations arise across groups, the
stereotype in the current paper is a believed cross-attribute association held by
one evaluator and produced by coherent updating on impoverished input, not by a
distortion of memory. 


The audit perspective has a precedent in the economics of discrimination.
\citet{Heckman1998} showed that audit-pair estimates of discrimination rest on an
assumption about unobserved productivity that ``nothing guarantees'' (p.~109), so
that such an audit ``can find discrimination when in fact none exists; it can also
disguise discrimination when it is present'' (p.~102), and \citet{Bohren2019}
identify the source of discrimination by conditioning on the history of
evaluations, since in static data, as they note, different sources generate the
same patterns of observable behaviour. The obstruction here is of the same kind
and arises on the belief side of an audit. The first-order signal in a protected
statistic is zero in the population itself, so its silence is a failure of
identification and not of estimation, which no sample size repairs, and the
identifying variable is the reading sequence, which pooled data discard.

\section{Which statistics register the sequence}
\label{sec:individual}

This section asks, of each statistic of a single evaluator's belief, whether the
two reading sequences move it at first order in the prior covariance $c$ or only at
second order. Two primitive statistics carry the comparison with the sequence-free
benchmark $\PB$ of Section~\ref{sec:jeffrey}, the \emph{gap}, the distance of the
evaluator's belief from $\PB$ (Definition~\ref{def:indgap}), and the
\emph{score-gap} $s(P)=\langle\vv,P\rangle$, the same distance read through the
decision weights and set against a threshold $\tau$.
Section~\ref{sec:individual_primitives} shows that both are first order.
\par
The belief statistics and the decision statistics are read from the same belief.
The belief statistics are the \emph{marginal probability} of each attribute and the
\emph{believed cross-attribute association}; the decision statistics are the
surplus-weighted loss $L(c)$ and the decision \emph{flip}, the indicator that the
sequence changes the evaluator's decision. Whether a statistic registers the
sequence is settled here, for one evaluator; Section~\ref{sec:aggregation} shows
that averaging over evaluators who met the cues in either sequence leaves the
classification unchanged across an open set of priors and every interior
mixture.

Before these are detailed, it is worth stating why the respective statistics were considered. For belief statistics, a two cue setting means that belief is a joint law on $\{0,1\}^{2}$ and a point of $\Delta^{3}$ - which in turn suggests three degrees of freedom. Thus, the marginal probability for each attribute together with a single association parameter were considered for belief statistics. The two decision statistics simply measure \emph{how many} are affected by the arrival sequence (flip indicator) and \emph{how much} they are affected (loss weighted by surplus $|u|$ ). This is the standard incidence-versus-intensity pairing of the measurement literature, where a headcount (a count of those past a threshold) and a mean shortfall (average of their distances from it) are first two members of a single parametrised family of indices \citep{FosterGreerThorbecke1984}.

\subsection{Primitives for single evaluator}\label{sec:individual_primitives}
Starting with the \emph{gap}, we first state the rather trivial assertion that the difference between Jeffrey and Bayes is absent on a single cue or on two cues on independent attributes\footnote{Since we focus on soft, credence-delivering evidence in the current paper, the fact that two hard cues---delivered as a likelihood--- already commute, remains out of scope (see Assumption~\ref{as:soft})}.

\renewcommand{\theproposition}{IMM}%
\begin{proposition}[exact immunity]\label{prop:IMM}
On a single cue, the Jeffrey posterior equals the matched Bayesian posterior identically, for
every prior and every impression. With two cues but $c=0$ (independent attributes),
$\PJ_{AB}=\PJ_{BA}=\PB$ exactly. \anchor{Prop.~IMM}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The single cue on $A$ means the matched likelihood $\ell^A_i=q_i Z/P(A{=}i)$, with $Z$ the normaliser fixing the posterior $A$-marginal to $q$---which gives $\PB(i,j)=P(i,j)\ell^A_i/Z=q_i\,P(i,j)/P(A{=}i)=(\PJ_A P)(i,j)$. For $c=0$ the prior factorises, $P(i,j)=P(A{=}i)P(B{=}j)$. Each Jeffrey step and the Bayesian update then act on independent coordinates, so both sequences and the Bayesian posterior equal the product $q\otimes r$.
\end{proof}

In other words, a decision turning on \emph{one} cue is immune. Next we specify the sequence effect at the individual level---which is first order in our setup and serves as a building block for the rest of the propositions. 

\renewcommand{\theproposition}{DIV}%
\begin{proposition}[micro divergence]\label{prop:DIV}
Let $Z:=\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)>0$. At $c=0$ both reading sequences coincide with the
benchmark, $\PJ_{AB}=\PJ_{BA}=\PB=q\otimes r$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] \textup{(gap.)} For $c\neq0$,
\[
  \PJ_{AB}-\PB=c\,\kappa R_1+\bigO(c^{2}),
  \qquad
  R_1=q\otimes(1,-1),
  \qquad
  \kappa=\frac{(\alpha-q_0)\,r_0(1-r_0)}{Z},
\]
\[
  \PJ_{BA}-\PB=c\,\kappa' R_2+\bigO(c^{2}),
  \qquad
  R_2=(1,-1)\otimes r,
  \qquad
  \kappa'=\frac{(\beta-r_0)\,q_0(1-q_0)}{Z},
\]
with $\kappa=0\iff q_0=\alpha$ or $r_0\in\{0,1\}$, and symmetrically for $\kappa'$. Writing
$\PJ_\sigma-\PB=c\,D_\sigma+\bigO(c^{2})$ for the leading gap direction on route $\sigma$, so
that $D_{AB}=\kappa R_1$ and $D_{BA}=\kappa'R_2$, we have hence
$D_\sigma\not\equiv0$.
\item[(ii)] \textup{(Sequence effect.)} $\PJ_{AB}-\PJ_{BA}=c\,\Delta_{\mathrm{seq}}+\bigO(c^{2})$
with $\Delta_{\mathrm{seq}}=\kappa R_1-\kappa' R_2$. Since $R_1,R_2$ are linearly independent,
$\Delta_{\mathrm{seq}}\equiv0$ if and only if $\kappa=\kappa'=0$; hence
$\Delta_{\mathrm{seq}}\not\equiv0$.
\end{enumerate}
\anchor{Prop.~DIV}
\end{proposition}

The proof is provided in Appendix \ref{app:proofs}. The mechanism behind it is straightforward. Since prior covariance $c$ is the panel's belief about how strongly competence and trustworthiness go together, learning about one attribute is informative about the other (in the panel's own prior). A Jeffrey update on the $A$-cue rescales the joint distribution row by row, preserving the conditional distribution of $B$ given $A$. Thus when the attributes are independent, preserving that conditional leaves $B$'s marginal untouched, so the two sequences trivially commute and coincide with the benchmark exactly (Proposition~\ref{prop:IMM}). When $c\neq0$, rescaling rows changes $B$'s marginal and the update reaches the second attribute through the covariance, so the two sequences no longer agree at first order in $c$.  

The conditions for the first-order are not particularly stringent. Since $\kappa\propto(\alpha-q_0)$, the gap $P^J_{AB}-P^B$ vanishes when $A$-impression coincides with the prior $A$-marginal or when $B$-impression is degenerate ($\kappa\propto r_0(1-r_0)$ ). In terms of the panel example, therefore, the gap has a nonzero leading coefficient when the credential's impression genuinely surprises ($q_{0}\neq\alpha$) and the letter leaves a genuinely soft impression ($r_{0}(1-r_{0})>0$).

The sequence effect (Definition~\ref{def:seqeffect})---i.e. the difference $\PJ_{AB}-\PJ_{BA}$ between the two reading sequences---is also first order for the individual evaluator. The $A$-first gap $\PJ_{AB}-\PB$ has leading coefficient $\kappa\,R_1$ with $R_1=q\otimes(1,-1)$ (
which holds the $A$-marginal fixed and moves only the $B$-conditional) and the $B$-first gap $\PJ_{BA}-\PB$ has $\kappa'\,R_2$ with $R_2=(1,-1)\otimes r$ (holding the $B$-marginal fixed). The sequence effect, being their difference, has the form $\PJ_{AB}-\PJ_{BA}=c\,(\kappa R_1-\kappa' R_2)+\bigO(c^2)$. Because $R_1$ and $R_2$ preserve different marginals, the sequence effect is two-dimensional and does not collapse to either gap or reduce to a rescaling of one of them.

The second primitive, \emph{score-gap} of a panel (given a fixed weight $\vv$) is the first-order coefficient with which its own score $s(P)=\langle\vv,P\rangle$ departs from the benchmark's. This is because for a particular route $\sigma$ ($AB$ or $BA$ for two cues), we have
\[
  s(\PJ_\sigma)-s(\PB)=c\,\delta_\sigma+\bigO(c^{2}), \qquad \delta_\sigma:=\big\langle\vv,\ \partial_c(\PJ_\sigma-\PB)|_{c=0}\big\rangle.
\]

\subsection{Belief Statistics for single evaluator}

The two belief-statistics we consider are the \emph{believed association} between the two attributes and the believed \emph{marginal probability} of each attribute. The evaluator panel's \emph{believed cross-attribute association} is the association carried by the evaluator's own posterior, $\assoc(\PJ_\sigma)$, set against the benchmark's $\assoc(\PB)$. In the example, it measures only how strongly the panel takes competence and trustworthiness to go together once the file is read (not how attributes are observed in the candidate pool). Unlike the gap and the score gap, the \emph{believed cross-attribute association} does not inherit the first order of Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV}. As Lemma \ref{lem:ASC} shows, the immunity that is implied is exact rather than generic and it is complete for a single evaluator (before any
averaging takes place).

\renewcommand{\thelemma}{ASC}%
\begin{lemma}[individual-level association immunity]\label{lem:ASC}
For each reading sequence $\sigma\in\{AB,BA\}$ the believed association departs from the
benchmark's only at second order,
\[
  \assoc(\PJ_\sigma)-\assoc(\PB)=\bigO(c^{2}),
\]
identically in $(\alpha,\beta,q_{0},r_{0})$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since $\assoc$ (see Definition \ref{def:assoc}) is a polynomial in the cells and hence smooth, and
$\PJ_\sigma-\PB=c\,D_\sigma+\bigO(c^{2})$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV}), the chain rule gives
\[
  \assoc(\PJ_\sigma)-\assoc(\PB)
   =c\,\big\langle\nabla\assoc(q\otimes r),\,D_\sigma\big\rangle+\bigO(c^{2}),
\]
\[
  \nabla\assoc\big|_{q\otimes r}
   =\begin{pmatrix}q_{1}r_{1} & -q_{1}r_{0}\\ -q_{0}r_{1} & q_{0}r_{0}\end{pmatrix}.
\]
With $D_{AB}=\kappa R_1$ and $D_{BA}=\kappa'R_2$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV}), both inner
products vanish termwise:
\[
  \big\langle\nabla\assoc(q\otimes r),\,R_1\big\rangle
   = q_0q_1r_1 + q_0q_1r_0 - q_0q_1r_1 - q_0q_1r_0
   = 0,
\]
\[
  \big\langle\nabla\assoc(q\otimes r),\,R_2\big\rangle
   = q_1r_0r_1 - q_1r_0r_1 + q_0r_0r_1 - q_0r_0r_1
   = 0,
\]
so the first-order term is zero on each route.
\end{proof}

The second belief-statistic of interest is the panel's \emph{marginal probability} for an attribute i.e. the belief about that attribute alone, $\PJ_\sigma(A{=}1)$. This
is a linear read-out of the gap, the relevant row or column sum of $\PJ_\sigma-\PB$. In audit terms, the marginal is the levels question, asking how competent evaluators take a given candidate from the pool to be and what share of evaluators would rate that candidate above a given bar. As can be seen in Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV}, its rows sum to zero and its columns to $\pm\kappa$. On route $AB$ the competence marginal---the attribute read \emph{first}---therefore carries no first-order term at all, while the trustworthiness marginal---read \emph{last}---moves at
$c\kappa$. Similarly, on route $BA$ we have the opposite split, rows summing to $\pm\kappa'$ and columns to zero. The marginal probability of whichever attribute is read first is thus $\bigO(c^{2})$ exactly, and of whichever is read last is $\Theta(c)$ generically.

\subsection{Decision Statistics for single evaluator}

Panels differ not only in what they believe but also in the effects of how they act on their beliefs. The two decision statistics we're concerned with are the decision-\emph{flip} (which becomes sequence-affected-share in the aggregate) and sequence-affected \emph{loss}.

The panel's \emph{flip} indicator, $\mathds{1}\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}$, records whether the first-order
perturbation $c\delta_\sigma$ of the score carries the net surplus across the threshold. At the individual level, a flip requires two things together---the perturbation
$c\delta_\sigma$ must point toward the threshold, in the opposite direction from $u$ itself,
and it must be large enough to actually reach it. Formally, $\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)$
holds exactly when $\sgn(c\delta_\sigma)=-\sgn(u)$ and $|c\delta_\sigma|>|u|$---equivalently,
when $u$ lies strictly between $0$ and $-c\delta_\sigma$. A perturbation $c\delta_\sigma$ pushing $u$ further from the threshold never flips it, however large $c$ grows.

More formally, with $c^{*}:=|u/\delta_\sigma|$ for the crossing point, a panel with $u\neq0$ and $\delta_\sigma\neq0$ satisfies
\begin{align*}
  \mathds{1}\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}= 
  \begin{cases}
    1, & |c|>c^{*} \ \wedge\ \sgn(c\delta_\sigma)=-\sgn(u),\\[2pt]
    0, & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
  \qquad
  \equiv 0 \ \text{ on } (-c^{*},c^{*}),
\end{align*}

and therefore
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\mathrm{d}c^{k}}
  \mathds{1}\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}\bigg|_{c=0}=0
  \quad \forall\, k\ge 0,
  \qquad\text{while}\qquad
  \mathds{1}\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}\not\equiv 0.
\end{align*}

Every Taylor coefficient at the origin vanishes even though the quantity itself does not, so it is a step function of $c$. As we describe in the next subsection, what the
population acquires comes instead from the measure of the flip set -- a band of width $\bigO(c)$ at the threshold rather than an average of individual coefficients.
\[
  \Pr\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}
   =\int_{\{|u|<|c\delta_\sigma|,\ \sgn(u)=-\sgn(c\delta_\sigma)\}} f(u)\,\mathrm{d}u
   =f(0)\cdot\bigO(c),
\]

The second decision statistic is the panel's \textbf{foregone surplus}, the cost $|u|$ borne when the decision flips. It weights the flip indicator by the stake at issue, so that panels barely past the threshold contribute little and panels far from it contribute much. Writing $L_\sigma(c):=|u|\cdot\mathds{1}\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}$ for the foregone surplus, both decision quantities share the threshold structure established above for $u\neq0,\ \delta_\sigma\neq0$. As the following shows, each vanishes identically until $|c|$ reaches the crossing point, and every derivative at the origin vanishes with it. Neither of the two is analytic in $c$ and there is no expansion at the level of a single evaluator from which a leading coefficient could be read.
\[
  \mathds{1}\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}\equiv 0,
  \qquad
  L_\sigma(c)\equiv 0,
  \qquad\text{on } (-c^{*},c^{*}),
\]
\[
  \frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\mathrm{d}c^{k}}\,\mathds{1}\{\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)\}
  \bigg|_{c=0}
  =
  \frac{\mathrm{d}^{k}}{\mathrm{d}c^{k}}\,L_\sigma(c)\bigg|_{c=0}
  =0
  \quad \forall\, k\ge 0,
\]

\section{Averaging Effects on statistics}
\label{sec:aggregation}

The previous section classified statistics for one evaluator. This section shows
that the classification survives averaging over a population in which a fraction
$\lambda\in[0,1]$ of evaluators meet the $A$-cue first (the credential in the
example) and the rest the $B$-cue first (the letter), so that the mean belief under
Jeffrey conditioning is $\Pbar_\lambda=\lambda\,\PJ_{AB}+(1-\lambda)\,\PJ_{BA}$. The population mean is a linear pool of the two posteriors, and the choice of
pooling rule is immaterial at first order, since the two posteriors coincide at
independence and a geometric pool of them differs from the linear one only at
second order.
Across an open set of priors, what makes a statistic first order or second order is
the statistic itself and not the mixture $\lambda$, since the mean belief lies in
the plane through $\PB$ spanned by the leading directions of the two sequences,
and a statistic's order is fixed by its differential on that plane
(Propositions~\ref{prop:ORD} and~\ref{prop:PRO}).

Two definitions are worth pinning down before we move further. First, we define a \emph{distortion} of an arbitrary smooth scalar statistic $F$ as the aggregate departure from the benchmark, $F(\Pbar)-F(\PB)$. This is the aggregate counterpart of the individual \emph{gap} and is always a population-average object by definition. Second, we define the notion of a \emph{protection}, which classifies that distortion by its order in $c$.

\begin{definition}[protection]\label{def:protection}
$F$ is \emph{protected} if its distortion is second order in the prior covariance,
$F(\Pbar)-F(\PB)=\bigO(c^{2})$, and \emph{unprotected} if that distortion is first order, $\bigO(c)$.
\end{definition}

The distortion compares the population average with the benchmark. The same
classification can be reached without either ingredient, by reading the sequence
effect of Definition~\ref{def:seqeffect} through the statistic itself. For a smooth
statistic $F$ the sequence effect read through it is $F(\PJ_{AB})-F(\PJ_{BA})$, and
it involves neither the benchmark $\PB$ nor the mixing weight $\lambda$.
\par
\renewcommand{\theproposition}{ORD}%
\begin{proposition}[between-sequence contrast]\label{prop:ORD}
For any weight $v$,
\[
\langle v,\;\PJ_{AB}-\PJ_{BA}\rangle
   \;=\; c\,\bigl(\kappa\,\langle v,R_1\rangle-\kappa'\,\langle v,R_2\rangle\bigr)
   \;+\;\bigO(c^{2}),
\]
with $\kappa$, $\kappa'$, $R_1$, $R_2$ as in Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV}. The
sequence effect on the $A$-marginal is $c\,\kappa'+\bigO(c^{2})$ and on the
$B$-marginal $-c\,\kappa+\bigO(c^{2})$; each is $\Theta(c)$ generically. The
sequence effect on the believed association is $\bigO(c^{2})$ exactly. Across an
open set of priors, a smooth statistic has a second-order sequence effect if and
only if it is protected (Definition~\ref{def:protection}); both conditions hold
exactly when the differential of the statistic at independence annihilates
$\mathrm{span}\{R_1,R_2\}$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:PRO} below). Nothing in the
display involves $\PB$ or $\lambda$. The classification is decided by the two
reading sequences, and every mixture $\Pbar_\lambda$ inherits it.
\end{proposition}
\par
\begin{proof}
By Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV}(ii), $\PJ_{AB}-\PJ_{BA}=c(\kappa R_1-\kappa'R_2)+\bigO(c^{2})$;
applying $\langle v,\cdot\rangle$ gives the display. With $R_1=q\otimes(1,-1)$
and $R_2=(1,-1)\otimes r$,
\[
  \langle\mathbf 1_{A=1},R_1\rangle=0,\quad
  \langle\mathbf 1_{A=1},R_2\rangle=-1,\qquad
  \langle\mathbf 1_{B=1},R_1\rangle=-1,\quad
  \langle\mathbf 1_{B=1},R_2\rangle=0,
\]
giving $c\,\kappa'$ and $-c\,\kappa$. For the association,
$\langle\nabla\assoc(q\otimes r),R_1\rangle=\langle\nabla\assoc(q\otimes r),R_2\rangle=0$
(proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:PRO}), so its sequence effect is
$\bigO(c^{2})$. For the equivalence, with
$M_\lambda=\lambda\kappa R_1+(1-\lambda)\kappa'R_2$ from
Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV}(i) and $\nabla F:=\nabla F(q\otimes r)$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \langle\nabla F,\Delta_{\mathrm{seq}}\rangle
    &=\kappa\,\langle\nabla F,R_1\rangle-\kappa'\,\langle\nabla F,R_2\rangle,\\
  \langle\nabla F,M_\lambda\rangle
    &=\lambda\kappa\,\langle\nabla F,R_1\rangle+(1-\lambda)\kappa'\,\langle\nabla F,R_2\rangle;
\end{aligned}
\]
across an open set of priors $\kappa$ and $\kappa'$ vary independently, so
either vanishes identically exactly when
$\langle\nabla F,R_1\rangle=\langle\nabla F,R_2\rangle=0$.
\end{proof}

A particularly surprising finding is that for a smooth statistic of the belief,
the answer is settled before any averaging over the aggregate takes place
(Proposition~\ref{prop:ORD}). The aggregation confines the average posterior to a two-dimensional plane, spanned by the leading directions $R_1$ and $R_2$ of the two reading sequences, so whether a statistic is protected turns only on whether its differential at independence annihilates that plane. The attribute-locality assumption and Lemma~\ref{lem:ASC} ensure that the annihilator is exactly $\mathrm{span}\{d\,\assoc\}$. As we summarise in Table~\ref{tab:order-summary}, the average believed cross-attribute association
(Proposition~\ref{prop:DEC}) and the surplus-weighted loss $L(c)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:LOS}) are
second order, whereas the aggregate gap, score gaps on unprotected weights, the marginal
probabilities, and the sequence-affected share remain first order.

The obvious consequence of this property is that detectability is a property of the instrument, not of the phenomenon. To an observer of finite measurement precision $\varepsilon$ , a small $c$ allows $\varepsilon$ to fall so that $c^{2}\ll\varepsilon\ll c$. An observer resolves a departure when the departure's size exceeds their precision $\varepsilon$ and cannot resolve it when the departure sits below $\varepsilon$. Thus, the observer may resolve a first-order departure ($\varepsilon \ll c$) but cannot resolve the second-order one ($ c^2 \ll \varepsilon$). In the panel example, two panels reading the same file in opposite sequences differ in what they believe about the candidate's trustworthiness, and near the threshold in whom they hire. A reviewer who computes the panels' average believed association between competence and trustworthiness finds it within $\bigO(c^{2})$ of the benchmark and sees nothing (Lemma~\ref{lem:ASC}, Proposition~\ref{prop:DEC}) -- whereas a reviewer computing the share of files whose decision the reading sequence changes finds a departure at $\bigO(c)$ instead. We detail the findings in the rest of the current section.

\subsection{Aggregate Primitive Statistics}
That both gap and score-gap remain $\Theta(c)$ in the population aggregate is somewhat trivial. This is because the aggregate simply mixes the two routes' own shifts together in proportion to 
$\lambda$ and generically retains the sequence effects that were applicable at the individual level.

\subsubsection{Gap distortion}
\label{sec:distortion}

The proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:PRO} gives $\Pbar_\lambda-\PB=c\,M_\lambda+\bigO(c^{2})$ directly, and because $R_1$ and $R_2$ are linearly independent, $M_\lambda$ vanishes at interior $\lambda$ only where the coefficients of both reading sequences vanish. The aggregate gap is therefore first order on the same generic set as the gap of Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV}. In other words, averaging over reading sequences reweights the leading coefficient without removing it. \anchor{Prop.~PRO}

\subsubsection{Score gap}
\label{sec:scoregap}

The average \emph{score gap} is also a straightforward measure of the weighted score of the belief and its first-order in aggregate follows from Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV}.

\renewcommand{\thelemma}{SCR}%
\begin{lemma}[first-order score gap]\label{lem:SCR}
$s(\PJ_\sigma)-s(\PB)=c\,\delta_\sigma+\bigO(c^{2})$, with $\delta_\sigma$ as defined in
Section~\ref{sec:individual}; generically
$\delta_{AB}\neq\delta_{BA}$, and $\delta_\sigma=0$ if $\vv$ is constant. \anchor{Lem.~SCR}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Apply the linear functional $s=\langle\vv,\cdot\rangle$ to
$\PJ_\sigma-\PB=cD_\sigma+\bigO(c^2)$ from Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV}. Constancy of $\vv$ makes
$\delta_\sigma$ telescope to $0$ since both posteriors are normalised.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Aggregate Belief Statistics}
The aggregation of belief statistics also shows that aggregation doesn't really change the sequence-effects from the individual case. As Lemma~\ref{lem:ASC} shows, a particular route doesn't shift association in first-order. Mixing them does not change that. On the other hand, the case for marginal probability is not symmetrical by definition. Whichever attribute a route reads last has its marginal pinned exactly to the impression and so departs from the benchmark at first order, whereas the route reading that attribute first moves with the benchmark and its shift is zero to first order. Mixing the two does not zero out the first-order effect. It's not the averaging that changes the sequence, but the nature of statistic itself determines whether arrival order matters or not.

\subsubsection{Aggregate cross-attribute association}
\label{sec:association}

Recall that the sense of stereotype in the paper depends on the cross-attribute association. With $\assoc(Q)=Q_{00}Q_{11}-Q_{01}Q_{10}$ as the association of a joint law $Q$, the \emph{stereotype} is the believed association absent from---or in excess of---the benchmark, a
nonzero $\assoc(\Pbar)-\assoc(\PB)$. By definition, stereotype is absent when the belief matches the Bayesian benchmark.

We now explain how the average believed cross-attribute association is second order because of a separable reweighting
(Lemma~\ref{lem:SEP})---rather than the Jeffrey conditioning or the presence of two cues. More particularly, every updating route in the model composes to a separable
reweighting---which can only rescale an association, never shift it---so the average posterior
moves along a single fixed direction. Since separability holds at every $N$ and for any rule whose steps read one attribute at a time, the protection is structural rather than a cancellation of terms and shows up as a property of attribute-locality (holding for any such rule, not Jeffrey
conditioning in particular). The lemma is stated and proved for arbitrary $N$.

\renewcommand{\thelemma}{SEP}%
\begin{lemma}[separability of attribute-local composites]\label{lem:SEP}
For any number $N$ of binary attributes and a joint prior $P$ over $\{0,1\}^{N}$, any finite
sequence of attribute-local updates produces a \emph{separable} reweighting
\[
  P'(x)=P(x)\prod_{a=1}^{N}g_{a}(x_{a})
\]
for some single-attribute functions $g_{a}$. Equivalently, $\log(P'/P)$ contains no interaction
terms at any level. \anchor{Lem.~SEP}
\end{lemma}

The proof is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}. It proceeds by induction on the update
sequence: each attribute-local step folds into that attribute's own multiplicative factor
without ever coupling two coordinates, so the product form survives any finite sequence of such
steps. 

Proposition~\ref{prop:DEC} now shows how the second-order behaviour of the distortion of the average believed association depends on the separability lemma. More specifically, Lemma~\ref{lem:SEP} forces every updating route to act
on the association by the pure multiplier $\assoc(Q')=(g_0g_1h_0h_1/Z^{2})\,\assoc(Q)$, so all of them share the same leading factor at independence and the $\bigO(c)$ terms in $\assoc(\Pbar)-\assoc(\PB)$ cancel identically. On the other hand, a non-separable updating route obeys no such
identity and can shift $\assoc$ additively rather than merely rescale it, leaving a first-order distortion.

\renewcommand{\theproposition}{DEC}%
\begin{proposition}[belief-level decoupling]\label{prop:DEC}
The distortion of the average believed association is second order---its first-order coefficient is
identically zero:
\[
  \assoc(\Pbar)-\assoc(\PB)=\bigO(c^{2}).
\]
\anchor{Prop.~DEC}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Association---the $\bigO(c)$ terms coincide by separable covariance.} For any rank-one
(separable) reweighting $Q'_{ij}=g_i h_j Q_{ij}/Z$ of a joint law $Q$, with $Z=\sum_{ij}g_i h_j Q_{ij}$,
\[
  \assoc(Q')=\frac{g_0 g_1 h_0 h_1}{Z^{2}}\,\assoc(Q),
\]
since the cross terms in $Q'_{00}Q'_{11}-Q'_{01}Q'_{10}$ carry the identical factor
$g_0g_1h_0h_1$. Explicitly,
\begin{align*}
  Q'_{00}Q'_{11}-Q'_{01}Q'_{10}
  &=\frac{(g_0h_0Q_{00})(g_1h_1Q_{11})-(g_0h_1Q_{01})(g_1h_0Q_{10})}{Z^{2}}\\
  &=\frac{g_0g_1h_0h_1}{Z^{2}}\bigl(Q_{00}Q_{11}-Q_{01}Q_{10}\bigr).
\end{align*}
Applying this with $Q=P$: by Lemma~\ref{lem:SEP}, every Jeffrey route composes to exactly such a
separable reweighting (at $N=2$, a rank-one form $g_ih_j$), and the Bayes update is one
directly ($g_i=q_i/\alpha_i,\ h_j=r_j/\beta_j$, writing $\alpha_0=\alpha,\ \alpha_1=1-\alpha$ and
similarly for $\beta$). Every route therefore
maps the prior association $\assoc(P)=c$ to a \emph{positive multiple} of $c$; none can shift it
additively. Evaluating the multiplier at $c=0$---where all intermediate marginals are
$(\alpha,1-\alpha)$ and $(\beta,1-\beta)$ regardless of route---gives the same leading factor
\[
  \assoc(\PJ_{AB})=\assoc(\PJ_{BA})=\assoc(\PB)=K\,c+\bigO(c^2),
  \qquad K=\frac{q_0 q_1\, r_0 r_1}{\alpha(1-\alpha)\beta(1-\beta)} .
\]
Explicitly, at $c=0$ one has $g_i=q_i/\alpha_i$, $h_j=r_j/\beta_j$ and $Z=1$, so the
multiplier equals $\bigl(q_0q_1/\alpha(1-\alpha)\bigr)\bigl(r_0r_1/\beta(1-\beta)\bigr)=K$.
Hence $\assoc(\Pbar)=\lambda\,\assoc(\PJ_{AB})+(1-\lambda)\,\assoc(\PJ_{BA})+\bigO(c^2)
=Kc+\bigO(c^2)$ (the mixture identity holds to leading order; $\assoc$ is quadratic, and the
cross terms are $\bigO(c^2)$) and
$\assoc(\Pbar)-\assoc(\PB)=\bigO(c^2)$, the $\bigO(c)$ coefficients cancelling since Jeffrey and Bayes are both separable, so both can only rescale the prior
association by the same leading factor.

\end{proof}

The multiplier identity in the proof above yields an exact statement for the odds-ratio forms of association. In the ratio $Q'_{00}Q'_{11}/(Q'_{01}Q'_{10})$ the factor
$g_0g_1h_0h_1/Z^{2}$ cancels, so every separable reweighting leaves the odds ratio unchanged wherever the entries are positive. It follows that $\PJ_{AB}$, $\PJ_{BA}$ and $\PB$ all carry the prior's odds ratio
identically in $c$. For believed association in odds-ratio form the departure from the benchmark is exactly zero rather than $\bigO(c^{2})$. Order effects live in the reset marginals, but not in the preserved
association.

The proposition's implications for the example vignette are also quite revealing. In a population of panels evaluating a common pool---where each receives the same two impressions, one bearing on competence and one on trustworthiness---each panel's posterior differs from the benchmark at $\bigO(c)$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV}). Yet the average believed association---whether the evaluators
collectively take the two attributes to go together more or less than the benchmark
warrants---carries no first-order trace of the sequence (Proposition~\ref{prop:DEC}). In other words, reading sequence cannot, to first order, build or erode the perceived relationship between the attributes,
because both updates reweight separably, and a separable reweighting only rescales the
association already present. Sequence effects therefore create no excess association or a stereotype in the above sense.

\subsubsection{Aggregate Marginal-probability}
\label{sec:marginal}

The current section shows that the reading sequence does move the marginal probability in the aggregate. Proposition \ref{prop:DRF} formalises this.

\renewcommand{\theproposition}{DRF}%
\begin{proposition}[marginal-probability drift]\label{prop:DRF}
The aggregate \emph{marginal-probability} distortion is first order, with
\[
  \big[\Pbar(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)\big]
   = c\cdot(1-\lambda)\,\frac{q_0(1-q_0)\,(r_0-\beta)}{\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)}+\bigO(c^2),
\]
so for $\lambda<1$ its sign equals $\sgn(r_0-\beta)$, sign-indefinite across
cue--marginal-probability configurations. At $\lambda=1$ this drift vanishes at first order and
the $B$-marginal drifts with coefficient
$\lambda\,r_0(1-r_0)(q_0-\alpha)/\big(\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)\big)$ with sign
$\sgn(q_0-\alpha)$. For every $\lambda\in[0,1]$, therefore, at least one marginal drifts at first order.
\anchor{Prop.~DRF}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
In the $BA$ sequence, the $A$-cue is processed last, and a Jeffrey step pins its own marginal ($\PJ_{BA}(A{=}1)=q_{1}$ identically
in $c$). Similarly, in the $AB$ sequence the later $B$-step pulls the $A$-marginal off the delivered value
through the covariance so that we have
\[
  \PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)=q_{1}-c\,\frac{q_{0}(1-q_{0})(r_{0}-\beta)}{\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)}
  +\bigO(c^{2}),
\]

Writing $K:=\dfrac{q_0(1-q_0)(r_0-\beta)}{\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)}$, the two routes give
\[
  \PJ_{BA}(A{=}1)=q_1,
  \qquad
  \PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)=q_1-cK+\bigO(c^2),
  \qquad
  \PB(A{=}1)=q_1-cK+\bigO(c^2),
\]
Hence,
\[
  \PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)=\bigO(c^2),
  \qquad
  \PJ_{BA}(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)=cK+\bigO(c^2).
\]
Averaging over routes,
\begin{align*}
  \Pbar(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)
   &=\lambda\bigl[\PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)\bigr]+(1-\lambda)\bigl[\PJ_{BA}(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)\bigr]\\
   &=\lambda\cdot\bigO(c^2)+(1-\lambda)\bigl(cK+\bigO(c^2)\bigr)
    =c\,(1-\lambda)K+\bigO(c^2).
\end{align*}
Since $q_0(1-q_0)>0$ and $\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)>0$ on the open cube, $\sgn K=\sgn(r_0-\beta)$, so
\[
  \lambda<1
  \ \Longrightarrow\
  \sgn\bigl[\Pbar(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)\bigr]=\sgn(r_0-\beta),
  \]
  When $\lambda = 1$, we have
 \[
  \lambda=1
  \ \Longrightarrow\
  \Pbar(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)=\bigO(c^2).
\]

\end{proof}

It's worth highlighting a key result that follows from the sequence affecting marginal probability at first order. The pool's average perceived competence drifts at $\bigO(c)$. In terms of the terminology we've used, the marginal probability lies outside the protected class. As a result, what an outside observer surveying the panels' joint beliefs and comparing them against the benchmark would find is that along the association axis, the average believed relationship between competence and trustworthiness would sit at essentially its benchmark value (order $c^{2}$). On the other hand, along the marginal-probability axis the average perceived marginal probability of either attribute would depart from the benchmark already at order $c$. It is only in the marginal probabilities that the observer could read off any trace of the sequence at all. The footprint of sequencing thus resides in marginal probabilities, not believed association. 

\subsection{Aggregate Decision Statistics}

Both decision-statistics look at evaluators inside the threshold band who may flip their decision when the sequence changes their score. However, \textbf{Share} depends only on the band's mass, $f(0)\cdot\bigO(c)$ -- thus leaving it first order. On the other hand, \textbf{Loss} weights each evaluator in that band by their stake $|u|$, and inside the band $|u|$ is itself bounded by $\bigO(c)$ from the threshold. A band of width $\bigO(c)$ weighted by a quantity of size $\bigO(c)$ leaves it second-order.

\subsubsection{Surplus-weighted loss}
\label{sec:welfare}

We now show that a fixed evaluator with net surplus $u\neq0$ is  unaffected by the reading sequence until $|c\delta_\sigma|$ reaches $|u|$, so their foregone surplus
is identically zero for small $c$ rather than second order in it. Whatever sequence the population
aggregate has is therefore not inherited from any member. Taking the score gap of
Lemma~\ref{lem:SCR} as given, the higher-order behaviour of the expected surplus loss $L(c)$ is
now stated as a theorem.

\renewcommand{\thetheorem}{LOS}%
\begin{theorem}[surplus-weighted loss]\label{thm:LOS}
Let $u=s(\PB)-\tau$ have, across the population and the sequence distribution, a joint density
whose $u$-marginal is integrable (Assumption~\ref{as:surplus}). Then the expected surplus loss $L(c)$ of the Jeffrey
population relative to the benchmark is $o(c)$---strictly higher order than the individual
divergence. If the $u$-marginal is bounded and continuous at $u=0$ with value
$f(0)>0$, the loss is exactly second order, with constant named:
\[
  L(c)=\frac{f(0)}{2}\,\E\!\big[\delta_\sigma^{2}\mid u=0\big]\,c^{2}+\bigO(c^{3}).
\]
\anchor{Thm.~LOS}
\end{theorem}

The proof -- provided in Appendix \ref{app:proofs} -- can be viewed as a band picture shown in Figure~\ref{fig:band}. The Jeffrey and benchmark
actions differ only when the perturbation $c\delta_\sigma$ flips the sign of the surplus $u$, so
the flip set is confined to a band of width $\bigO(c)$ at the threshold. On that band the cost
$|u|$ is itself $\bigO(c)$. An $\bigO(c)$ mass of individuals, each costing $\bigO(c)$, yields an
$\bigO(c^{2})$ loss. More particularly, a candidate is mis-decided only when the first-order perturbation flips the sign of the surplus, $\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)$---which in turn requires
$|u|\le|c\delta_\sigma|=\bigO(c)$. The affected evaluators are exactly those lying within $\bigO(c)$ of the threshold---the thin strip around
$u=0$ that we call the \emph{band}. Its mass, the fraction of the population sitting in it, is
$\Pr\{|u|\le|c\delta_\sigma|\}=f(0)\cdot\bigO(c)$, since a strip of width $\bigO(c)$ carries that
much probability when $f$ is continuous at $u=0$. The foregone surplus $|u|$ on the band is itself $\bigO(c)$ and the loss must obey
\[
  L(c)\;\le\;\underbrace{\bigO(c)}_{\text{cost }|u|}\cdot\underbrace{\bigO(c)}_{\text{band mass}}
       \;=\;\bigO(c^{2}).
\]

\subsubsection{Sequence-affected share---incidence versus intensity}
\label{sec:criterion}

The mechanism for the sequence-affected share is largely the same as that for surplus-weighted loss. Both
integrate over the same set---the band of evaluators whose decisions the perturbation flips, of
width $\bigO(c)$ at the threshold---and differ only in what they weight each member by. The surplus criterion weights by the stake $|u|$, which the band itself holds to $\bigO(c)$ whereas the $0$--$1$ criterion weights by $1$ and returns the band's mass alone.

Membership in that band is forced by a comparison of two lengths. An evaluator's distance from
the threshold is $|u|$, and the reading sequence moves their score by $c\delta_\sigma$; a flip
requires the second to be at least the first, so $|u|>|c\delta_\sigma|$ leaves the action
unchanged whichever sequence the cues may be in. As $\delta_\sigma$ is bounded across the population,
only evaluators within $\bigO(c)$ of the threshold can be affected at all. The condition is
necessary but not sufficient---the perturbation must also point towards the threshold rather
than away from it---so the affected form a subset of the band, and it is the band's mass,
$f(0)\cdot\bigO(c)$, that sets the first-order rate. The following proposition formalises this.

\renewcommand{\theproposition}{SHR}%
\begin{proposition}[sequence-affected share]\label{prop:SHR}
Suppose the $u$-marginal is bounded and continuous at $u=0$ with $f(0)>0$ (the same density
hypothesis as Theorem~\ref{thm:LOS}). Then under the $0$--$1$ criterion---weight $1$ for each
individual whose Jeffrey and benchmark actions differ, $0$ for everyone else, so that the
aggregate is the \emph{incidence} of sequence-affected individuals, their share of the
population---the aggregate is genuinely $\bigO(c)$, not merely $o(1)$, since the band mass
itself scales as $f(0)\cdot\bigO(c)$. \anchor{Prop.~SHR}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Both criteria integrate over the same set, differing only in the weight attached to a member.

\emph{Step 1: the affected set.} By Lemma~\ref{lem:SCR} the Jeffrey score perturbs the benchmark
score by $c\delta_\sigma+\bigO(c^{2})$, so
\[
  \text{affected}
  \iff
  \sgn(u)\neq\sgn\bigl(u+c\delta_\sigma+\bigO(c^{2})\bigr)
  \ \Longrightarrow\
  u\in S_c:=\bigl\{\,|u|\le|c\,\delta_\sigma|+\bigO(c^{2})\,\bigr\},
\]
a band of width $\bigO(c)$ about the threshold $u=0$, where $\sgn(u)$ turns over.

\emph{Step 2: the band mass.} With $f$ bounded, continuous at $0$, and $f(0)>0$,
\[
  \mu(S_c)=\int_{|u|\le|c\delta_\sigma|}\!\! f(u)\,du=f(0)\cdot\bigO(c),
\]
first order rather than merely bounded by it, since $f(0)>0$ and $\delta_\sigma\neq0$
generically (Lemma~\ref{lem:SCR}).

\emph{Step 3: the two criteria.} On $S_c$ the stake obeys $|u|\le|c\delta_\sigma|=\bigO(c)$,
whence
\[
  \underbrace{\textstyle\int_{S_c} 1\,d\mu}_{\text{incidence}}=\mu(S_c)=\bigO(c),
  \qquad
  \underbrace{\textstyle\int_{S_c} |u|\,d\mu}_{\text{surplus}}
   \le \bigO(c)\cdot\mu(S_c)=\bigO(c^{2})
  \quad (\text{Theorem~\ref{thm:LOS}}).
\]
The extra factor of $c$ is the weight $|u|$, which the band forces to first order.
\end{proof}

What follows from the above proposition has strong implications for what an audit can measure. A first-order \emph{number} of individuals are genuinely affected, each near-marginal. The second-order quantities are the surplus-weighted loss and the believed association. Therefore, the audit result cannot ever be phrased as ``few are affected,'' because the incidence is first order \anchor{Prop.~SHR}. Whatever significance attaches to those individuals---their decisions turned on the sequence in which two impressions happened to arrive---attaches to this first-order count, not to the second-order aggregates. The two questions ``how many are affected?'' and ``what do the aggregate dependence and surplus statistics show?'' thus decouple by one order in $c$. Propositions~\ref{prop:SHR} and~\ref{prop:PRO} show that the criterion, not the phenomenon, selects which answer an aggregate instrument returns.

\subsection{The protected class of aggregate statistics}
\label{sec:protected}

We now describe the necessary and sufficient conditions under which an arbitrary smooth statistic becomes second order. We define a differential condition for a smooth statistic of the average belief---a condition whose fulfilment leaves no first-order trace of the sequence effects. More specifically, this differential condition on $F$ is $\nabla F(q\otimes r)\in\mathrm{span}\{J,\ \nabla\assoc(q\otimes r)\}$ (where $J$ is
the all-ones matrix and $R_1=q\otimes(1,-1)$, $R_2=(1,-1)\otimes r$ are the two route
directions).

Recall that \emph{protection} means that the aggregate's first-order coefficient vanishes identically, so the statistic sits within $\bigO(c^{2})$ of its benchmark value (even
though every member of the population is $\bigO(c)$ away). What we now prove is that a smooth statistic is \emph{protected} exactly when its differential at the independent posterior $q\otimes r$ points along $d\,\assoc$ (the differential of the association) so that to first order the statistic moves only as the association moves. Every other smooth statistic departs at first order generically (i.e. on all but a lower-dimensional set of priors). The following proposition formalises this.

\renewcommand{\theproposition}{PRO}%
\begin{proposition}[uniqueness of the protected statistic]\label{prop:PRO}
For $(q_0,r_0)\in(0,1)^2$, $F$ as $C^1$ in a neighbourhood of $q\otimes r$ in the
probability simplex, and with the mean belief $\Pbar_\lambda$ of
Section~\ref{sec:aggregation}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] \textup{(Protection.)} If the differential of $F$ at $q\otimes r$, restricted to the
tangent space of the simplex, is a scalar multiple of $d\,\assoc$, then
$F(\Pbar_\lambda)-F(\PB)=\bigO(c^2)$ for every prior $(\alpha,\beta)$ and every $\lambda$.
\item[(ii)] \textup{(Uniqueness.)} Conversely, fix $\lambda\in(0,1)$. If
$F(\Pbar_\lambda)-F(\PB)=o(c)$ for all $(\alpha,\beta)$ in some open set, then $dF$ at
$q\otimes r$, restricted to the simplex, is a multiple of $d\,\assoc$.
\end{enumerate}
\anchor{Prop.~PRO}
\end{proposition}

The proof is provided in Appendix \ref{app:proofs}. What drives it is that each sequence's leading gap is a scalar
multiple of a single rank-one direction---$\kappa\,q\otimes(1,-1)$ for one sequence and, exchanging
the roles of the cues, $\kappa'\,(1,-1)\otimes r$ with $\kappa'=(\beta-r_0)\,q_0(1-q_0)/Z$ for
the other (Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV} and its mirror)---so the annihilator of the two directions
is two-dimensional and spanned by the constants and $\nabla\assoc$, and the converse follows
from the nonconstancy of $\kappa/\kappa'$ on any open set of priors.

\textbf{The smooth statistics protected to second order are exactly} the \emph{local dependence
measures}, those that, to first order at independence, depend on the posterior only through its
association. This includes the association itself (Proposition~\ref{prop:DEC} is the case
$F=\assoc$), the odds ratio, its logarithm, and Yule's $Q$; among expectations
$\langle v,P\rangle$ it admits exactly those whose $v$ lies in the two-dimensional span of the
constants and $\nabla\assoc$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:SCR}). No statistic outside the class is second order on an open
set of priors.

The quantification over $\lambda$ in part~(i) is what makes the class a classification of
statistics rather than of populations: the aggregate first-order coefficient is
$\lambda\langle\nabla F,\kappa R_1\rangle+(1-\lambda)\langle\nabla F,\kappa' R_2\rangle$, and
requiring it to vanish at every interior $\lambda$ forces both inner products to vanish
separately rather than merely cancel against each other at some particular composition of the
population. Protection in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:protection} is thus annihilation of
the route directions, not cancellation between them.

In the panel example, the classification says which audit questions can register the sequencing, and
the boundary falls in an instructive place. Ask how likely the panels believe a
competent-seeming candidate to be trustworthy, $\Pbar(B{=}1\mid A{=}1)$, and the distortion is
first order, since this conditional probability carries a component along the marginal
probabilities and the marginal probabilities drift at $\bigO(c)$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:DRF}).
Ask how much \emph{likelier} they believe a competent-seeming candidate to be trustworthy than
an incompetent-seeming one, $\Pbar(B{=}1\mid A{=}1)-\Pbar(B{=}1\mid A{=}0)$, and the distortion
is second order, since the subtraction removes the component along the marginal probabilities
and leaves a differential at independence that is a positive multiple of $d\,\assoc$, placing
this difference in the protected class. Stereotypes do persist, but not as trivially as is often presumed.

\section{Scope and robustness}
\label{sec:scope}

\subsection{Scope}\label{sec:scope-assumptions}
\par
We now revisit how Assumptions~\ref{as:localc}--\ref{as:surplus} in
Section~\ref{sec:assumptions} define the scope of the conclusions drawn from the
model. First, a prior covariance outside the leading-order regime voids the separation (Assumption~\ref{as:localc}) that is key to much of the propositional claim in the paper. More particularly, Assumption~\ref{as:localc} states the results as $c\to0$, they compare $\bigO(c)$ to $\bigO(c^{2})$, a comparison that is informative only when the prior covariance $c$ is small relative to its feasibility bound, since $\bigO(c^{2})$ is then smaller than $\bigO(c)$. When $c$ is instead a sizable fraction of that bound, the two orders are no longer numerically distinguishable, and the local-in-$c$ results do not apply. Second, Assumption~\ref{as:soft} states that a single cue, taken alone, produces no phenomenon at all. As Proposition~\ref{prop:IMM} shows, the Jeffrey and benchmark posteriors coincide exactly on one cue and the question of sequence-dependence does not arise when Assumption~\ref{as:soft} is not met. Third, attribute-locality (Assumption~\ref{as:local}) makes every Jeffrey update a separable reweighting (Lemma~\ref{lem:SEP}) and is the engine of Proposition~\ref{prop:DEC}. A cue delivering an impression on a joint event---a credence on both attributes together rather than on a single attribute's partition, which Assumption~\ref{as:local} excludes---is out of scope. Such a cue reweights the joint law non-separably and could itself manufacture association from independence, so the immunity of Proposition~\ref{prop:IMM} would not apply. Fourth, a non-surplus criterion breaks the surplus decoupling (Assumption~\ref{as:surplus}), since the aggregate loss is first order under a fixed per-error cost (Proposition~\ref{prop:SHR}). Fifth, the population modelled throughout is homogeneous in one further respect: every evaluator shares the same prior $(\alpha,\beta,c)$, and only the encounter sequence---and, for the surplus-weighted loss, the stakes $u$---varies across the pool. A population whose members differ in the prior covariance $c$ itself is outside the scope of stated results.

A separate scope question concerns rival mechanisms rather than broken
assumptions. Sequence effects have an explanation that competes with full adoption.
In the belief-adjustment model of \citet{HogarthEinhorn1992}, when the first cue is
adopted in full and later cues only in part the first impression is protected, and
at zero adoption of later cues it is never moved, whereas when every cue is adopted
only in part from a prior anchor the same model predicts recency. That first
account and full adoption point in opposite directions, since the protected
impression is the first one there and the last one here. An observable separates
them. Under partial adoption of the second cue with weight $\omega$
(Proposition~\ref{prop:ADJ}), the marginal that ignores the prior association is
the last-read one at $\omega=1$, the first-read one at $\omega=0$, and neither in
between. A reading on which neither marginal ignores the association is consistent
with an interior weight and with the benchmark alike; the two are separated by the
sequence comparison, under which the benchmark shows no sequence effect at any $c$
and an interior weight shows one already at $c=0$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:ADJ}).
Since every rule here is a separable reweighting, all of them carry the prior's
odds ratio (Lemma~\ref{lem:SEP}), so the odds ratio separates none of them and the
two marginals are the only statistics that do. Three cautions bound the claim. The
classification covers this one-parameter family, not every conceivable mechanism.
\citet{Asch1946} is evidence that sequence moves marginals, six stimulus terms read
in two sequences moving the proportions on eighteen response traits, and not
evidence for either endpoint, since his own account is that early terms set a
direction for the reading of later ones, so that what a later cue delivers depends
on its position, a mechanism outside the family here, in which each cue delivers
the same credence in either position. And full adoption is a substantive
commitment rather than a consequence of the level reading. \citet{Hawthorne2004}
objects that it ``seems implausible that the most recent experience or
non-propositional state should completely dictate belief strengths for basis
sentences, with no regard for the import of previous experiences or states''. The
objection applies here, and his own two-basis example shows the overwriting
explicitly, so that the credential's implication for trustworthiness is erased
once the letter fixes that attribute. The adoption weight is his objection stated
as a parameter, and the reply offered here is not that the objection misses but
that the weight is recovered from three ratings of one marginal rather than
assumed.

\subsection{Partial adoption and factor inputs}\label{sec:robust}
\par
The results of Sections~\ref{sec:individual} and~\ref{sec:aggregation} rest on two
premises about a cue. It delivers a credence on its own attribute rather than a
factor, and the credence is adopted in full. Full adoption is the setting in which
an evaluator adopts a cue alike whichever position it arrives in, so that the
sequence can act only through the believed link between the attributes. A weight on
the later cue that depends on its position is a property of the evaluator rather than
of the evidence, and it is the subject of the belief-adjustment literature
\citep{HogarthEinhorn1992}. Whether a population adopts in full can be read from its ratings, since
Proposition~\ref{prop:ADJ} below recovers the adoption weight from three marginals
of one reading group, and Proposition~\ref{prop:LAD} says what to expect when it
does not. What partial adoption leaves in place is the odds ratio, which is the same
in both sequences at every weight, and a second-order class of statistics that no
longer contains the association. Table~\ref{tab:robust} collects the answers.
\par
\paragraph{The two channels.} Sequence dependence has two channels in this
setting, and the results of Sections~\ref{sec:individual} and~\ref{sec:aggregation}
concern one of them. One is the association channel. A cue on one attribute moves
the belief about the other through the prior association, so the second cue meets
a belief the first has already changed. The association channel's effect is first
order in $c$ and vanishes at independence, where each cue sets its own marginal and
touches nothing else (Proposition~\ref{prop:IMM}). The other is the position
channel. If the cue read second is adopted only in part, then which cue is weakened
depends on the sequence, and the two sequences differ even at $c=0$, by
$(1-\omega)(\alpha-q_0)$ on the competence marginal and $-(1-\omega)(\beta-r_0)$ on
the trustworthiness marginal. The position channel is not a feature of updating on delivered credences. A
Bayes-factor update that gives the second cue's factor the weight $\omega$ is
sequence-dependent in the same way, the marginals differing at order zero and the
believed association at first order while the odds ratio is unchanged, since
commutation requires the same factor in either position \citep{Wagner2002}. Under full adoption the
position channel is absent and the association channel is the only one, which is
the setting of the results. Under partial adoption the position channel is present
at $c=0$ and moves every marginal at order zero, while the believed association
still does not differ between sequences at $c=0$ and differs at first order
(Proposition~\ref{prop:LAD}). The classification by order in $c$ shifts by one order,
each statistic keeping its place relative to the others, and the odds ratio is the
same in both sequences at every weight. The mean belief of a population mixing the two sequences
acquires a cross-product association of
$-\lambda(1-\lambda)(1-\omega)^2(\alpha-q_0)(\beta-r_0)$ that no member holds, and the
separation between the share of decisions changed and the loss is lost. What the
paper says about the position channel is confined to Propositions~\ref{prop:ADJ}
and~\ref{prop:LAD}, the first recovering its strength from the ratings and the
second stating what it does to the order in $c$ at which each statistic registers
the sequence. The
position channel is a temporal bias of the observer, present whether or not the
attributes are believed related, while the association channel runs through what
one cue implies about the other and exists only when they are.

\paragraph{Partial adoption.} The two sequences of Sections~\ref{sec:individual} and~\ref{sec:aggregation} adopt
each delivered credence in full. Suppose instead
that the second cue is adopted only in part, so that after the first Jeffrey step
the response to the second cue is a Jeffrey step on its partition to the target
$(1-\omega)\,m+\omega\,r_1$, where $m$ is the marginal the first step left and
$0\le\omega\le1$ is the adoption weight of Section~\ref{sec:setup}. The rule is the
averaging form of the belief-adjustment model,
$S_k=(1-w_k)S_{k-1}+w_k s(x_k)$ \citep[Eq.~4]{HogarthEinhorn1992}, applied to the
second cue only, which is their end-of-sequence form for two items with a constant
weight, and embedded in the joint law by a Jeffrey step. Write $P^{\omega}_{AB}$
and $P^{\omega}_{BA}$ for the two sequences under this rule; $\omega=1$ recovers
$\PJ_{AB}$ and $\PJ_{BA}$, and $\omega=0$ ignores the second cue. Let $P^{A}$ and
$P^{B}$ denote the belief after the $A$-cue alone and after the $B$-cue alone.
\par
\renewcommand{\theproposition}{ADJ}%
\begin{proposition}[adoption weight]\label{prop:ADJ}
For every $c$ and every $\omega$, in sequence $AB$,
\begin{align*}
P^{\omega}_{AB}(B{=}1)-r_1 &=(1-\omega)\bigl[P^{A}(B{=}1)-r_1\bigr],\\
P^{\omega}_{AB}(A{=}1)-q_1 &=\omega\,\bigl[\PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)-q_1\bigr],\\
P^{\omega}_{AB}(A{=}1)-P^{\omega}_{BA}(A{=}1)
  &=\omega\,\bigl[\PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)-q_1\bigr]+(1-\omega)\bigl[q_1-P^{B}(A{=}1)\bigr].
\end{align*}
Hence the last-read marginal equals its delivered credence identically in $c$
exactly when $\omega=1$, the first-read marginal does so exactly when
$\omega=0$, and for $0<\omega<1$ neither does. The first-order coefficient in
$c$ of the first-read marginal is $\omega$ times the benchmark's coefficient
for the same marginal. At $c=0$ the third line equals $(1-\omega)(\alpha-q_0)$,
so a partially adopting evaluator shows a sequence effect at independence, where
the fully adopting evaluator (Proposition~\ref{prop:IMM}) and the benchmark
show none. Whenever $P^{A}(B{=}1)\neq r_1$, the first line gives the weight from
three marginals of one reading group, for every $c$,
\[
  \omega=\frac{P^{A}(B{=}1)-P^{\omega}_{AB}(B{=}1)}{P^{A}(B{=}1)-r_1}.
\]
\end{proposition}
\par
The proof is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.
\par
In Example~\ref{ex:panel}, an evaluator who reads the credential
first rates trustworthiness at $.56$ before the letter, the letter alone delivers
$.30$, and a final rating of $.43$ gives $\omega=(.56-.43)/(.56-.30)=\tfrac12$.

Proposition~\ref{prop:ADJ} reads the weight from the ratings. The next result says
what the weight does to the order in $c$ at which each statistic first differs between
the two sequences. A statistic is \emph{protected
for every pair of cues} if its differential at every independent belief is a
multiple of the differential of the association, the condition of
Proposition~\ref{prop:PRO} imposed at every point of the independence surface
rather than at $q\otimes r$ alone; the association and the correlation coefficient
are examples.
\par
\renewcommand{\theproposition}{LAD}%
\begin{proposition}[partial adoption]\label{prop:LAD}
Let $0\le\omega\le1$, $t_0=(1-\omega)\beta+\omega r_0$, $s_0=(1-\omega)\alpha+\omega q_0$,
$t=(t_0,1-t_0)$, $s=(s_0,1-s_0)$ and $Z=\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] At $c=0$ the two sequences end at the independent beliefs $q\otimes t$
and $s\otimes r$, and
\[
  P^{\omega}_{AB}-P^{\omega}_{BA}\big|_{c=0}
  =(1-\omega)(\beta-r_0)\,R_1+(1-\omega)(q_0-\alpha)\,R_2 .
\]
Every marginal therefore differs between the sequences at order zero in $c$, the
$A$-marginal by $(1-\omega)(\alpha-q_0)$, while a statistic protected for every pair
of cues differs between them by $\bigO(c)$.
\item[(ii)] The believed association differs between the sequences by
\[
  \assoc(P^{\omega}_{AB})-\assoc(P^{\omega}_{BA})=c\,\frac{(1-\omega)H}{Z}+\bigO(c^{2}),
\]
\[
  H=q_0q_1\bigl[\beta(1-\beta)+\omega(\beta-r_0)^2\bigr]
   -r_0r_1\bigl[\alpha(1-\alpha)+\omega(\alpha-q_0)^2\bigr],
\]
which is first order whenever $\omega<1$ and $H\neq0$, and second order at $\omega=1$.
\item[(iii)] A statistic protected for every pair of cues differs between the
sequences by $\bigO(c^{2})$ for every $\omega$ if and only if its differential at
every independent belief is a multiple of the differential of the log odds ratio,
as for $\assoc(P)/\bigl(P(A{=}0)P(A{=}1)P(B{=}0)P(B{=}1)\bigr)$. The odds ratio
itself is the same in both sequences for every $c$ and every $\omega$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\par
The proof is provided in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.
\par
Partial adoption therefore moves the sequence effect on each belief statistic other
than the odds ratio one order earlier in $c$, the marginals staying ahead of the
association and the association ahead of the odds ratio, which never moves. Decisions
do not keep their places. At $c=0$ the
benchmark is $q\otimes r$ while sequence $AB$ ends at $q\otimes t$, so under partial
adoption an evaluator's score departs from the benchmark's at order zero, and the
share of decisions the sequence changes and the surplus-weighted loss are then both
of order zero. The separation of Theorem~\ref{thm:LOS} is a property of full
adoption.

The paper's central contribution is a characterisation that delineates the conditions under which an arbitrary smooth statistic of the average belief is protected, at first order, from the non-commutativity of coherent sequential updating. This characterisation is built up from the fundamental observation that the gap is not an arbitrary perturbation. Each reading sequence contributes one route direction ($R_1$ and $R_2$), so the leading-order departures of the average posterior from the benchmark are confined to the plane those two directions span. Whether a statistic is protected therefore turns on whether its differential annihilates the route plane or not.

This answer carries three implications for audits where soft-cue updating can be assumed. First, an aggregate matching the Bayesian benchmark does not
license the inference that its members are Bayesian. The population's mean believed association
and its surplus-weighted loss sit within $\bigO(c^{2})$ of the benchmark while every member's
gap diverges at $\bigO(c)$. At leading order, a population's aggregate beliefs can
look externally Bayesian---sequence-free, benchmark-conforming on the protected
statistics---even when no member's belief is.

Second, what an audit can detect is decided by the statistic it computes. The protections of
the two layers have separate sources---separability keeps the mean believed association
first-order immune (Proposition~\ref{prop:DEC}), while the stake-weighting of the flip band
keeps the surplus-weighted loss second order (Theorem~\ref{thm:LOS})---and both leave the
protected statistics silent about a first-order incidence of sequence-decided outcomes. A null
on a protected statistic is therefore evidence about the instrument rather than about the
population. Collecting more observations and disaggregating into subgroups address the
estimation error, not the quantity estimated. Enlarging the sample sharpens the estimate of a
protected aggregate but leaves it at $\bigO(c^{2})$, and a subgroup is a subpopulation with its
own mixture of reading sequences. The aggregate results are, in this
sense, reassuring exactly insofar as they are cautionary. For the two-cue setting, there are no protected statistics beyond the local dependence measures and, for the threshold decision, the surplus-weighted loss.

Third, the instrumental point bears on how a stereotype is looked for
outside the model. The instruments a society most naturally trusts for detecting stereotype are
of the belief-auditing kind---asking what a population believes, in aggregate, about how
attributes go together---and the association they measure is protected
(Table~\ref{tab:audit-questions}). What the paper finds is that a population could pass every
belief-level audit sincerely while the footprint of sequence-dependence lives entirely in
outcomes. Debates that set
improving attitudes against persistent outcome disparities may, on this reading, be comparing
instruments of different asymptotic order rather than disagreeing about the same quantity.

Section~\ref{sec:literature} framed the debate over Jeffrey conditioning as whether its
sequence dependence is a problem. The results do not answer that objection so much as
locate it, in the levels rather than in the believed link between the traits, and in
the number of decisions changed rather than in their cost. The normative question is
left where \citet{Doring1999} and \citet{Hawthorne2004} leave it. What the paper
settles is where the dependence sits and what it costs, and both are properties of
full adoption, since under partial adoption the believed link moves at first order as
well (Proposition~\ref{prop:LAD}).

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV}}

\emph{Step 1: coincidence at $c=0$.} The prior factorises, $P(i,j)=P(A{=}i)P(B{=}j)$, and each
Jeffrey step resets one coordinate's marginal while leaving the other untouched, as does the
matched Bayesian update. Hence
\[
  \PJ_{AB}\big|_{c=0}=\PJ_{BA}\big|_{c=0}=\PB\big|_{c=0}=q\otimes r,
  \qquad
  \bigl(\PJ_{AB}-\PB\bigr)\big|_{c=0}
   =\bigl(\PJ_{AB}-\PJ_{BA}\bigr)\big|_{c=0}=0 .
\]

\emph{Step 2: Taylor expansion.} Each posterior is a rational function of $c$ whose denominator
is nonvanishing near $0$, so both differences are analytic there and, having no constant term by
Step 1,
\[
  \PJ_{AB}-\PB=c\,D+\bigO(c^{2}),
  \qquad
  \PJ_{AB}-\PJ_{BA}=c\,\Delta_{\mathrm{seq}}+\bigO(c^{2}) .
\]

\emph{Step 3: the leading gap.} Differentiating at $c=0$, with
$Z=\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)>0$,
\[
  D_{00}=-D_{01}=\frac{q_0 r_0\,(q_0-\alpha)(r_0-1)}{Z},
  \qquad
  D_{10}=-D_{11}=\frac{-\,r_0\,(q_0-\alpha)(q_0-1)(r_0-1)}{Z},
\]
\[
  \text{equivalently}\qquad
  D=\kappa\,q\otimes(1,-1),
  \qquad
  \kappa=\frac{(\alpha-q_0)\,r_0(1-r_0)}{Z} .
\]
The $(1,-1)$ contrast makes the $A$-marginal preservation manifest, $D_{i0}=-D_{i1}$.

\emph{Step 4: $D\not\equiv0$.} The scalar $\kappa$ factors into the surprise in the
$A$-impression and the spread of the $B$-impression,
\[
  \kappa\propto(\alpha-q_0),
  \qquad
  \kappa\propto r_0(1-r_0),
  \qquad\text{so}\qquad
  \kappa=0
  \iff
  q_0=\alpha \ \ \text{or}\ \ r_0\in\{0,1\},
\]
and hence $D\not\equiv0$ for any $q_0\neq\alpha$ in $(0,1)$ and $r_0\in(0,1)$.

\emph{Step 5: the sequence effect.} For the diagonal,
\[
  \Delta_{\mathrm{seq},\,00}
   =\frac{q_0 r_0\bigl[(\alpha-\beta)+r_0(1-\alpha)-q_0(1-\beta)\bigr]}{Z},
\]
whose bracket vanishes only on a single hypersurface, so $\Delta_{\mathrm{seq},\,00}$ is nonzero
generically. On the symmetry locus $\{\alpha=\beta,\ q_0=r_0\}$ the bracket vanishes and the
effect is confined to the antisymmetric off-diagonal part,
\[
  \Delta_{\mathrm{seq},\,01}=-\Delta_{\mathrm{seq},\,10}
   =\frac{q_0(1-q_0)\,(q_0-\alpha)}{\alpha^{2}(1-\alpha)^{2}}
  \ \neq 0
  \quad\text{unless } q_0=\alpha .
\]
Both coefficients satisfy $\sum_k D_k=\sum_k\Delta_{\mathrm{seq},\,k}=0$, as a difference of
probability distributions must.
\qed

\subsection{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:SEP}}

Write the update sequence as $P_{0}=P$ and, for $t=1,\dots,T$,
\[
  P_{t}(x)=P_{t-1}(x)\,h_{t}(x_{a_{t}}),
\]
where $a_{t}$ is the attribute the $t$-th update reads and $h_{t}$ is a nonnegative function
of that single coordinate. Jeffrey updates take exactly this form:
\[
  h(x_{a})=\frac{q_{x_{a}}}{P_{t-1}(A_{a}{=}x_{a})},
\]
state-dependent only through its normaliser $P_{t-1}(A_a{=}x_a)$---a function of $x_{a}$ alone,
which is all the induction below uses.

We claim, by induction on $t$, that
$P_{t}(x)=P(x)\prod_{a=1}^{N}g^{(t)}_{a}(x_{a})$ for some single-attribute functions
$g^{(t)}_{a}$. The base case $t=0$ holds with $g^{(0)}_{a}\equiv1$. For the inductive step,
suppose the claim at $t-1$; then
\[
\begin{gathered}
  P_{t}(x)
  =P(x)\Big[\prod_{a}g^{(t-1)}_{a}(x_{a})\Big]h_{t}(x_{a_{t}})
  =P(x)\prod_{a}g^{(t)}_{a}(x_{a}),
  \\[4pt]
  g^{(t)}_{a}:=
  \begin{cases}
    g^{(t-1)}_{a}\cdot h_{t}, & a=a_{t},\\[2pt]
    g^{(t-1)}_{a}, & a\neq a_{t}
  \end{cases}
\end{gathered}
\]
the new factor folds into $g^{(t)}_{a_t}$ alone; no step couples two coordinates. Taking
$g_{a}:=g^{(T)}_{a}$ gives the displayed form.

For the equivalent formulation, on the common support (cells with $P(x)>0$ and every
accumulated factor positive) take logarithms:
\[
  \log\frac{P'(x)}{P(x)}=\sum_{a=1}^{N}\log g_{a}(x_{a}),
\]
an additive function of the individual coordinates; write $\varphi:=\log(P'/P)$. For any pair
$a<b$, define the pairwise contrast
\[
  \Delta_{ab}\varphi(x)\;:=\;\varphi(x)\big|_{x_{a}=1,x_{b}=1}-\varphi(x)\big|_{x_{a}=1,x_{b}=0}
  -\varphi(x)\big|_{x_{a}=0,x_{b}=1}+\varphi(x)\big|_{x_{a}=0,x_{b}=0},
\]
holding every coordinate other than $a,b$ fixed. Writing $R:=\sum_{c\neq a,b}\log g_{c}(x_{c})$
for the (fixed) contribution of the remaining coordinates,
\begin{align*}
  \Delta_{ab}\varphi
  &=\bigl[\log g_{a}(1)+\log g_{b}(1)+R\bigr]-\bigl[\log g_{a}(1)+\log g_{b}(0)+R\bigr]\\
  &\quad-\bigl[\log g_{a}(0)+\log g_{b}(1)+R\bigr]+\bigl[\log g_{a}(0)+\log g_{b}(0)+R\bigr]
  \;=\;0,
\end{align*}
valid for every pair $a<b$ and every fixing of the other $N-2$ coordinates. Higher-order
contrasts telescope into this one: for any three coordinates $a,b,c$, and any $\varphi$
whatsoever (additive or not),
\[
  \Delta_{abc}\varphi \;=\; \Delta_{ab}\varphi\big|_{x_{c}=1}-\Delta_{ab}\varphi\big|_{x_{c}=0},
\]
and analogously at every order above three, so $\Delta_{ab}\varphi\equiv0$ for all pairs forces
every $\Delta_{S}\varphi\equiv0$, $|S|\ge2$---exactly the interaction coefficients $\lambda_{S}$
of the log-linear (M\"obius) decomposition
$\varphi=\lambda_{\emptyset}+\sum_{a}\lambda_{a}(x_{a})+\sum_{a<b}\lambda_{ab}(x_{a},x_{b})
+\cdots$. Conversely,
\[
  \lambda_{\emptyset}:=\varphi(0),\qquad
  \lambda_{a}(1):=\varphi(x_{a}{=}1,x_{-a}{=}0)-\varphi(0),\qquad \lambda_{a}(0):=0
\]
reproduces $\varphi(x)$ exactly at every $x$ once every pairwise contrast vanishes, by the same
telescope run backward. Separability of $P'/P$ and vanishing interaction of order $\ge2$ are
thus the same condition both ways. Nothing above is specific to Jeffrey updating, $N=2$, or
sequence length.
\qed

\subsection{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:LOS}}

\emph{Step 1: bounded coefficient.} The two actions differ iff
$\sgn(u)\neq\sgn(u+c\delta_\sigma)$. At the fixed prior and impressions
$\|\partial_c(\PJ_\sigma-\PB)|_{0}\|<\infty$, and $\|\vv\|\le V<\infty$ across the population
(Assumption~\ref{as:surplus}), so
\[
  |\delta_\sigma|
   =\bigl|\langle\vv,\partial_c(\PJ_\sigma-\PB)|_{0}\rangle\bigr|
   \le M<\infty,
  \qquad \sigma\in\{AB,BA\}.
\]

\emph{Step 2: the flip set lies in a thin band.} For fixed $\delta$ the flip set in $u$ is the
open interval strictly between $0$ and $-c\delta$, whence
\[
  \{\mathrm{flip}\}\ \subseteq\ B_c:=\{\,u:\ 0<|u|\le cM\,\},
  \qquad
  u\in B_c\ \Longrightarrow\ |u|\le cM.
\]
That the band excludes $u=0$ does matter in the later Step~4.

\emph{Step 3: the bound.} With $\mu$ the marginal law of $u$, Tonelli's theorem
\citep[Corollary~1.7.23]{tao2011measure} gives
\[
  L(c)=\iint_{\mathrm{flip}}|u|\,g(u,\delta)\,du\,d\delta
   \;\le\; cM\!\iint_{u\in B_c}\! g(u,\delta)\,du\,d\delta
   \;=\; cM\,\mu(B_c).
\]

\emph{Step 4: the band is null in the limit.} The bands nest and exhaust,
\[
  c'<c\ \Longrightarrow\ B_{c'}\subseteq B_c,
  \qquad
  \bigcap_{c>0}B_c=\varnothing,
\]
since $u\neq0$ exits once $cM<|u|$ and $u=0$ lies in no $B_c$. As $\mu$ is finite, continuity
from above \citep[\S1.4]{tao2011measure} yields
\[
  \mu(B_c)\downarrow\mu(\varnothing)=0,
  \qquad
  \frac{L(c)}{c}\le M\,\mu(B_c)\longrightarrow0,
  \qquad
  L(c)=o(c),
\]
for \emph{any} integrable density, and irrespective of an atom at $u=0$, where $|u|=0$.

\emph{Step 5: named constant available under continuity.} Let the $u$-marginal be continuous at $0$ with
$f(0)>0$. For fixed $\delta$,
\[
  \int_0^{|c\delta|}t\,g(0,\delta)\,dt=\tfrac{1}{2}\,g(0,\delta)\,\delta^{2}c^{2}+\bigO(c^3),
\]
and with
\[
  f(0)=\int g(0,\delta)\,d\delta,
  \qquad
  \E[\delta^2\mid u=0]=\frac{1}{f(0)}\int\delta^2 g(0,\delta)\,d\delta,
\]
integration over $\delta$ gives
\[
  L(c)=\frac{f(0)}{2}\,\E[\delta_\sigma^2\mid u=0]\,c^{2}+\bigO(c^3).
\]
\qed

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:PRO}}

\emph{Step 1: the aggregate moves in a plane.} By Proposition~\ref{prop:DIV} and its mirror,
\[
  \PJ_{AB}-\PB=c\,\kappa R_1+\bigO(c^2),
  \qquad
  \PJ_{BA}-\PB=c\,\kappa' R_2+\bigO(c^2),
\]
\[
  R_1=q\otimes(1,-1),\quad \kappa=\frac{(\alpha-q_0)r_0(1-r_0)}{Z},
  \qquad
  R_2=(1,-1)\otimes r,\quad \kappa'=\frac{(\beta-r_0)q_0(1-q_0)}{Z},
\]
so with $M_\lambda:=\lambda\kappa R_1+(1-\lambda)\kappa' R_2$ the chain rule gives
\[
  \Pbar_\lambda-\PB=c\,M_\lambda+\bigO(c^2),
  \qquad
  F(\Pbar_\lambda)-F(\PB)=c\,\bigl\langle\nabla F(q\otimes r),M_\lambda\bigr\rangle+\bigO(c^2).
\]

\emph{Step 2: the annihilator is $\mathrm{span}\{J,\nabla\assoc\}$.} The directions are
independent---$aR_1+bR_2=0$ gives $b(r_0+r_1)=b=0$ on adding the top-row entries, then $aq_0=0$
and $a=0$---so
\[
  \dim\bigl\{V\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times2}:\ \langle V,R_1\rangle=\langle V,R_2\rangle=0\bigr\}=4-2=2 .
\]
Both $J$ (all entries $1$) and $\nabla\assoc(q\otimes r)$ lie in this space,
\[
  \langle J,R_1\rangle=\langle J,R_2\rangle=0,
  \qquad
  \langle\nabla\assoc,R_1\rangle=\langle\nabla\assoc,R_2\rangle=0
  \quad(\text{Lemma~\ref{lem:ASC}}),
\]
and are linearly independent, hence span it.

\emph{Step 3: part (i).} If $dF|_{q\otimes r}$ restricted to the simplex is a multiple of
$d\,\assoc$, then
\[
  \nabla F(q\otimes r)=xJ+y\,\nabla\assoc(q\otimes r)
  \ \Longrightarrow\
  \bigl\langle\nabla F,M_\lambda\bigr\rangle=0
  \quad\text{identically in }(\alpha,\beta,\lambda).
\]

\emph{Step 4: part (ii).} Put $x=\langle\nabla F,R_1\rangle$ and $y=\langle\nabla F,R_2\rangle$,
so the first-order coefficient $\lambda\kappa\,x+(1-\lambda)\kappa'\,y$ vanishes on an open set
$U$ of priors. Suppose $y\neq0$. If $x=0$ then $\kappa'y\equiv0$ on $U$, and $\kappa'$ vanishes
only on $\{\beta=r_0\}$, forcing $y=0$; so $x\neq0$ and, on $U\setminus\{\beta=r_0\}$,
\[
  \frac{\kappa}{\kappa'}=-\frac{(1-\lambda)y}{\lambda x}=\text{const},
  \qquad\text{while}\qquad
  \frac{\kappa}{\kappa'}=\frac{(\alpha-q_0)\,r_0(1-r_0)}{(\beta-r_0)\,q_0(1-q_0)},
\]
\[
  \frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}\frac{\kappa}{\kappa'}
   =\frac{r_0(1-r_0)}{(\beta-r_0)\,q_0(1-q_0)}\neq0
  \quad\text{wherever }\beta\neq r_0,
\]
a contradiction. Hence $y=0$, then $\kappa x\equiv0$ on $U$ forces $x=0$, so
$\nabla F(q\otimes r)\in\mathrm{span}\{J,\nabla\assoc\}$. On the tangent space of the simplex,
whose vectors have coordinates summing to zero, $J$ acts as zero, so $dF$ is a multiple of
$d\,\assoc$.
\qed

The interior-$\lambda$ hypothesis in \emph{(ii)} is essential rather than technical. At
$\lambda=0$ or $\lambda=1$ only one route direction enters the first-order coefficient, and
the annihilator of a single direction is three-dimensional rather than two, so vanishing on
an open set of priors pins $\nabla F$ only to that larger space, not to the span of the
constants and $\nabla\assoc$.

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:ADJ}}

With $Q$ the belief after the first step and $t=(t_0,t_1)$,
$t_1=(1-\omega)\,Q(B{=}1)+\omega r_1$, the target of the second, column $j$
is multiplied by $t_j/Q(B{=}j)$, so that
\[
\begin{aligned}
  P(B{=}1)&=t_1,\\
  P(A{=}1)&=\sum_j Q(A{=}1,B{=}j)\,\frac{t_j}{Q(B{=}j)}\\
  &=(1-\omega)\,Q(A{=}1)+\omega\sum_j Q(A{=}1,B{=}j)\,\frac{r_j}{Q(B{=}j)}.
\end{aligned}
\]
In sequence $AB$, $Q=P^{A}$, $Q(A{=}1)=q_1$, and the last sum is
$\PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)$, giving
\[
  P^{\omega}_{AB}(B{=}1)-r_1=(1-\omega)\bigl[P^{A}(B{=}1)-r_1\bigr],\qquad
  P^{\omega}_{AB}(A{=}1)-q_1=\omega\bigl[\PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)-q_1\bigr].
\]
In sequence $BA$, by symmetry,
$P^{\omega}_{BA}(A{=}1)=(1-\omega)\,P^{B}(A{=}1)+\omega q_1$; subtracting
gives the third line. Neither bracket vanishes identically, since
\[
  P^{A}(B{=}1)-r_1\Big|_{c=0}=r_0-\beta,\qquad
  \PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)-q_1=-c\,\frac{q_0(1-q_0)(r_0-\beta)}{\alpha\beta(1-\alpha)(1-\beta)}+\bigO(c^2),
\]
the second by Proposition~\ref{prop:DRF}, whose proof also gives
$\PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)-\PB(A{=}1)=\bigO(c^2)$, hence the coefficient claim. At
$c=0$ a step leaves the other marginal unchanged, so
\[
  \PJ_{AB}(A{=}1)=q_1,\qquad P^{B}(A{=}1)=1-\alpha,\qquad
  P^{\omega}_{AB}(A{=}1)-P^{\omega}_{BA}(A{=}1)=(1-\omega)(\alpha-q_0).
\]
For $P^{A}(B{=}1)\neq r_1$ the first line gives
\[
  1-\omega=\frac{P^{\omega}_{AB}(B{=}1)-r_1}{P^{A}(B{=}1)-r_1},\qquad
  \omega=\frac{P^{A}(B{=}1)-P^{\omega}_{AB}(B{=}1)}{P^{A}(B{=}1)-r_1}.
\]
\qed

\subsection{Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:LAD}}

Every Jeffrey step, damped or not, multiplies the rows or the columns of the table
by constants, so after both steps $P^{\omega}_{\sigma}(i,j)=a_i b_j P(i,j)$ for some
factors $a_0,a_1,b_0,b_1$. Such a rescaling multiplies the association by
$a_0a_1b_0b_1$ and leaves the odds ratio unchanged, and $\assoc(P)=c$, so
$\assoc(P^{\omega}_{\sigma})=c\,k_\sigma$ with $k_\sigma=a_0a_1b_0b_1$, and the odds
ratio claim of (iii) follows. (i) At $c=0$ a step on one attribute leaves the other
marginal unchanged (Proposition~\ref{prop:IMM}), so sequence $AB$ reaches $q\otimes\beta$
and then $q\otimes t$, and sequence $BA$ reaches $s\otimes r$; subtracting gives the
display. A statistic protected for every pair of cues has a differential that
annihilates both tangent directions of the independence surface at each of its
points (Lemma~\ref{lem:ASC}), so it is constant on the surface and takes the same
value at $q\otimes t$ and $s\otimes r$. (ii) At $c=0$ the factors give
$k_{AB}=q_0q_1t_0t_1/Z$ and $k_{BA}=s_0s_1r_0r_1/Z$, and $k_{AB}-k_{BA}=(1-\omega)H/Z$.
(iii) Near the independence surface a statistic protected for every pair of cues
is $F=F_0+\assoc\cdot h$ with $h$ smooth, and writing $h=g/m$ with
$m=P(A{=}0)P(A{=}1)P(B{=}0)P(B{=}1)$, the first-order coefficient of
$F(P^{\omega}_{AB})-F(P^{\omega}_{BA})$ is
$k_{AB}\,h(q\otimes t)-k_{BA}\,h(s\otimes r)=\bigl[g(q\otimes t)-g(s\otimes r)\bigr]/Z$,
since $m(q\otimes t)=q_0q_1t_0t_1$ and $m(s\otimes r)=s_0s_1r_0r_1$. It vanishes for
every pair of cues and every $\omega$ exactly when $g$ is constant on the
independence surface, that is when the differential of $F$ there is a multiple of
the differential of $\assoc/m$, which at an independent belief equals the
differential of the log odds ratio.
\qed
