\noindent Setting. The prior covariance is $c=0$, so the two attributes are
believed unrelated, and the adoption weight satisfies $0<\omega<1$, so the cue
read second is adopted in part. At $c=0$ the paper's channel, sequence
dependence carried through the prior association, is switched off. What
remains is a second channel, the weakening of whichever cue is read second,
which is at full strength here. Full adoption ($\omega=1$) is the setting of
Sections~4 and~5, where the second channel is absent and the first vanishes at
$c=0$ (Proposition IMM). All quantities below are exact at $c=0$; the
$\bigO(c)$ corrections are in the script and are not the point.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{The two settings compared.} The primacy regime is taken for
the interior, since with $\omega$ on the second cue every interior value
weakens the later impression. Weisberg (\emph{Commutativity or Holism?}, read
in full 2026-09-27; see \texttt{literature/weisberg2009}) supplies the
vocabulary. He separates the \emph{rule} that propagates a change on a
partition from the \emph{inputs problem} of how an experience fixes the values
on that partition; Lange's point is that reversing two experiences does not
reverse their input values; Wagner's theorem, read as a dilemma, says that
commutativity on experiences forces each experience to carry the same Bayes
factor whatever background it meets, which is anti-holism; and rigidity, the
holding of conditionals fixed, prevents a later discovery from ever
\emph{undercutting} earlier support, leaving only rebutting. Marks: $\dagger$
a row of Table~1 or of \texttt{verify\_interior\_omega.py}; $\ddagger$
inferred from those rows, not separately checked; $\diamond$ a mapping onto
Weisberg's terms, not a computation.

\medskip
\noindent What the comparison says about scope. The paper's contribution lives
in the regime where the sequence effect is subtle, arising in the rule rather
than in the inputs, existing only because the traits are believed related, and
therefore small and spread unevenly across statistics. That is the regime in
which an audit can be fooled by choosing the wrong statistic. In the primacy
regime the effect is blunt and any marginal shows it; the question becomes not
whether the sequence registers but how much of the earlier impression
survived, which is a question about $\omega$, answered by ADJ and nothing
else. The two regimes are two different questions. Under full adoption the
association audit fails by silence; under partial adoption it fails by
fabrication as well.

\medskip
\noindent Two sentences for the scope statement (approved 2026-09-27).

\noindent The position channel is a temporal bias of the observer, present
whether or not the traits are believed related, while the paper's channel is
an interaction between the cues that exists only when they are.

\noindent Full adoption is the setting in which the
only source of sequence dependence is the one the paper studies, the
cross-attribute effect carried through the prior association, which vanishes
at $c=0$; partial adoption adds a second source, the weakening of whichever
cue is read second, which is present at $c=0$ and so lies outside the
expansion in $c$ on which the results rest.

\section*{Two paragraphs for Section 6}

\noindent Both drafted 2026-09-27 and approved in discussion. The first names
the two channels; the second turns the scope condition into opposite
predictions about one procedure. They follow 6.A in the plan. Every quantity
is a row of Table~1 or a proposition already in the manuscript; the decision
row of Table~1, which is inferred, is not used. Rubric scoring is a new
element, an extension of the hiring-panel example, and no empirical claim is
made about whether rubrics work.

\paragraph{The two channels.}
Sequence dependence has two sources in this setting, and the results of
Sections~4 and~5 concern one of them. The first is the cross-attribute
effect. A cue on one attribute moves the belief about the other through the
prior association, so the second cue meets a belief the first has already
changed. This effect is first order in $c$ and vanishes at independence,
where each cue sets its own marginal and touches nothing else (Proposition
IMM). The second is position. If the cue read second is adopted only in
part, then which cue is weakened depends on the order, and the two sequences
differ even at $c=0$, by $(1-\omega)(\alpha-q_0)$ on the competence marginal
and $-(1-\omega)(\beta-r_0)$ on the trustworthiness marginal. The second
source is not a feature of updating on delivered credences. A Bayes-factor
update that gives the second cue's factor the weight $\omega$ is
order-dependent in the same way, since commutation requires the same factor
in either position (Wagner 2002). Under full adoption the second source is
absent and the first is the only one, which is the setting of the results.
Under partial adoption the second source is present at $c=0$ and dominates
the first near it, so the classification by order in $c$ no longer measures
what it was built to measure; the marginals differ at zeroth order, the
believed association still does not differ between sequences at $c=0$, and
the mean belief of a population mixing the two sequences acquires a
cross-product association of
$-\lambda(1-\lambda)(1-\omega)^2(\alpha-q_0)(\beta-r_0)$ that no member
holds. What the paper says about the second source is confined to
Proposition ADJ, which recovers its strength from the ratings. The position
channel is a temporal bias of the observer, present whether or not the
traits are believed related, while the paper's channel is an interaction
between the cues that exists only when they are.

\paragraph{Opposite predictions about one procedure.}
Whether a procedure removes the sequence effect depends on which channel it
removes. Consider rubric scoring, in which the rating on each attribute is
fixed by the document on that attribute and a set scale, so that what each
document delivers no longer depends on what was read first. Under full
adoption the rubric removes the position channel and leaves the paper's
channel untouched. The recorded scores carry the delivered credences in
either sequence, the belief about the attribute read second agrees with its
score, and the belief about the attribute read first sits at $cK$ from its
score (Proposition DRF), so the sequence effect lives in that belief and in
the decision made from it, vanishes when the attributes are believed
unrelated (Proposition IMM), and is invisible in the scores and in the
association between them. Under partial adoption the rubric removes the
position channel from the record only. The belief about the attribute read
second sits at $(1-\omega)[P^{A}(B{=}1)-r_1]$ from its score (Proposition
ADJ), which at independence equals $(1-\omega)(r_0-\beta)$, so the two
reading sequences differ even when the attributes are believed unrelated.
The two settings therefore predict opposite things about the same procedure,
and the difference is observable. In the rubric setting $r_1$ is on the
record, so Proposition ADJ needs only the belief about trustworthiness after
the credential alone and after both documents to say which prediction holds.

\section*{Where the prelude is expanded in the manuscript}

\noindent The introduction's prelude (approved 2026-09-27) names the two
channels and says which one the paper studies:

\begin{quote}\small
More specifically, arrival-sequence dependence can enter through two
channels, an \textbf{interaction} between the cues that exists only when the
attributes are believed related, and a temporal \textbf{bias} of the observer
that weights the later cue less whatever the attributes are
\citep{HogarthEinhorn1992,Asch1946}. Unlike the bias setting, where every
marginal registers the sequence even when the attributes are believed
unrelated, the interaction setting is one where the sequence registers
unevenly across statistics, because each impression is adopted in full
\citep{DiaconisZabell1982} and the bias is absent. The current paper explores
this setting.
\end{quote}
