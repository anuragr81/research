\section*{1. The prelude}

\noindent For the introduction, immediately after the opening sentence saying
that how sequence dependence registers in observed statistics has received
little attention, and before the paper's own contribution is stated (plan
1.A2). Approved 2026-09-27, with the settings made explicit the same day.

\begin{quote}
More specifically, arrival-sequence dependence can enter through two
channels, an \textbf{association} channel, in which one cue changes what the
other implies and which exists only when the attributes are believed related,
and a \textbf{position} channel, in which the observer weights the later cue
less whatever the attributes are \citep{HogarthEinhorn1992,Asch1946}. Which
channels operate is fixed by how fully the later cue is adopted. When it is
adopted only in part, the position channel operates and every marginal
registers the sequence even when the attributes are believed unrelated. When
each impression is adopted in full \citep{DiaconisZabell1982}, the position
channel is absent, the association channel is the only one, and the sequence
registers unevenly across statistics. The current paper explores the
full-adoption setting. Table~\ref{tab:settings} in Section~6 sets the two
settings side by side.
\end{quote}

\noindent The prelude does four things, and each section below expands one
of them. It names the two channels (Sections~4 and~5 below give their
mechanics). It says that the adoption weight decides which channels operate
(Section~3). It says what each setting does to the statistics (the table it
cites, Section~2). And it says which setting the paper studies (Section~6,
the two paragraphs for the scope section).

since with $\omega$ on the second cue every interior value weakens the later
impression. Weisberg (\emph{Commutativity or Holism?}, read in full
2026-09-27; see \texttt{literature/weisberg2009}) supplies the vocabulary. He
separates the \emph{rule} that propagates a change on a partition from the
\emph{inputs problem} of how an experience fixes the values on that
partition; Lange's point is that reversing two experiences does not reverse
their input values; Wagner's theorem, read as a dilemma, says that
commutativity on experiences forces each experience to carry the same Bayes
factor whatever background it meets, which is anti-holism; and rigidity, the
holding of conditionals fixed, prevents a later discovery from ever
\emph{undercutting} earlier support, leaving only rebutting. Marks: $\dagger$
a row of Table~\ref{tab:indep} or of \texttt{verify\_interior\_omega.py},

\noindent What the comparison says about scope. The paper's contribution lives
in the regime where the sequence effect is subtle, arising in the rule rather
than in the inputs, existing only because the traits are believed related, and
therefore small and spread unevenly across statistics. That is the regime in
which an audit can be fooled by choosing the wrong statistic. In the primacy
regime the effect is blunt and any marginal shows it; the question becomes not
whether the sequence registers but how much of the earlier impression
survived, which is a question about $\omega$, answered by ADJ and nothing
else. The two regimes are two different questions. Under full adoption the
association audit fails by silence; under partial adoption it fails by
fabrication as well.

\section*{6. Two paragraphs for Section 6, and the scope sentences}

\noindent These expand the prelude's last clauses, that the paper explores
the full-adoption setting and that the adoption weight decides which channels
operate. They follow 6.A in the plan (6.B), with Table~\ref{tab:settings}
beside them. Every quantity is a row of Table~\ref{tab:indep} or a
proposition already in the manuscript; the decision row of
Table~\ref{tab:indep}, which is inferred, is not used. Rubric scoring is a
new element, an extension of the hiring-panel example, and no empirical claim
is made about whether rubrics work.

\paragraph{The two channels.}
Sequence dependence has two channels in this setting, and the results of
Sections~4 and~5 concern one of them. The first is the association channel.
A cue on one attribute moves the belief about the other through the
prior association, so the second cue meets a belief the first has already
changed. This effect is first order in $c$ and vanishes at independence,
where each cue sets its own marginal and touches nothing else (Proposition
IMM). The second is the position channel. If the cue read second is adopted
only in part, then which cue is weakened depends on the order, and the two sequences
differ even at $c=0$, by $(1-\omega)(\alpha-q_0)$ on the competence marginal
and $-(1-\omega)(\beta-r_0)$ on the trustworthiness marginal. The position
channel is not a feature of updating on delivered credences. A Bayes-factor
update that gives the second cue's factor the weight $\omega$ is
order-dependent in the same way, since commutation requires the same factor
in either position (Wagner 2002). Under full adoption the position channel
is absent and the association channel is the only one, which is the setting
of the results. Under partial adoption the position channel is present at
$c=0$ and moves every marginal at zeroth order, while the believed
association still does not differ between sequences at $c=0$ and differs at
first order; the classification by order in $c$ shifts by one order without
changing the ranking of statistics (Proposition LAD, corrected 2026-10-01), and
the mean belief of a population mixing the two sequences acquires a
cross-product association of
$-\lambda(1-\lambda)(1-\omega)^2(\alpha-q_0)(\beta-r_0)$ that no member
holds. What the paper says about the position channel is confined to
Propositions ADJ and LAD, the first recovering its strength from the ratings
and the second stating what it does to the ranking. The position
channel is a temporal bias of the observer, present whether or not the
attributes are believed related, while the association channel runs through
what one cue implies about the other and exists only when they are.

\paragraph{Opposite predictions about one procedure.}
Whether a procedure removes the sequence effect depends on which channel it
removes. Consider rubric scoring, in which the rating on each attribute is
fixed by the document on that attribute and a set scale, so that what each
document delivers no longer depends on what was read first. Under full
adoption the rubric removes the position channel and leaves the association
channel untouched. The recorded scores carry the delivered credences in
either sequence, the belief about the attribute read second agrees with its
score, and the belief about the attribute read first sits at $cK$ from its
score (Proposition DRF), so the sequence effect lives in that belief and in
the decision made from it, vanishes when the attributes are believed
unrelated (Proposition IMM), and is invisible in the scores and in the
association between them. Under partial adoption the rubric removes the
position channel from the record only. The belief about the attribute read
second sits at $(1-\omega)[P^{A}(B{=}1)-r_1]$ from its score (Proposition
ADJ), which at independence equals $(1-\omega)(r_0-\beta)$, so the two
reading sequences differ even when the attributes are believed unrelated.
The two settings therefore predict opposite things about the same procedure,
and the difference is observable. In the rubric setting $r_1$ is on the
record, so Proposition ADJ needs only the belief about trustworthiness after
the credential alone and after both documents to say which prediction holds.

\paragraph{Two sentences for the scope statement (approved 2026-09-27).}
The position channel is a temporal bias of the observer, present whether or
not the attributes are believed related, while the association channel runs
through what one cue implies about the other and exists only when they are.
Full adoption is the setting in which the only source of sequence dependence
is the association channel, which vanishes at $c=0$; partial adoption adds
the position channel, the weakening of whichever cue is read second, which is
present at $c=0$ and so lies outside the expansion in $c$ on which the
results rest.
