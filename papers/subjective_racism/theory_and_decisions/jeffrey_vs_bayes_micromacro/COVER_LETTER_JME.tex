Dear Editors,

I submit the manuscript ``Effects of Reading Sequence on Belief and Decision Statistics
under Jeffrey Conditioning'' for consideration as a research article in the
\emph{Journal of Mathematical Economics}. The paper is single-authored and original, and it
is not under consideration elsewhere.

An evaluator who revises by Jeffrey conditioning can reach different beliefs from the same
two impressions read in opposite sequences. The literature has asked whether this dependence
is acceptable. The paper asks instead how it registers in the statistics an observer
measures.

In a two-attribute setting, with the prior covariance $c$ as the believed link between the
attributes, each evaluator's belief departs from a sequence-free benchmark at first order,
$\mathcal{O}(c)$. Averaged over a population, the marginal probabilities and the share of
decisions the sequence changes stay first order, while the believed association and the
surplus-weighted loss fall to second order, $\mathcal{O}(c^{2})$. A smooth statistic of the
average belief is second order exactly when its differential at independence is proportional
to that of the believed association. A belief audit can therefore pass while hiring decisions
still record the sequence.

The paper fits the journal's aim of giving economic ideas a formal mathematical expression.
Its object is an audit question with economic stakes, whether a hiring panel's beliefs and
decisions record the sequence in which it reads a file. Its answers are exact statements of
the order in $c$ at which each statistic departs from the benchmark. The proofs are given in
full in the appendix, and they have also been checked in the Lean~4 proof assistant.

I have no competing interests to declare. The manuscript includes a declaration of the use of
generative AI in its preparation, as the journal's guide requires.
