# Stereotype Rigidity in Social Inferences: A "Mimic Men" Dilemma

**Summary**

Anurag Srivastava · Riskcare Ltd., London · University of Reading

---

Existing models of persistent discrimination — notably Coate and Loury (1993) — derive self-confirming stereotypes from strategic feedback under Bayesian employers on the labour-market contracting channel. Loury's (2002) Du Bois lectures distinguish "discrimination in contract" from "discrimination in contact"; his formal work with Coate addresses the contracting channel. This paper shows that none of the contracting-channel machinery is needed: without discriminatory preferences or strategic interaction through the labour market, soft-signal processing in everyday social contact alone generates a self-sustaining closure trap. The conditions supposed to dissolve discrimination — integration, social mixing, removal of formal barriers — are precisely the conditions that expose markers to the updating mechanism that sustains it.

When people encounter someone new, they read soft markers — accent, dress, phenotype, consumption patterns. These markers are outputs of a slow-moving social system (cultural transmission, residential geography, historical experience) that do not update when an individual's economic class changes. A marker's evidential channel runs through group membership: it shifts credence across groups, and class inference follows from group-level priors held fixed. This is the evidential structure for which Jeffrey conditioning is the unique coherent updating rule. Under it, a high-class member of a stigmatised group is reclassified as an exception while the group stereotype stays put. Under standard Bayes, the same counter-example would revise the group prior upward and stereotypes would erode over time.

The mechanism turns on two governing parameters — marker salience and the group prior — which enter at different points and cannot substitute for one another. The model establishes a mobility threshold, increasing in marker salience, above which the trap is eliminated through a saddle-node bifurcation. The closure trap exists even in a racially homogeneous society; racial discrimination enters as a perturbation. A second source of persistence operates at the institutional level: profit-maximising platforms reproduce discriminatory compositions through the same Jeffrey mechanism at scale. A third result is normative: the welfare loss the trap generates contains an informational component that no policy can remove without altering the soft-signal character of social interaction itself — an irreducibility that distinguishes the contact channel from the contracting channel normatively, not merely descriptively. At the Bayesian limit, the model recovers the standard statistical-discrimination benchmark (Phelps 1972, Arrow 1973); the closure trap is the contact-channel counterpart of Coate and Loury's patronising equilibrium, generated by Jeffrey rigidity in the updating rule rather than by the belief–investment feedback of their model.
