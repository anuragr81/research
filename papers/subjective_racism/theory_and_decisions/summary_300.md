# Discrimination from Soft Signals: Dilemmas of Mimic Men

**Summary**

Anurag Srivastava · Riskcare Ltd., London · University of Reading

---

Existing formal models of self-confirming stereotypes — notably Coate and Loury (1993) — derive persistent discrimination from strategic belief–investment feedback under Bayesian employers. Loury (2002) identifies a broader conceptual programme, distinguishing "discrimination in contract" from "discrimination in contact," but does not formalise the inferential mechanism through which contact-channel discrimination persists. This paper provides that microfoundation.

When people encounter someone new, they read soft signals — accent, dress, phenotype, consumption patterns. These markers are outputs of a slow-moving social system (cultural transmission, residential geography, historical experience) that do not update when an individual's economic class changes. The observer cares about the other's economic class, not their group — group enters only as information about class. But a soft marker carries no likelihood linking it to class: it merely shifts probability across groups. With no likelihood there is nothing for standard Bayes to condition on; writing group directly into preferences would assume the discrimination one is trying to explain. What the marker delivers is a shift in credence over groups with the group-level class priors held fixed — the exact evidential form for which Jeffrey conditioning is the unique coherent updating rule. Under it, a high-class member of a stigmatised group is reclassified as an exception while the group stereotype stays put.

The mechanism turns on two governing parameters — marker salience and the group prior — which enter at different points and cannot substitute for one another. Jeffrey updating generates a self-sustaining closure trap as a stable equilibrium even in a racially homogeneous society. A second, independent source of persistence operates at the institutional level: profit-maximising platforms reproduce discriminatory compositions through the same Jeffrey mechanism at institutional scale. The model establishes a mobility threshold γ*, increasing in marker salience, above which the trap is eliminated through a saddle-node bifurcation. At the Bayesian limit the model recovers the standard statistical-discrimination benchmark (Phelps 1972, Arrow 1973); the closure trap is the formal analogue of Coate and Loury's patronising equilibrium, generated by Jeffrey rigidity rather than strategic feedback.
