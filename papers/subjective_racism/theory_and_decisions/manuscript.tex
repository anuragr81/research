\small\noindent\textbf{Abstract.}
Social boundaries persist despite counter-examples, legal
equality, and anti-discrimination instruments. Existing models of
persistent discrimination, notably \citet{CoateLoury1993}, derive
self-confirming stereotypes from strategic feedback on the
labour-market contracting channel. This paper shows that none of
that machinery is needed: without discriminatory preferences or
strategic interaction through the labour market, soft-signal
processing in everyday social contact alone generates a
self-sustaining closure trap. When people read soft
markers---accent, dress, phenotype---the
marker shifts credence across groups while leaving group-level
class priors fixed; this is the evidential structure for which
Jeffrey conditioning, not Bayes, is the unique coherent updating
rule. Under it, counter-examples are absorbed as exceptions and the
stereotype is untouched, whereas Bayes would erode it. The
mechanism turns on two governing parameters---marker salience and
the group prior (the sediment of economic history)---which enter at
different points and cannot substitute for one another. Class-based
closure exists even in a racially homogeneous society, with racial
discrimination a perturbation; there is a mobility threshold,
increasing in marker salience, above which the trap is eliminated
through a saddle-node bifurcation. A second source operates
institutionally, where profit-maximising platforms reproduce
discriminatory compositions through the same mechanism. The
apparatus generalises beyond classical discrimination: the same
comparative statics apply to contemporary algorithmic environments
where platform-minted markers (verification, visibility, content
classification) replace cultural ones.
At the standard-Bayesian limit the model recovers the
statistical-discrimination benchmark \citep{Phelps1972, Arrow1973};
the closure trap is the contact-channel counterpart of Coate and
Loury's patronising equilibrium, generated by Jeffrey rigidity
rather than strategic feedback.\\[0.5em]

\noindent\textbf{JEL codes:} D83, J15, J71, D62, Z13.\\
\textbf{Keywords:} Jeffrey conditioning, soft markers,
discrimination in contact, closure equilibrium, marker salience,
intergenerational mobility.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Social divisions persist in modern society. Recent debates on immigration and integration leave little doubt that the social boundaries have outlived the laws meant to dissolve them. While formal equality has removed several barriers to integration, social divisions continue to constrain opportunity. Conditional on parental income, for instance, the Black–white earnings gap in the United States has barely narrowed across generations \citep{Chetty2020}. A prominent framework to understand such a persistence -- discrimination in particular -- is offered by \citet{Loury2002}
who distinguishes ``discrimination in contract'' (formal,
labour-market) from ``discrimination in contact'' (informal,
social) -- arguing that the latter sustains inequality even after
the former is removed. However, the contracting channel -- the feedback on which creates self-confirming stereotypes  -- remains the more tractable method in economics to explain social division. The tractability reflects a fundamental asymmetry between contracting and contact channels - in that the former certifies its outcomes---loans repaid, hires assessed, etc. ---while the latter certifies none.
The presented paper considers the updating of the contact channel a structural matter rather than one of taste -- demonstrating how the persistence of social differences follows from contact channel alone and poses limits to corrective policies. The paper shows that social inferences in a general soft-markers environment generate a self-sustaining closure trap that depends neither on discriminatory preferences nor on strategic labour market interactions.

The direction of current work is motivated by two features of soft discrimination in the contact channel that often help it escape scrutiny. The first is that its harm is rarely acute -- since it is more often experienced as persistent friction of routine encounters rather than discrete catastrophic events. Held below the threshold of legal redress by formal equality, such behaviour may go unrecorded--- explaining why it is articulated more often in sociology, psychology, and public health than in economics \citep{WilliamsYuJacksonAnderson1997,SueCapodilupoTorinoBucceriHolderNadalEsquilin2007}. The second is that contact discrimination is not easily separable from economic stratification -- since social boundaries do exist in homogeneous societies and lower economic classes often bear the brunt of the worst social discrimination. The current paper considers the economics of statistical discrimination as its backbone, but it identifies soft markers -- such as accent, body language, phenotype -- as a mechanism through which social distance is mediated in the contact channel. This mechanism is formulate as an interaction between the economic system and a slower-moving social layer whose state is the group's imputed standing, following \citet{ColeMailathPostlewaite1992}.


While the economic framework remains at the heart of the model, the paper pins down two particular mechanisms through which a social system bears its weight on the economic system via the contact channel. More particularly, the extent to which an economic segregation follows from budgetary mismatches is made explicit in the model. A poor man may walk into a bar or club and engage socially, but he would struggle to sustain those relationships if he cannot afford such visits frequently enough. The two mechanisms through which this social layer bears on the economic system are a marker-based \textbf{social distance} mechanism, governed by salience $s_g$, and a \textbf{belief-updating} mechanism, governed by rigidity $\rho$, for social markers (e.g.\ accent, phenotype, dress). A more general updating rule accounts for the higher relevance for marker on grounds of their direct observability relative to economic class of an individual. In other words, while economic class isn't directly observed in social contact, markers such as phenotype, accent, or dress code are much easier to observe. As we further elaborate, Jeffrey conditioning becomes the more appropriate \emph{updating rule} than standard Bayes for admitting such soft marker evidence. Unlike Bayes rule, Jeffrey conditioning revises beliefs about group membership while leaving the standing imputed to the group fixed -- as a soft marker carries no certifiable information about an individual's class, only about which group they belong to. The class is therefore forecast from the marker rather than observed---and a forecast drawn from the group's standing cannot revise that standing.

A direct consequence of the formulation is that not only do social inferences of marker-based distance demonstrate a closure trap from bilateral interactions, they also extend to a model of institutional aggregation of such biases through market incentives.  The consequence is a rigidity condition under which individual counter-examples are absorbed without revising the group prior. Reading others through the typical case is an ordinary cognitive necessity and costless activity \citep{Allport1954}. The rigidity here is of that same ordinary kind---not the agent's refusal to learn but the absence of anything coherent to learn from. It acquires a cost only where the category is a standing coupled to economic position. For stigmatised groups that condition is the rule, not the exception, and there the failure to revise compounds into a closure trap. In the long-run, the trap can be sustained through iterations across generations, with the drag that soft markers impose on subsequent economic position. This is the very impossibility of becoming a recognised whole through marker adoption---that post-colonial literature often notes. \footnote{One example is V.S.\ Naipaul's novel \emph{The Mimic Men} \citep{Naipaul1967}---which is cast as the memoir of Ralph Singh, a Caribbean politician in London exile, who recounts a life of adopted manners, borrowed gestures, and the suspicion that his preparations were always for a life whose original lay elsewhere.} 

Following \citet{ColeMailathPostlewaite1992}, who partition the economy into a market sector and a non-market sector governed by distinct allocation rules, we base the social contact dynamics on \emph{economic} and \emph{social systems} as two coupled layers, each with its own state and speed. The economic system carries an individual's class---income, wealth, occupation---mobile within a generation at rate $\gamma$. The social system presents itself as an \emph{inertial belief layer}: its state is the standing $\bar{c}_g$ imputed to a group, read from slow-moving markers (cultural transmission, residential geography, historical experience) of salience $s_g$ and revised only with rigidity $\rho$. The closure trap is then a property of neither layer alone but of their speed mismatch---the standing updating too slowly, through high $s_g$ and $\rho$, for mobility $\gamma$ to close the gap. An individual who achieves upward economic mobility
does not thereby shed the markers of their origin group -- the ``mimic men'' predicament.

There are two primary contributions that the paper makes. First, it shows that the
contact channel alone generates a self-sustaining closure trap as
a stable equilibrium outcome, even in a racially homogeneous
society (Proposition~\ref{prop:class-closure}). The mechanism is
governed by a \emph{rigidity parameter} $\rho$, measuring how far
belief updating departs from standard Bayes: at the $\rho \to 0$
limit the model reduces to the standard-Bayesian benchmark of the
statistical-discrimination tradition
\citep{Phelps1972,Arrow1973}, where counter-examples erode the
stereotype; contact-channel discrimination arises only once
rigidity exceeds a critical threshold $\rho > \rho^*$
(Appendix~\ref{app:loury}). The trap is path-dependent and intergenerationally persistent. 

Second, the paper shows the limits of policy targeted towards discrimination. The model derives a
threshold level of intergenerational mobility $\gamma^*$ above which the
closure trap is eliminated through a saddle-node bifurcation. That this threshold rises with marker salience isolates the social system's irreducible contribution. More particularly, two groups with identical economic histories but different marker salience face different closure dynamics, the critical mobility $\tilde{\gamma}^*$ rising with $s_g$ (Corollary~\ref{cor:salience-threshold}). As a result, social discrimination cannot be reduced to economic history alone. Supporting results develop the platform channel---``platform'' here denoting any venue, neighbourhood, school, or institution that
mediates encounters. The paper uses the soft-marker framework to articulate how profit-maximising platforms create tokenism as
a structural outcome (Proposition~\ref{prop:tokenism});
anti-discrimination law constrains the contracting channel but
leaves the contact channel unconstrained, generating a
shadow-information market whose intensity rises with legal
strictness (Proposition~\ref{prop:shadow}).

In what follows, Section~\ref{sec:lit} positions
the model relative to existing theories of discrimination.
Section~\ref{sec:model} develops the model in full: primitives,
Jeffrey conditioning, bilateral updating, the closure equilibrium,
comparative statics, the mobility threshold, and the platform
channel (Section~\ref{sec:platform}) through which profit-maximising
institutions reproduce the trap at scale.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} then discusses a few instances where Jeffrey conditioning framework offers a richer analytical vocabulary for stereotype rigidity. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} summarises the conclusion of the paper. All
proofs are collected in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}; the platform extension
is developed in Appendix~\ref{app:platform}.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:lit}
% =============================================================================

What may now appear to be a post-war optimism is the idea that competitive
markets erode discrimination over time---a result that
\citet{Becker1957} had derived using discrimination as an exogenous taste
parameter, a utility cost of interacting with members of a particular
group. Later approaches---the statistical discrimination tradition, initiated by
\citet{Phelps1972}---qualify this optimism with models for employers who use group membership as a
signal about worker productivity under costly individual information.
\citet{Arrow1973} went further to show that statistical discrimination can sustain
multiple equilibria, including a discriminatory steady state in which
group-level beliefs persist. \citet{CoateLoury1993} formalise this
self-confirming mechanism: if workers from a stigmatised group face
lower returns to skill investment because of employer beliefs, the
resulting underinvestment confirms the prior.

A different route is taken by \citet{AkerlofKranton2000}, introducing
identity as a direct argument in the utility function; discrimination
in their framework arises from preferences rather than inference.
\citet{BordaloEtal2016} model stereotypes through representativeness
heuristics, generating belief distortions as a cognitive limitation.
The mechanism we use in the current paper is more aligned with the
\citet{ColeMailathPostlewaite1992} approach, where identities never
enter the utility function directly. Where our model departs from \citet{ColeMailathPostlewaite1992}
significantly is in the mechanism it uses to update the agent's
inferences. Alternatives to Bayesian updating are common in the
literature; the several variants either modify the agent's preferences
or impose constraints on the agent's cognitive architecture.

On the preference side, Bayesian rationality has been generalised
through multiple priors with maxmin evaluation
\citep{GilboaSchmeidler1989}, subjective second-order uncertainty
over priors with separable ambiguity attitude
\citep{KlibanoffMarinacciMukerji2005}, or convergence of Bayesian
updating within a misspecified subjective model
\citep{EspondaPouzo2016}. In each case the locus of departure is
either the agent's preferences or the agent's model of the
data-generating process. Our model holds preferences standard; the
evidence itself carries the structural feature that necessitates
Jeffrey rather than Bayesian conditioning. The tendency to anchor inference on the
typical case is independently documented outside
economics---categorisation as a cognitive necessity
\citep{Allport1954}, typification in everyday interaction
\citep{BergerLuckmann1966}, and the schematic organisation of
culture \citep{DiMaggio1997}---which we take as backdrop, not
foundation. A related approach is
offered by \citet{BohrenImasRosenberg2019}, who show that Bayesian
agents with misspecified models can sustain persistent discrimination
through biased learning.

On the cognitive-architecture side, \citet{FryerJackson2008} develop a
model in which a fixed number of categories forces
variance-minimising lumping, producing coarse sorting of minority
groups and discrimination without a taste for it.
\citet{Mullainathan2002} develops a complementary coarse-Bayesian
model in which agents are constrained to hold beliefs at a finite set
of posteriors and update by selecting the category most consistent
with the data, generating under-reaction within categories and
over-reaction at category switches, with convergence to standard
Bayesian updating as the number of categories grows. While such models
locate the departure from full Bayesian updating in features of the
agent, the approach taken in the current paper recognises that soft
signals in social interaction carry no certifiable evidence about an
individual's class, only about which group they belong to.

The updating rule used in this paper is based on Jeffrey
conditioning, drawing on the subjective probability literature
\citep{Jeffrey1965,Jeffrey2004}. Rather than as a consequence of
agent-level deviations from full rationality, categorical
discrimination emerges in our framework as a property of rational
probability revision under soft evidence\footnote{A complementary
distinction can be drawn against cumulative prospect theory
\citep{TverskyKahneman1992}. In the taxonomy of
\citet{Schoemaker1982}, our agents are expected-utility maximisers
with Jeffrey-revised probabilities, not prospect-theoretic agents who
apply decision weights to outcomes. The Jeffrey revision produces
\emph{new} probabilities $P'(g_k)$ that continue to satisfy the
Kolmogorov axioms in response to soft evidence, whereas CPT decision
weights $w(p)$ are cognitive distortions of probabilities that do not
generally satisfy them.}.
The family of models closest in spirit to ours is the
information-economics tradition of signalling, first articulated by
\citet{Spence1973}: if marginalised individuals credibly signal their
type, the pooling equilibrium breaks down and discrimination erodes.
However, \citet{Spence1973} rests on Bayesian updating over hard
signals---a university degree, a certification, a warranty---where the
signal partitions the type space and the observer's posterior moves
with each observation. Applied to social discrimination, it implies
that marginalised communities should be able to erode group priors by
signalling their true economic quality more assertively. This is the
implicit logic of the self-confirming equilibria in
\citet{CoateLoury1993} as well, who show that affirmative action can
sustain ``patronising equilibria'' in which the policy itself lowers
the incentive for the targeted group to invest in skills; the remedy
is to shift beliefs so that the signalling equilibrium unlocks. Our
model identifies precisely why this does not transfer to the social
domain. In \citet{Spence1973} (as well as in \citet{Akerlof1970}),
the signal is hard: it determines a definite evidential relationship
with the type, and Bayesian updating on hard signals aggregates
cleanly from individuals to the group prior. In social interaction,
the operative signals are soft---accent, dress, phenotype---and the theoretically appropriate rule for soft signals is
Jeffrey conditioning rather than Bayesian updating. Here, the rigidity condition
(Remark~\ref{rem:graded}) decouples individual reclassification
from group prior revision: the compliant individual is reclassified,
the group prior $\bar{c}_g$ does not move. Further, no volume of individual
signalling can drive $\bar{c}_g$ across the tipping point
$\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$. Our model provides a
different---and complementary---justification for affirmative action:
temporary forced integration shifts the system from the
high-discrimination to the low-discrimination equilibrium, after
which the policy is no longer needed
(Proposition~\ref{prop:class-closure}).

The current paper answers the question of persistence by inspecting
the contact channel. Other approaches, such as
\citet{ChaudhuriSethi2008}, introduce peer effects into a
Coate--Loury-type framework and ask, in effect, our
question---whether integration eliminates negative
stereotypes---deriving a threshold level of segregation below which
negative stereotypes become unsustainable. The answer provided by
\citet{ChaudhuriSethi2008} is that integration can eliminate the
discriminatory equilibrium, but with ambiguous welfare consequences:
depending on the curvature of peer effects, integration may raise or
lower skill levels in both groups. \citet{BowlesLourySethi2014} have
also shown that segregation in social networks combined with peer
effects can sustain group inequality in skill shares indefinitely even
without labour market discrimination. While these results operate
through the contracting channel (labour markets, human capital
investment, employer screening), the current model attempts to understand how soft
markers themselves may create a trap despite integration
dissolving the contracting-channel stereotype. In
Appendix~\ref{app:loury} we embed the mechanism in a one-parameter
family indexed by the rigidity parameter $\rho$. At $\rho = 0$
(standard Bayes), individual counter-examples fully update the group
prior and the closure trap disappears---the standard-Bayesian
benchmark of the statistical-discrimination tradition
\citep{Phelps1972,Arrow1973}. The closure trap requires
$\rho > \rho^*$; it is the contact-channel counterpart of the
patronising equilibrium of \citet{CoateLoury1993}, generated here by
Jeffrey rigidity in the updating rule rather than by the
belief--investment feedback of their model.

The Jeffrey conditioning follows from the
two-system structure of the social environment
(Section~\ref{sec:model}): markers are outputs of a slow-moving
social system and carry no likelihood function linking them to
current economic class, so the standard Bayesian presupposition of a
sharp evidence event fails. This approach allows the paper to approach discrimination in a more general setting from a consumption and
industrial organisation perspective, rather than from the identity
and self-perception channel. The question we ask is not only how
discrimination affects the self-image, aspiration, or social identity
of those discriminated against, but how business incentives interact with discrimination as a
market equilibrium phenomenon in an environment of soft market inferences. We find that while market power does generate countervailing power on the other side, the risk that this power deposits the group in a tokenism zone is real. More particularly, while corporate interests partially do align with the inclusion of marginalised communities when the stigmatised group's aggregate purchasing power grows \citep{Galbraith1952}, the exclusion threshold on the group prior,
$\bar{c}_g^{\text{excl}}$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:exclusion}) means that the platform's profit motive eventually generates voluntary partial integration.

The key insight from the model is that none of the contracting-channel machinery---employers,
human capital investment, strategic signalling---may be necessary to generate
persistent social boundaries -- an implication that may explain the reasons for their ubiquity. The model develops this claim formally, proving that the resulting closure trap is a stable equilibrium governed by a rigidity parameter~$\rho$ that nests the
standard-Bayesian benchmark as a limiting case. Section~\ref{sec:platform} further elaborates how profit-maximising platforms may reproduce and institutionalise the trap at scale.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}
% =============================================================================

We first define the model primitives in Section \ref{sec:model_primitives}. This includes a
continuum of agents characterised by economic class, observable soft
markers, and group membership. The engagement decision governed by a utility that in turn depends on economic class distance then follows in Section \ref{subsec:bilateral}. The equilibrium dynamics and implications for intergenerational mobility are discussed in Section \ref{subsec:dynamics}. Finally, Section \ref{sec:platform} discusses how platforms ---a venue,
neighbourhood, or institutions that mediate encounters---can reproduce the trap at
scale.


\subsection{Primitives}
\label{sec:model_primitives}
Consider a continuum of agents indexed by $i$ and $j$. When two
agents encounter each other, each agent's soft markers place that
agent within the group partition as the other reads them, and the
prior attached to the placement supplies the other's expected economic
class. Engagement is decided bilaterally on these expected class
distances, not on any observed class. What drives this engagement is economic compatibility: gender signals,
cultural indicators, racial markers, and other soft cues enter the engagement decision
only insofar as they are informative about economic class through the
group prior. This does not claim that agents consciously reason about
income - only that the latent variable mediating social
engagement is economic class, and soft markers are its observable
correlates.

Each agent is characterised by:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
  \item Economic class $c_i \in \C = [0,1]$, where $c = 0$ is the
  minimum and $c = 1$ the maximum. Determines consumption bundle and
  is the latent variable underlying the engagement decision.
  
  \item Social marker $m_i \in \M$, an observable characteristic
  (accent, dress, name). Correlated with $c_i$
  but does not determine it. This is a \emph{soft signal}.
  
  \item Group membership $g_i \in \G = \{g_1, \ldots, g_K\}$.
  Group membership may be directly observable (race, visible gender)
  or latent and inferred from markers (class, ethnicity, religion).
  In either case what matters for the model is not group membership
  itself but the economic class distribution conditional on group
  membership, which is always a soft inference.
\end{itemize}

The formal distinction between these primitives reflects the
two-system structure introduced in Section~\ref{sec:intro}. Economic
class $c_i$ lives in the economic system: it is mobile within a
generation and in principle responsive to individual effort, investment,
and opportunity. Social markers $m_i$ are outputs of the social
system: they are generated by group membership $g_i$ via a likelihood
$P(m \mid g_k)$---that is, by the social system's slow-moving
inheritance through cultural transmission, residential geography,
and accumulated historical experience
\citep[population-level persistence:][]{BisinVerdier2001}---and they
do not update when an individual's economic class changes
\citep[individual-level durability:][]{Bourdieu1984}. An agent who achieves $c_i = 1$ from a group whose historical mean class
$\bar{c}_{g_k}$ is near zero retains the markers of that group---the
``mimic man'' of the title. It is this decoupling that makes the
marker a soft signal rather than a hard one. Where $m_i$ is driven
far more by $g_i$ than by $c_i$, even a marker-to-class likelihood
that exists is dominated by the marker-to-group channel; Jeffrey
conditioning over the group partition is the rule that respects this
relative strength (Section~\ref{subsec:jeffrey}).

\begin{assumption}[Timescale separation]
\label{ass:timescale}
The two systems evolve on separated timescales. Within an
encounter---the interval over which a soft marker is read and a single
engagement is decided---the group standing $\bar{c}_g$ and the
within-group conditionals $F_g$ are fixed; they evolve only across
generations, through the law of motion of
Section~\ref{subsec:dynamics}. Economic class is mobile within a
generation at rate $\gamma$; the social substrate is not. The closure
trap is a property of this mismatch---the standing updating too
slowly, through high $s_g$ and $\rho$, for mobility $\gamma$ to close
the gap.
\end{assumption}

The framework of statistical discrimination in an economic system is interpreted in the model by admitting the constraints on social engagement through economic distance. The simple illustration of this problem is that while a poor man may walk into a bar or club and engage socially with richer members, he may struggle to sustain those relationships if he cannot afford such visits frequently enough. More formally, two agents in the model are \textbf{economically compatible} if
$d(c_i, c_j) \leq \bar{d}$, where $d(c_i, c_j) = (c_i - c_j)^2$ is
squared economic class distance and $\bar{d}$ is the compatibility threshold.
Squared distance decomposes expected
incompatibility into a bias term (distance between group means) and
a variance term (within-group heterogeneity).

Agent $j$'s prior over the economic class of a member of group $g$:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{c}_g = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} c_{g,t}
  \label{eq:prior}
\end{equation}
where $c_{g,t} \in [0,1]$ is the class outcome of the $t$-th
historical member of group $g$ and $T$ is the historical window.
The group mean $\bar{c}_g$ is a \textbf{sufficient
statistic of the group's economic history}. Groups with histories of
marginalisation---slavery, colonial extraction, persistent
poverty---have low $\bar{c}_g$.

As we ultimately wish to explore implications of the above exclusion mechanism on intergenerational mobility, we consider how the two systems couple across a
generation. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:loop} , the economic system sets the group prior while the social system produces markers correlated with that prior. A discrimination rate follows from the soft maker environment -- and is determined  Jeffrey conditioning. The economic cost of exclusion, $\delta$, feeds this back into the
group's economic position and the resulting prior is what the next
generation's markers are read against. Therefore, the two systems interact at
two points: the economic system fixes the \emph{meaning} of markers,
and the social system erodes the \emph{position} of the group.

\subsection{Social engagement and the closure equilibrium}
\label{subsec:jeffrey}
\label{subsec:bilateral}
\label{subsec:interaction}
\label{sec:closure}

Jeffrey conditioning generalises Bayesian conditionalisation to evidence
that does not admit a likelihood function over the partition of interest
\citep{Jeffrey1965,Jeffrey2004}. The standard Bayesian update presupposes
a sharp evidence event $E$ with conditional probabilities $P(E \mid g_k)$
defined for each cell of the partition, yielding
$P(g_k \mid E) \propto P(E \mid g_k) P(g_k)$. On other hand, Jeffrey conditioning is more appropriate when the agent's evidence is a passage of experience that cannot be reduced to a proposition 
with belief certainty---Jeffrey's founding example is observation of a coloured
cloth under poor light, leaving the observer with revised degrees of
belief over its possible colours but no proposition $E$ they have come
to believe with certainty \citep[\S 11.1]{Jeffrey1965}. The appropriate extension proposed by 
\citet{Jeffrey1965} is the following probability kinematics:
\begin{equation}
  P'(H) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} P(H \mid g_k) \cdot P'(g_k)
  \label{eq:kinematics}
\end{equation}
for any hypothesis $H$ outside the partition $\{g_1, \ldots, g_K\}$,
where the post-experience partition weights $P'(g_k)$ are produced
directly by the uncertain experience. The formula derives from the law
of total probability applied to the posterior, under the
\emph{invariance condition} $P'(H \mid g_k) = P(H \mid g_k)$ for all $k$
and $H$ \citep[\S\S 11.3, 11.6]{Jeffrey1965}.  The rule has an axiomatic foundation in \citet{DiaconisZabell1982}: holding the within-group conditionals fixed under soft evidence is not an approximation but what coherence requires, since revising them on a class reading that was itself forecast from those same conditionals would double-count the evidence. We use the property of conditional probabilities being preserved  given each partition cell across the revision -- to contrast the difference between group-identification and marker-inference. The standard Bayesian conditionalisation then becomes the limiting case in which the partition weights concentrate on a single cell \citep[\S 11.5]{Jeffrey1965}. 

The hypothesis of interest in our setting is class $c$ and the partition
is group membership $\{g_1, \ldots, g_K\}$. The within-group
conditional distributions $F_{g_k} = P(c \mid g_k)$ play the role of the
invariant conditionals, and the kinematics formula specialises as
follows. Agent $j$ observes soft marker $m_i$. This shifts $j$'s
distribution over group membership from $P(g_k)$ to $P'(g_k)$ without
determining which group $i$ belongs to.

\begin{assumption}[Group sufficiency]
\label{ass:sufficiency}
Conditional on group, the soft marker is uninformative about class:
$c \perp m \mid g$, equivalently $P(c \mid m, g_k) = P(c \mid g_k) =
F_{g_k}$ for every $k$. The marker's entire bearing on class runs
through the group partition; it carries no likelihood $P(m \mid c)$ of
its own. A marker that did---one whose realisation shifts the class
posterior \emph{within} a group---is to that extent a hard signal, and
falls outside the soft-marker regime treated here.
\end{assumption}

\noindent Under
\textbf{Jeffrey conditioning} we have:
\begin{equation}
  P'(c) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} F_{g_k} \cdot P'(g_k)
  \label{eq:jeffrey}
\end{equation}

Given that we consider the incompatibility of economic classes, what the engagement decision needs is the partner's class, not their group \emph{per se}: engagement turns on class distance (Section~\ref{sec:model_primitives}),
so the marker must be converted into a posterior over class, $P'(c)$.
Constrasting Bayes and Jeffrey again, consider that both rules reach $P'(c)$ by the same two-step route---marker $\to$ group weights $P'(g_k) \to$ class $P'(c)$---and they share the second
step, equation~(\ref{eq:jeffrey}), which carries the group weights into
a class distribution through the conditionals $F_{g_k}$ (it takes this
form because of Assumption~\ref{ass:sufficiency}: conditional on group,
the marker says nothing further about class). They differ only at the \emph{first} step. Bayes
\emph{computes} the group weights from a likelihood,
$P'(g_k) = P(m \mid g_k)\,P(g_k)/P(m)$, so its full path to the class
posterior is $P'(c) = \sum_k F_{g_k}\,P(m \mid g_k)P(g_k)/P(m)$ -- depending on the likelihood $P(m \mid g_k)$. On the other hand, Jeffrey computes
nothing at the first step: it \emph{takes $P'(g_k)$ as given}---the
revised group credence is the experience's direct deliverance received as input, entering the model as the salience
parameter $s_g$ -- and then feeds those weights through the second step it shares with Bayes in order to obtain the class posterior $P'(c)$. The Jeffrey rule essentially shifts focus to the inferences from markers instead of on the economic distributions for groups -- which are far more difficult to observe and less relevant than social inference from observable markers (such as accent, clothing styles or skin-colour).


Put differently, the divergence between Bayes and Jeffrey appears only when evidence bears on class directly: a counter-example leads Bayes to revise the conditionals $F_{g_k}$, whereas Jeffrey, by
the invariance condition, holds them fixed. An accent, an item of dress, a phenotype gives
Bayes nothing to compute $P'(g_k)$ from -- whereas Jeffrey's acceptance of
a directly given shift lets the inference proceed.\footnote{$F_{g_k}$
is only a formal representation of the observer's expectations
about a typical member of group $g_k$ -- not something the observer
must reason about in making a decision as such.} The two rules thus differ in the channel through which
information reaches belief. Bayes routes evidence through a likelihood ratio
whereas Jeffrey routes evidence through belief structure implied by partition weights. Regardless of the experience, $F_{g_k}$ does not move under Jeffrey conditioning while $P'(g_k)$ can. Bayes revises the stereotype, whereas Jeffrey, holding the
conditionals fixed, processes the same fact as a reclassification -- so the incoming information in a soft marker dominated environment only changes which group a person is
assigned to, rather than the standing imputed to the group. Simply put, Bayes relies on $P'(m|g)$ but Jeffrey must use $P'(g|m)$ (with fixed $F_{g_k}$)  Jeffrey conditioning becomes more appropriate for the soft marker environment because $F_{g_k}$, the long-term substrate from the social system, is fixed relative to the belief-adjustments that follow from markers in shorter-term. A numerical illustration is provided in Example \ref{ex:jeffrey-bayes}. A more real-world instance is discussed in Example \ref{ex:misaddressed}.

Above fixes the model's domain. Three conditions must hold jointly for
marker-judgment to govern rather than stereotype-adjustment. First, the
partner's class must be forecast from the marker rather than certified. Second, the group
membership must be a soft, re-attributable inference, so that a
surprising or unconventional individual can be reassigned. If group membership were certified rather than inferred, a high-class member could not be reassigned out of the group,
and the counter-example would bear on $F_{g_k}$ after all.  Third, the conditional $F_{g_k}$ should be allowed to be anchored as the slow-moving social substrate. Where any of
the three fails---class is certified (Assumption~\ref{ass:sufficiency}
breaks), membership is fixed, or the marker
moves as fast as class itself---the counter-example has nowhere to land
but the conditional, and belief reverts to the Bayesian branch in which
stereotypes adjust.

\begin{example}[Jeffrey versus Bayes example]
\label{ex:jeffrey-bayes}
An observer meets a stranger at a professional gathering, with prior
credence split evenly between two groups, $P(g_A) = P(g_B) = 0.5$. The
observer's group priors---the group-level probabilities of high economic
class---are $\bar{c}_{g_A} = 0.7$ and $\bar{c}_{g_B} = 0.3$. Before any
signal, the expected probability that the stranger is high-class is
$0.5 \times 0.7 + 0.5 \times 0.3 = 0.50$.

The stranger speaks, and a soft marker (accent) shifts the observer's
group credence to $P'(g_A) = 0.2$, $P'(g_B) = 0.8$. The marker carries
no likelihood $P(m \mid \text{class})$: it is informative about group,
not directly about class.\footnote{Markers do correlate with class in
aggregate; as established in Section~\ref{sec:model_primitives}, that
correlation runs through group membership (the chain $P(m \mid g)\cdot
P(c \mid g)$) rather than a primitive $P(m \mid c)$ channel.} Jeffrey conditioning therefore holds the group
priors $\bar{c}_{g_k}$ fixed and revises only the group weights, giving
an updated class expectation of $0.2 \times 0.7 + 0.8 \times 0.3 = 0.38$.
The stranger's inferred standing falls from $0.50$ to $0.38$---not
because the accent revealed anything about this individual's income, but
because it reweighted the groups whose priors the observer already held.

Now suppose the stranger is in fact high-class. Under standard Bayesian updating,
observing a high-class member of $g_B$ raises the posterior
$\bar{c}_{g_B}$ above $0.3$; repeated such encounters drive it toward its
true value and the stereotype erodes.  Under Jeffrey
conditioning, however, the observer just reclassifies the individual---attributing them
to $g_A$ and treating them as an exception within $g_B$---while
$\bar{c}_{g_B}$ remain unchanged. The next member of $g_B$ is met with the
same prior $0.3$ -- presumably set over a much longer time.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[The misaddressed parcel]
\label{ex:misaddressed}
A delivery agent calls at a house in a predominantly majority
neighbourhood. The resident's appearance reads, unambiguously, as
minority ($g_B$); the name on the parcel reads as majority ($g_A$). The
agent must settle one thing: does the name belong to this
resident---deliver---or is the parcel misaddressed? On a rushed round the
call is made at the doorstep in seconds, with only the label and the face
to go on: no document to check, no recourse.

Two soft signals point in opposite directions. The appearance fixes the
group attribution; the name is anomalous under the group-level
association $F_{g_B}$, which places almost no weight on majority names
within $g_B$. The surprise must be absorbed somewhere---but the
appearance marker is \emph{hard}: it cannot be re-read, so the agent
cannot resolve the tension by attributing the resident to $g_A$.

A Bayesian agent treats the anomalous name as evidence bearing on the
association itself. Unable to move the group attribution, she revises
$F_{g_B}$---minority residents carry majority names more often than she
had assumed, through marriage, conversion, or naturalisation---judges
that the name plausibly belongs, and delivers.\footnote{The comparison
turns on $P(\text{majority name}\mid g_B)$: frozen near zero it sends all
weight to ``misaddressed''; revised upward it lets ``belongs'' compete.}

A Jeffrey agent holds $F_{g_B}$ fixed by construction. With the group
attribution pinned by the hard marker and the association frozen, a
majority name on a $g_B$ resident is all but impossible \emph{if the name
belongs}---so the coherent reading is that it does not: the parcel is
misaddressed, or the resident is filed as a lone exception. This is not a
reassignment of the resident to $g_A$, which the hard marker forbids; it
is the individual set aside as atypical, with the group association left
exactly as it was. The next such parcel is met with the same $F_{g_B}$ as
the last.
\end{example}

The parcel case is the difficulty of social inferences in a miniature form -- in that we do not claim to
identify the mechanism behind any given decision i.e. which one of inference,
rigidity, or taste produced it -- something that choice does not often reveal
\citep{Sen1973}. The example also fixes the scope of the mechanism. Stripped to
inference alone---one encounter, no institution and no antagonism---the
trap still forms, in a single rational inference. Institutions enter
later, through aggregation: the platform of Section~\ref{sec:platform}
scales the same reading---a distinct channel of persistence that still
rests on the rigidity the parcel isolates
(Remark~\ref{rem:platform-bayes}). The history institutions carry is
present too, but as the value of the group prior $\bar{c}_g$---the
sediment of past economic position---not as a force acting within the
encounter. What lies outside the model is the case where an outsider's
success provokes resentment or coalitional threat: there the response is
a contest over standing rather than an inference from it.

\rule{15cm}{0.4pt}   

As the Example \ref{ex:jeffrey-bayes} illustrates, $P(g_k)$ is the observer's credence that the encountered individual
belongs to group $g_k$. Before a marker is observed, this credence
may be vague---no group attribution yet formed---or may already be
tilted by context such as the setting or prior information. Upon
observation, the marker reweights this credence: an accent
identified with $g_k$ raises $P'(g_k)$ and lowers the others; a
phenotype associated with $g_j$ does the opposite. The shift from
$P(g_k)$ to $P'(g_k)$ is the immediate inferential act of placing
the individual into a group. It is fast, it is discrete, and it can
move sharply on a single observation, because the marker is
precisely the kind of evidence that carries direct information
about group assignment. 


To reiterate, the reason why the paper argues for separation of marker inference from group assignment as different channels is that the social system and the economic system operate at different
speeds. A marker is read in seconds and shifts $P(g_k)$ immediately;
$F_{g_k}$ updates through a different channel---the accumulated
statistical experience and social knowledge that does not arrive
with any single marker observation. The inferential asymmetry has a
particular shape: the slow-moving cultural substrate, whose stable
outputs are legible at speed, is quickly inferred from at the
encounter, while the fast-moving economic position is processed
through slower, indirect channels. The end-result is that the social encounter performs a reclassification, not a
revision of the stereotype. Whether persistent group-level
stereotypes obtain depends on how $F_g$ evolves between encounters
under the intergenerational dynamics of
Section~\ref{subsec:dynamics}---through mobility, the discrimination
externality, and marker decay.

The discrimination arises from this asymmetry of processing, and it does
so whether or not the group's standing is accurate. The only thing an
encounter could revise is $\bar{c}_{g_k}$, the standing imputed to the
group; but the evidence it delivers is a marker, from which the
individual's class is forecast through that very standing. A forecast
drawn from the standing cannot then revise it without double-counting,
and the contact channel supplies no independently observed class outcome
that could. A high-class member of a low-standing group is therefore
reassigned---$P'(g_k)$ shifts: they are mentally placed in a different
group, or treated as an exception---while $\bar{c}_{g_k}$ is left
untouched. This is not a distortion that fails to self-correct. Instead, it is holding
the standing fixed that is a coherent response to evidence that speaks to
group membership and not to group standing.



\textbf{ Social Distance-based Utility} The above bilateral, reflexive information structure is updated simultaneously by two interacting agents -- each forming a Jeffrey-revised assessment of the other's class from the observed markers. The bilateral Jeffrey structure leads to two consequences immediately. First, because the Jeffrey revision
(\ref{eq:jeffrey}) is driven by the partition weights $P'(g_k)$ and
these are produced by observed markers, agent $i$ has incentive to
manipulate markers to shift $P'(g_k)$ in $j$'s revision---the social
analogue of \citet{Spence1973}'s signalling model
(\emph{endogenous signal production}). Second, the rigidity keeps the group standing $\bar{c}_g$ fixed
whatever a single encounter shows. Since no encounter delivers class
evidence independent of the marker, excluded agents learn less about
economic opportunities than they otherwise would. The closure trap
(Section~\ref{subsec:intergenerational}) is therefore also an
information trap \footnote{The same structure carries a self-directed reading---encounters signalling exclusion can also depress an agent's own aspirations---though the model does not track this as a separate channel.}. 

The rigidity condition operates at the inference moment, not across
time (Assumption~\ref{ass:timescale}). The within-group distributions $F_g$ are held fixed at the
encounter---the timescale on which a soft marker is read and
processed---but they are not eternal. The within-group distributions are set to evolve over generations
through the intergenerational dynamic of
Section~\ref{subsec:dynamics}, where mobility, the discrimination
externality, and marker decay shift $\bar{c}_g^t$ over time. The
closure trap arises precisely when these slower forces balance to
keep $\bar{c}_g$ below a tipping point. Above that point, the same
forces drive the system to a high attractor and the marker-borne
stereotype dissolves.

We now define the 
utility function that defines the engagement decision where class distance acts as a friction:
\begin{equation}
  U(c_i, c_j) = v - \kappa \cdot d(c_i, c_j)
  \label{eq:utility}
\end{equation}
where $v > 0$ is baseline interaction value, $\kappa > 0$ is
incompatibility sensitivity, and $d(\cdot)$ is the class distance.
The baseline value $v$ is the payoff from engaging with an agent of
identical class ($d(c_i, c_j) = 0$) . The baseline represents the intrinsic
benefit of social contact when the interaction partner is perfectly
compatible, and is reduced linearly by class distance through the
sensitivity parameter $\kappa$.
The agent's outside option $\bar{u}$ is the reservation payoff from
declining to engage---the utility available from remaining within an
existing social network rather than interacting with the encountered
individual. 

Notice that the utility depends on class distance
alone -- group membership $g$ does not enter as an argument of $U$. In other words, group enters only indirectly through the marker: the observed marker reweights
the group partition (Jeffrey-updated as in~(\ref{eq:jeffrey})), and the
prior it activates fixes the expected $c_i$ the distance is computed
against. Equation~(\ref{eq:utility}) is therefore a consequence
function, not a decision rule: the world realises the distance
$d(c_i,c_j)$ from true compatibility---the poor man cannot sustain the
club's relationships however he is read---while the agent, never
observing $c_i$, only acts on the inference from markers.

The expected utility under the Jeffrey-updated forecast can be stated as :
\begin{equation}
  \E[U \mid m_i] = v - \kappa \cdot \E[d(c_i, c_j) \mid m_i]
  \label{eq:exp-utility}
\end{equation}

Agent $j$ engages iff:
\begin{equation}
  \E[d(c_i, c_j) \mid m_i] \leq
  \frac{v - \bar{u}}{\kappa} \equiv \Delta^*(c_j)
  \label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}

Notice that only the net surplus $v - \bar{u}$ enters the decision, through the
cutoff $\Delta^*$. Together with $\kappa$, it is absorbed into the
discrimination-rate function $F$ this threshold induces below.

Under bilateral structure, engagement requires condition
(\ref{eq:threshold}) to hold for both agents simultaneously. The above applies to any number of groups $K$ and any soft marker space.

\begin{assumption}[Preferences]
\label{ass:preferences}
Agents are risk neutral in class distance, with utility
$U = v - \kappa\,d$ and $d(c_i,c_j) = (c_i-c_j)^2$. Variance within a
group therefore enters expected utility at the same weight $\kappa$ as
the squared mean gap, with no separate risk parameter.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Within-group beliefs]
\label{ass:beliefs}
Beliefs about class within a group are Beta--de~Finetti exchangeable
(Appendix~\ref{app:bernoulli}). This gives
$\sigma_g^2 = \bar{c}_g(1-\bar{c}_g)/(1+\nu)$.
\end{assumption}

To develop the closed-form equilibrium dynamics, we consider two groups $\G = \{g_1, g_2\}$ (host and out-group respectively) -- across which the probability mass gets reassigned through Jeffrey revision (\ref{eq:jeffrey}). We parametrise the strength of that reassignment by a scalar $s_g \in [0,1]$, the probability the observer attributes the
encountered individual to the out-group upon observing their
markers:
\begin{equation}
  P'(g_2 \mid m_i) \equiv s_g
  \label{eq:salience}
\end{equation}
\emph{Marker salience} is a property of the \emph{marker--observer
interaction}, not an intrinsic property of the group. A South Asian
individual in Norway has high $s_g$ (phenotypic markers strongly
identify out-group membership) -- but indeed the same individual in South Asia itself
has $s_g \approx 0$. Mutable markers (accent, dress)
can be modified, reducing $s_g$ over time; immutable markers (skin
colour, facial structure) produce permanently high $s_g$. 

Under this parametrisation, the expected class distance for an
agent $j$ with class $c_j$ encountering an individual whose markers
trigger salience $s_g$ decomposes into bias and variance
components. Writing $\sigma_{g_k}^2$ for the within-group variance
of class outcomes in group $g_k$ (defined in
Appendix~\ref{app:bernoulli}):
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  \E[d(c_i, c_j) \mid m_i]
  &= P'(g_2 \mid m_i) \cdot D_2 + P'(g_1 \mid m_i) \cdot D_1 \\
  &= s_g \underbrace{\left[(\bar{c}_{g_2} - c_j)^2 + \sigma_{g_2}^2\right]}_{D_2}
  + (1 - s_g) \underbrace{\left[(\bar{c}_{g_1} - c_j)^2 + \sigma_{g_1}^2\right]}_{D_1}
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:Ed-salience}
\end{equation}

The first line is the standard expected-utility form---a sum of
conditional expected distances weighted by posterior probabilities;
the second uses the definition $s_g \equiv P'(g_2 \mid m_i)$ from
(\ref{eq:salience}) and the binary partition $P'(g_1 \mid m_i) = 1 -
s_g$ to express the same expectation in the marker-salience
shorthand used in the remainder of the paper.
$D_k = (\bar{c}_{g_k} - c_j)^2 + \sigma_{g_k}^2$ is the
expected squared distance under certain attribution to group $g_k$.\footnote{This is the standard bias--variance decomposition of expected squared loss: both the squared gap between $c_j$ and the group mean (bias) and the within-group dispersion (variance) enter the agent's expected utility. Variance enters mechanically rather than as a separate risk-aversion parameter---the agent maximises expected utility under a quadratic distance metric, and $\E[(c_i - c_j)^2 \mid g_k] = (\E[c_i \mid g_k] - c_j)^2 + \mathrm{Var}(c_i \mid g_k)$ by definition. The linear-in-distance utility and quadratic distance metric used in (\ref{eq:Ed-salience}) yield the bias--variance decomposition that supports the closed-form analysis below (see Assumption~\ref{ass:functional} for the scope of these functional-form choices).}
The within-group variance term $\sigma_{g_k}^2$ matters in its own
right: even when the group mean is close to the agent's class,
within-group heterogeneity raises expected class distance and the
probability that the engagement threshold (\ref{eq:threshold}) is
violated.
When $s_g = 1$, the observer attributes the individual to the
out-group with certainty and the expression reduces to:
\begin{equation}
  \E[d(c_i, c_j) \mid g_2]
  = (\bar{c}_{g_2} - c_j)^2 + \sigma_{g_2}^2
  \label{eq:Ed-group}
\end{equation}
which is the workhorse expression of the model. When $s_g = 0$,
the markers are invisible and the observer treats the individual
as a member of the host group: no discrimination occurs regardless
of economic history.

The engagement threshold is violated when either the bias (class
distance between observer and group mean) or the within-group
heterogeneity, weighted by marker salience, is too
large. When the threshold (\ref{eq:threshold}) is violated, agent
$j$ declines to engage with $i$: this is the model's discrimination
event. The discrimination mechanism therefore has two governing
parameters corresponding to the spatial and temporal dimensions of
social observation.\footnote{\label{rem:spacetime}Every soft signal
that enters the model is either a spatial property of the individual
observable at the encounter or encodes temporal information
accumulated across the group's economic history. Consequently,
spatial interventions (reducing marker visibility through assimilation
or ``passing'') and temporal interventions (raising the mobility
parameter $\gamma$ above its bifurcation threshold
$\gamma^*$ through mobility programmes) are categorically different
and cannot substitute for each other.} Marker salience $s_g$ is a
\emph{spatial} property: it is determined by what the observer
perceives at the point of encounter---phenotype, dress, accent, body
language---and governs \emph{whether} the Jeffrey revision fires and
how strongly. The group prior $\bar{c}_g$ is a \emph{temporal}
property: it is the sediment of the group's economic history across
generations and governs \emph{what} the Jeffrey revision maps to
once it fires. The model's central result---that sustained
discrimination requires both channels to be active---can be restated:
sustained discrimination requires both spatial identifiability
($s_g > 0$) and temporal disadvantage ($\bar{c}_g <
\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$).


\subsection{Intergenerational dynamics and the closure equilibrium}
\label{subsec:dynamics}

Bilateral engagement decisions aggregate into a population-level
dynamic through two steps. First, within generation $t$, each
encounter between an in-group agent and a group-$g$ individual
produces engagement or refusal according to (\ref{eq:threshold});
the discrimination rate $\phi^t$ is the population fraction of
such encounters in which the threshold is violated. Second,
$\phi^t$ feeds back into the next generation's group prior
$\bar{c}_g^{t+1}$ through three economic channels: exclusion
(refused engagements deprive group $g$ of class-improving social
contact and erode the prior), natural convergence (an exogenous
intergenerational tendency toward the population mean), and
intergenerational mobility (within-group regression to the mean,
which depends on the current heterogeneity of group outcomes).
Combining these channels, the group prior evolves as:
\begin{equation}
  \bar{c}_g^{t+1} =
  \underbrace{\bar{c}_g^t(1 - \delta \phi^t)}_{\text{exclusion erodes prior}}
  + \underbrace{\mu(1 - \bar{c}_g^t)}_{\text{natural convergence}}
  + \underbrace{\tilde{\gamma}\,\bar{c}_g^t(1-\bar{c}_g^t)}_{\text{mobility from within-group heterogeneity}}
  \label{eq:dynamic}
\end{equation}
where $\delta > 0$ is the economic cost of social exclusion per unit
of discrimination, $\mu \in (0,1)$ is the exogenous intergenerational
convergence rate, and $\gamma \in (0,1)$ is the intergenerational
mobility parameter governing regression to the group mean.

The parameter $\delta$ is a reduced form. Social exclusion translates
into economic erosion through at least three channels that the model
does not separately distinguish: the matching market, where exclusion
from elite social spaces reduces the probability of assortative
pairing with higher-class partners
\citep{ColeMailathPostlewaite1992}; cultural capital, where
participation in social environments builds dispositions---accent,
self-presentation, institutional fluency---with direct economic
returns \citep{Bourdieu1984}; and information networks, where
informal social contact mediates access to economic opportunities
such as job referrals, mentorship, and business formation
\citep{Granovetter1973}. Each channel converts social exclusion into
a drag on intergenerational economic position; $\delta$ captures
their aggregate effect without requiring the model to specify which
dominates. We take $\delta$ as constant across groups and
generations---a single reduced-form intensity, not a group- or
state-dependent one.

Consider as an example how the three channels operate for a group's economic position across generations. First, the
exclusion-erosion term $\bar{c}_g^t(1 - \delta \phi^t)$ carries
forward the parents' position but discounts it by the discrimination
rate---every economic interaction prevented by discrimination
reduces the group's economic position over time. Second, the
natural-convergence term $\mu(1 - \bar{c}_g^t)$ is a rising tide
that lifts the group regardless of its current position---productivity
gains, public-good expansion, baseline improvement independent of
group status. Third is the mobility term $\tilde{\gamma}\,\bar{c}_g^t(1-\bar{c}_g^t)$
 which captures intergenerational regression to the group mean---children
of high-class parents within the group may fall, children of
low-class parents may rise---and is largest at intermediate $\bar{c}_g$
where the within-group population is most heterogeneous. The
decomposition's analytical power is what it implies for two groups
in the same economy: identical $\mu$, identical heterogeneity,
identical mobility parameter $\tilde{\gamma}$---yet if only one of
them faces $s_g > 0$, the trajectories diverge from generation $t$
onward through the discrimination rate $\phi^t$. These three
channels are precisely the components labelled in the
intergenerational-dynamic node of Figure~\ref{fig:loop}.


To express (\ref{eq:dynamic}) as the composed map governing the
one-dimensional dynamics of $\bar{c}_g^t$, two further specifications
are required: the closed
form of the mobility term, and the closed form of the discrimination
rate $\phi^t$.

For the mobility term, consider that under the Beta--De~Finetti structure
(Appendix~\ref{app:bernoulli}), the within-group variance is
$\sigma_g^2 = \bar{c}_g(1-\bar{c}_g)/(1+\nu)$, where $\nu > 0$ is
the within-group concentration parameter. Therefore the mobility term
becomes $\tilde{\gamma}\,\bar{c}_g(1-\bar{c}_g)$ where
$\tilde{\gamma} = \gamma/(1+\nu)$ is the effective mobility
parameter. This term is maximised at
group prior $\bar{c}_g = 1/2$ (maximum heterogeneity) and vanishes at $\bar{c}_g = 0$
and $\bar{c}_g = 1$ (homogeneous group). It captures
intergenerational regression to the group mean: children of
high-class families face some probability of downward mobility, and
children of low-class families have some chance of upward mobility.

The discrimination rate $\phi^t$ is determined by the engagement threshold (\ref{eq:threshold}),
modulated by marker salience $s_g$
(Remark~\ref{rem:salience}) as $\phi^t = s_g \cdot F(\bar{c}_g^t)$ , where $F: [0,1] \to [0,1]$ is
the group-level discrimination rate function induced by the
threshold: when $s_g = 0$, markers are
invisible and no discrimination fires.
\begin{assumption}[Discrimination-rate regularity]
\label{ass:Freg}
$F$ is $C^1$, strictly decreasing, and convex on $[0,1]$ with
$F(1)=F'(1)=0$; the canonical form $F(\bar{c}_g)=(1-\bar{c}_g)^2$
satisfies it.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}[Exogenous mobility and convergence]
\label{ass:exog}
The mobility rate $\gamma$ and the convergence rate $\mu$ are
exogenous---independent of the discrimination rate $\phi^t$ and of
salience $s_g$. Mobility captures within-group variance arising from
talent, effort, and wealth shocks, independent of the group's social
position.
\end{assumption}

This is a conservative choice. If $\gamma$ were endogenous---decreasing in
the discrimination rate $\phi$ through reduced access to education,
mentorship, and opportunity networks---the closure trap would be more
severe and the bifurcation threshold $\gamma^*$ harder to reach. The stated $\gamma^*$ understates the required mobility intervention, because in the trap basin
$\gamma$ would be endogenously depressed, making the threshold
a moving target that recedes as discrimination intensifies. The policy implication is thus sharper than stated -- as the
required intervention must be larger than stated by the exogenous model assumption.

\textbf{Composed Map} The above mechanism implies closure trap as a low-level equilibrium in which discrimination keeps a group economically depressed, and that depressed position in turn sustains the discrimination, so beliefs and outcomes reproduce one another across generations. Raising intergenerational mobility above a threshold breaks this loop, leaving the integration equilibrium as the only stable outcome.

The intergenerational dynamic (\ref{eq:dynamic}) can thus be reduced to a 
composed map---a one-dimensional discrete dynamical system in the
group prior $\bar{c}_g$ (see \citealp{Galor2007,MedioLines2001}). The fixed points of the map are the equilibria of the intergenerational system: group priors that reproduce themselves from one generation to the next. An equilibrium is locally stable when the map's slope there is less than one in
absolute value, so that nearby trajectories are drawn toward it. When the system admits more than one stable equilibrium, which one a group reaches is selected by its history rather than by parameters alone. 

The map essentially combines three forces. The baseline drift $\mu$ pulls
upward toward integration; the mobility term
$\tilde{\gamma}\,\bar{c}_g(1 - \bar{c}_g)$ acts as intergenerational
regression to the group mean; the discrimination rate
$\phi^t = s_g\,F(\bar{c}_g^t)$ enters with a negative sign, transmitting
to the group prior the downward pressure that the Jeffrey-updating
mechanism generates at the encounter level. The closure trap arises
when this third term dominates the first two across a basin of priors,
holding successive generations in a low-attractor steady state.
Substituting the discrimination rate $\phi^t = s_g\,F(\bar c_g^t)$ and
the Beta--De~Finetti mobility term $\tilde\gamma\,\bar c_g^t(1-\bar c_g^t)$
into the dynamic (\ref{eq:dynamic}) gives
\begin{equation*}
\bar c_g^{t+1} = \bar c_g^t(1 - \delta s_g F(\bar c_g^t)) + \mu(1 - \bar c_g^t) + \tilde\gamma\,\bar c_g^t(1 - \bar c_g^t),
\end{equation*}
and collecting by powers of $\bar c_g^t$ yields the composed map:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{H}(\bar{c}_g;\, s_g) = \mu + \bar{c}_g(1 - \mu + \tilde{\gamma}
  - s_g\,\delta\, F(\bar{c}_g))
  - \tilde{\gamma}\,\bar{c}_g^2
  \label{eq:composed}
\end{equation}

The boundary conditions $\mathcal{H}(0; s_g) = \mu > 0$ and
$\mathcal{H}(1; s_g) = 1$ hold for all marker salience $s_g$, since $F(1) = 0$ and the
$\tilde{\gamma}$ terms cancel at $\bar{c}_g = 1$.

The map $\mathcal{H}$ governs the intergenerational evolution of the group
prior: $\bar{c}_g^{t+1} = \mathcal{H}(\bar{c}_g^t; s_g)$, so iterating $\mathcal{H}$
traces the group's class trajectory across generations. Fixed
points $\bar{c}_g^* = \mathcal{H}(\bar{c}_g^*; s_g)$ are self-reproducing
states---generations whose class profile replicates itself across
the composed map. Whether such a state is desirable
depends on where it sits in $[0,1]$: a fixed point near
$\bar{c}_g = 1$ is integration, a fixed point near $\bar{c}_g = 0$
is exclusion. The three terms in $\mathcal{H}$ compete to determine which
arise as stable equilibria. Mean-reversion ($\mu$) pulls upward from
low priors; the exclusion drag $-s_g \delta F(\bar{c}_g)$
generated by the engagement threshold pulls downward when
discrimination fires; and mobility, contained in the
$\tilde{\gamma}\,\bar{c}_g(1 - \bar{c}_g)$ term, generates
regression to the group mean. When exclusion dominates at low
$\bar{c}_g$, $\mathcal{H}$ dips below the 45-degree line and a second stable
fixed point appears at the low end. This is the closure trap,
which we now formalise.

\begin{definition}[Closure Equilibrium]
\label{def:closure}
A closure equilibrium is a fixed point $(\phi^*, \bar{c}_g^*)$ of the
composed map such that beliefs are consistent with outcomes and
outcomes with beliefs.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Class closure in a homogeneous society]
\label{prop:class-closure}
In the single-group model ($\G = \{g_1\}$), markers signal class
position directly. For sufficiently small $\gamma$ and sufficiently
large $\delta$, the map $\mathcal{H}$ has at least two stable fixed points
$\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{low}} < \bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{high}} = 1$, separated by an unstable tipping point
$\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$. The low fixed point constitutes a
within-group class closure trap: a segment of the population is
permanently excluded from high-class social spaces across
generations, without any inter-group dynamic.
\end{proposition}

\noindent\emph{Sketch.} For large $\delta$ the exclusion drag dominates at
low $\bar{c}_g$, bending the composed map below the $45^\circ$ line so that,
alongside the integrated fixed point at $\bar{c}_g = 1$, a stable low fixed
point and an unstable tipping point appear. The full argument is in
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\subsection{Comparative statics and the mobility threshold}
\label{subsec:intergenerational}

We now characterise the individual-level mechanics of the
discrimination mechanism. The result that follows describes how the Jeffrey framework
produces graded rather than binary discrimination; the comparative
statics on own class, education, and economic history follow by
direct differentiation of $\E[d(c_i, c_j) \mid m_i]$: higher own
economic class $c_j$ expands the engagement set when $c_j <
\bar{c}_g$; lower group history $\bar{c}_g$ pushes more potential
partners below the engagement threshold; higher individual education
sharpens marker-to-group inference and reduces the weight placed on
the group prior.

\begin{remark}[Graded discrimination]
\label{rem:graded}
The Jeffrey framework predicts continuous variation in avoidance with
marker ambiguity. A faint accent produces less avoidance than a
strong one. $\E[d(c_i, c_j)]$ is quadratic in $\bar{c}_g$
($\partial^2 \E/\partial \bar{c}_g^2 = 2 > 0$), distinguishing the model
from Phelps--Arrow's binary response to hard group membership.
\end{remark}

We now turn to the role of marker salience---the spatial dimension
of the model. The result that follows establishes how salience
interacts with economic history to determine whether discrimination
is sustained or extinguished.

\begin{remark}[Marker salience effect]
\label{rem:salience}
Higher marker salience amplifies expected incompatibility when the
out-group is economically disadvantaged:
\[
  \frac{\partial \E[d(c_i, c_j) \mid m_i]}{\partial s_g}
  = D_2 - D_1 > 0
  \quad \text{when } \bar{c}_{g_2} < \bar{c}_{g_1}.
\]
Two groups with identical economic histories $\bar{c}_g$ face
different closure dynamics if their marker salience differs. Marker
salience and economic history are jointly necessary for sustained
discrimination: $s_g = 0$ eliminates discrimination regardless of
$\bar{c}_g$, and $\bar{c}_{g_2} = \bar{c}_{g_1}$ eliminates
discrimination regardless of $s_g$.
\end{remark}

The preceding comparative statics describe how individual-level
parameters and marker salience shape the discrimination rate within
a single generation. The remaining results address two deeper
questions. First, can the closure trap be eliminated permanently, or
is it a structural feature of the system for any positive marker
salience? Second, what happens when a second group enters the model
with its own economic history and marker salience profile, and what
trajectories do its members follow? The next three results answer
these in sequence: the saddle-node bifurcation
(Proposition~\ref{prop:bifurcation}) characterises the mobility
threshold above which the trap is eliminated; the salience-threshold
corollary (Corollary~\ref{cor:salience-threshold}) shows how
immutable markers raise the threshold; and the outsider-group result
(Proposition~\ref{prop:enemy}) traces the integration trajectory of
a low-prior out-group. A further extension---adverse selection through
individual marker suppression, which worsens the position of those who
remain visible---is developed in Appendix~\ref{app:passing}.

\begin{proposition}[Mobility threshold and bifurcation]
\label{prop:bifurcation}
There exists $\gamma^* > 0$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item If $\gamma < \gamma^*$: the closure trap persists (two stable
  fixed points).
  \item If $\gamma > \gamma^*$: the closure trap is eliminated
  (unique stable fixed point at $\bar{c}_g = 1$).
  \item At $\gamma = \gamma^*$: a saddle-node bifurcation \citep[see][ch.~5]{MedioLines2001}---the low
  fixed point and the tipping point collide and annihilate.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\noindent\emph{Sketch.} Raising $\gamma$ lifts $\mathcal{H}$ in the interior
until, at $\gamma^*$, the low fixed point and the tipping point merge
($\mathcal{H}' = 1$) and annihilate in a saddle-node bifurcation; above
$\gamma^*$ only the integrated fixed point survives. See
Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\centering

\caption{Bifurcation diagram of fixed points $\bar{c}_g^*$ against the

The intergenerational persistence of class position near the
bifurcation is characterised in Appendix~\ref{app:Tstar}.

\begin{corollary}[Marker salience raises the mobility threshold]
\label{cor:salience-threshold}
The critical mobility threshold $\tilde{\gamma}^*$ is strictly
increasing in marker salience:
$\partial \tilde{\gamma}^* / \partial s_g > 0$. At $s_g = 0$,
$\tilde{\gamma}^* = 0$ and no closure trap exists for any positive
mobility rate. At $s_g = 1$, $\tilde{\gamma}^*$ attains its maximum,
recovering the base-model threshold. Consequently, groups with
immutable markers (high $s_g$) require strictly higher
intergenerational mobility to escape the closure trap than groups
with mutable markers (low $s_g$), even when starting from identical
economic positions.
\end{corollary}

\noindent\emph{Sketch.} Higher $s_g$ lowers $\mathcal{H}$ in the interior,
deepening the trap and requiring a larger $\tilde{\gamma}$ to remove it;
implicit differentiation of the saddle-node conditions yields
$d\tilde{\gamma}^*/ds_g > 0$. See Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

\begin{definition}[Outsider group]
Group $g_2$ is an outsider group relative to host group $g_1$ if
$\bar{c}_{g_2}^0 < \bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$---their prior starts
in the high-discrimination basin.
\end{definition}

The outsider group's integration trajectory depends on two dimensions:
economic history ($\bar{c}_{g_2}^0$) and marker salience ($s_g$).
A group with low $\bar{c}_{g_2}^0$ but also low $s_g$---phenotypically
similar to the host, with mutable cultural markers---may escape
the trap rapidly because the discrimination channel fires weakly.
A group with comparable $\bar{c}_{g_2}^0$ but high marker salience $s_g$---visibly
distinct, with immutable phenotypic markers---faces a structurally
harder path.

\begin{proposition}[Outsider trap]
\label{prop:enemy}
If $g_2$ enters with $\bar{c}_{g_2}^0 < \bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$
and $s_g$ sufficiently high that $\tilde{\gamma} <
\tilde{\gamma}^*(s_g)$, then $g_2$ converges to the
high-discrimination equilibrium $\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{low}}(g_2)$ independently
of $g_1$'s trajectory, and $g_1$'s equilibrium is unaffected.
If $s_g$ is sufficiently low that $\tilde{\gamma} >
\tilde{\gamma}^*(s_g)$, the trap does not exist and $g_2$
converges to the integration equilibrium regardless of initial
economic position.
\end{proposition}

The embedding of the model in a one-parameter family, with the
standard-Bayesian benchmark recovered as $\rho \to 0$, is developed
in Appendix~\ref{app:loury}.

\subsection{Role of Institutions: the platform channel}
\label{sec:platform}
% =============================================================================

We now turn to conditions where a market actor participates in social engagement. This may entail a situation where a restaurant calibrates its atmosphere to attract preferred compositions, a
school sets admission criteria, a neighbourhood configures itself
through pricing or zoning and so on. We use ``platform'' to denote any such
venue, neighbourhood, school, or institution that mediates
encounters through an atmosphere vector and an admission policy.

The bilateral mechanism locates the first source of persistence at
the individual level: the rigidity condition ensures that compliance
does not aggregate into group prior revision. The platform identifies
a second source, which operates at the institutional level and is
independent of the first: even if bilateral encounters did not
generate persistence, profit-maximising platforms acting on soft
signals would produce discriminatory compositions as an emergent
outcome.

The formal platform model---experience utility, the
profit-maximisation problem, and its equilibrium
characterisation---is developed in Appendix~\ref{app:platform}.
Embedding bilateral Jeffrey updating within a profit-maximising
platform produces three results absent from the bilateral setting: a
shadow information market whose intensity rises with legal strictness
(Proposition~\ref{prop:shadow}); optimal exclusion of the outsider
group that relaxes into a tokenism equilibrium before genuine
integration is achieved
(Propositions~\ref{prop:exclusion}--\ref{prop:tokenism}); and a host
destabilisation channel through which including the out-group can
trigger closure dynamics within the host group
(Proposition~\ref{prop:spillover}). The structural counterpart is
tokenism: visible inclusion without the belief revision that
integration would require---an emergent outcome of the Jeffrey
mechanism and profit incentives, not of any imputed intent.

It is illustrative how the above platform framework applies to issues of immigration. An
immigrant's assimilation trajectory is determined by the economic
tier at which initial social attachment occurs: the host's Jeffrey
revision maps the foreign marker bundle $m_i^{\text{foreign}}$ onto
an initial prior $\bar{c}_g^{\text{init}}$ that depends on marker
legibility within the host's marker space $\M$, not on actual
economic class at origin. Immigrants with legible high-class markers may thus
access high-class platforms from the outset -- whereas those with illegible
markers enter the closure trap from below. This generates two channels in host preferences over
immigration composition: a \emph{closure-sustainability channel}
(high-income hosts accept pre-sorted high-class immigrants who expand
the high-income network without disrupting stratification) and a
\emph{competition channel} (same-tier arrivals compete in labour and
social spaces, cutting against the first motive). Several empirical studies confirm the within-ethnic differential treatment by class markers \citep{LeeZhou2015, Waters1999}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We now discuss three sets of observations where the separation of group-assignment from marker inference in the current paper allows us to understand social boundaries as a closure trap. First, we discuss how market incentives affect the contact channel through platforms. Second, we consider how stereotypes in the contact channel may sustain economic inequality. Third, we use the model's terminology to comment on movements that attempt to forcefully change the marker significance.

Consider first how platform effects transform the bilateral encounter into a systemic
pattern: modern rideshare platforms mediate millions of such
encounters daily, and the platform's atmosphere instruments---
ratings, neighbourhood-based pricing, photograph display--- can create
the conditions under which a shadow information market operates
at scale. For example, anonymous digital environments---text-only forums, pseudonymous
platforms---are settings where marker salience $s_g \approx 0$ by
design, and the
model predicts zero discrimination regardless of the group prior
$\bar{c}_g$
(Remark~\ref{rem:salience}). But when platforms introduce
identity-revealing features---real names, profile photographs,
video---$s_g$ jumps discontinuously and discrimination emerges
immediately, with intensity proportional to the gap between the
revealed group's $\bar{c}_g$ and the host mean. The empirical
literature on platform discrimination is consistent with this prediction. More specifically, discrimination on rental and rideshare platforms correlates with the introduction
of photographs and real names, not with any change in the underlying
population. When we consider the market with soft markers, the platforms reproducing discrimination are mere economic actors pursuing private returns. A top restaurant that is formally open to all who can pay, cannot rule out that its clients may associate eliteness with distance from lower economic backgrounds. Experience utility therefore depends on room
composition through Jeffrey updating as well. The restaurant,
to maximise perceived quality, manages room composition through
informationally mediated instruments such as dress codes. Similarly, reservation systems may filter by social network, location systems may create transport barriers and so on. While racial correlation is no longer an input consideration, it ends up being an indirect consequence of markers-driven platforms nevertheless. These instruments operate on the contact channel rather than
the contracting channel of \citet{Loury2002}. Thus, while anti-discrimination law
constrains the contracting channel, it does not reach the
contact channel far enough.

Second, we consider how the inferential
rigidities already in place---racial stereotypes, historical adverse
selection patterns--- may propagate themselves across generations and
across cases, sustaining the economic structure that produced them.
\citet{Loury2002} gives the example of taxi drivers who do not stop
for black passengers in high-crime areas. In his framework, this is
adverse selection: non-robbers (more sensitive to the cost of
waiting) exit the pool first, leaving a pool enriched in robbers,
which confirms the prior. In our framework it is in fact a special case. The
Loury mechanism operates through the $\delta \phi^t$ term in
equation~(\ref{eq:dynamic})---the exclusion cost $\delta$ times the
discrimination rate $\phi$. Therefore, repeated discrimination erodes the
group's economic position, which reinforces the prior. What the
Jeffrey framework adds is the explanation of \emph{why} the prior is
rigid to individual counter-examples. A taxi driver who does stop
and finds a law-abiding passenger updates $P'(g_k)$ for that
individual---``this person seemed respectable''---but does not necessarily update
$\bar{c}_g$ for the group: the encounter reassigns the individual rather
than supplying the group-level evidence that alone could move
$\bar{c}_g$. This is the limitation of the soft-marker environment.

That said, it is worth being explicit about what this explanation of persistence does and does not claim. The statement is not behavioural: it does not assert that the driver refuses
to learn or reasons poorly. Nor does it claim that stereotypes never
change. The hopeful Bayesian---who expects that enough good individual
encounters will gradually revise how a group is seen---describes the case
of no belief rigidity (the $\rho \to 0$ limit), in which counter-examples
do erode the prior. The claim is that once the group prior is held with
any rigidity (the generic case), this gradual revision does not proceed
as Bayesian conditioning leads one to expect, because the hopeful
Bayesian is counting on the wrong kind of evidence. In other words, a favourable
encounter is coherent evidence about the individual, whom the observer
re-attributes, and carries nothing about the group's distribution. So
when a group's standing does shift, it shifts through the group's actual
mobility across generations, not through the accumulation of individual
examples.

Finally, we consider how language revival movements and anti-colonial movements -- respectively attempt to change marker salience $s_g$ and attempt collective action on the mobility parameter $\gamma$. The language revival movements---Welsh, Hebrew, M\={a}ori---present an
apparent paradox: a suppressed group deliberately increases its own
marker salience. The model predicts that higher $s_g$ raises the
mobility threshold $\tilde{\gamma}^*(s_g)$
(Corollary~\ref{cor:salience-threshold}), so voluntary $s_g$ increase
should intensify discrimination. This may be rational in the model
when the group prior $\bar{c}_g >
\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$. If the group's economic position is
already above the tipping point, the closure trap does not bind and
increasing marker salience $s_g$ carries an identity value without triggering the
trap. The model predicts that successful language revival movements
should emerge \emph{after} economic convergence, not before. Hebrew
is the confirming case: revival accompanied state-building and
economic development. Indigenous language revival in contexts of
persistent economic disadvantage is the hard case---the model
predicts it is collectively costly even if individually
identity-affirming. 

It is not uncommon for anti-establishment movements to combine
collective action on the mobility parameter $\gamma$ with a
coordinated refusal to suppress the stigmatised marker---adopting
dress, food, language, naming practices, or grooming conventions
that the host classification system codes as low-status. Joining
high-$c_i$ behaviour to the stigmatised marker raises within-group
$\sigma_g^2$, which structures a trade-off the model makes
explicit. On one side, expected class distance widens and
engagement contracts, foregoing economic benefits from cross-group
interaction. On the other, the marker becomes a less reliable
signal of class and the inference from marker to group weakens,
reducing discrimination on the contact channel. 

What arises as a consequence of the model is the clarification that when social encounters in a soft-marker environment do not alter the group prior, the compliance behaviour can remain persistent since individual
investment in host markers does deliver personal reclassification. In other words, while the local incentive to comply is positive
at every encounter, the closure trap is invisible from inside the
basin -- since agents cannot observe that the tipping point is
unreachable. The Mimic-Men dilemma is the separation of this persistence of group prior from individual gains through marker adoption.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =============================================================================

This paper develops a formal model of social boundaries grounded
in Jeffrey conditioning---the probability-theoretic framework for
updating beliefs from soft evidence. The central result is that
the contact channel alone suffices to generate a self-sustaining
closure trap, without discriminatory preferences and without
strategic interaction through the labour market. The environment of markers shows that conditions
supposed to dissolve discrimination---integration, social mixing,
removal of formal barriers---are precisely what may also expose soft markers
to an updating mechanism that sustains it.

We find that the Jeffrey updating rule, applied to soft social
markers correlated with economic history, generates a
\emph{self-sustaining closure trap} as a stable equilibrium outcome,
even in the absence of racial heterogeneity. The trap is
path-dependent and intergenerationally persistent, and resists
individual counter-examples within any single encounter---each is
reassigned rather than counted against the group's standing, the rigidity
being not a refusal to learn but the absence of anything coherent to learn
from---though not across generations, where mobility above $\gamma^*$
dissolves it.
Racial discrimination enters as a perturbation of this already-operating
equilibrium --- a second group whose group prior $\bar{c}_{g_2}$
starts in the high-discrimination basin --- not, within this regime, a separate
phenomenon requiring separate explanation. This holds only where
soft-marker inference leaves the stereotype unchanged; where an
outsider's success instead provokes resentment or coalitional threat, the
response lies outside the model. The closure trap requires
a minimum level of Jeffrey rigidity $\rho > \rho^*$; below this
threshold, the model reduces to the standard-Bayesian benchmark of
the statistical-discrimination tradition
\citep{Phelps1972,Arrow1973}, where individual counter-examples
erode stereotypes.

There exists a threshold level of intergenerational mobility
$\gamma^*$ below which the closure trap persists permanently and
above which it is eliminated through a saddle-node bifurcation.
Near the bifurcation, the number of generations required to escape
diverges as $(\gamma - \gamma^*)^{-1/2}$---the universal scaling of
saddle-node ghosts. This provides a formal explanation for why
apparent progress can be painfully slow even when the direction is
correct, and why programmes calibrated below $\gamma^*$ produce
measurable improvement followed by reversion.


The model identifies two qualitatively different policy instruments
operating on different channels. Anti-discrimination law remains
necessary on the formal contracting channel; the contact channel
calls for instruments that operate on the intergenerational class
distribution. Assimilation incentives may channel resources toward
marker investment and away from the mobility
investment that addresses the contact channel directly.
Temporary forced integration shifts a cohort above the tipping point
but does not change the trap's structure: the trap regenerates for
subsequent cohorts. Mobility programmes raising
$\gamma$ above $\gamma^*$ eliminate the trap permanently through the
saddle-node bifurcation; above $\gamma^*$, the group converges to
$\bar{c}$ from any starting position without requiring integration.
The two instruments are complements in the sub-threshold regime:
when $\gamma$ is near but below $\gamma^*$, integration manages
cohort positions while mobility investment pushes $\gamma$ toward
$\gamma^*$. Integration without mobility leaves the trap intact for
future cohorts; mobility above $\gamma^*$ is sufficient on its own.
Anti-discrimination law and mobility investment thus
operate on different channels; neither substitutes for the other, and both are
necessary in the regimes where their respective channels are
active.


A particular structural feature of the model worth highlighting is that the individually rational response to the
closure trap---marker investment, compliance, personal
reclassification---becomes collectively self-defeating, because the
Jeffrey architecture ensures that individual exceptions do not
aggregate into group prior revision. The route to permanent trap
escape runs through raising the mobility parameter above its
threshold ($\gamma > \gamma^*$), not through individual marker
investment $e^*$. The
two compete for the same scarce resources, and the Jeffrey
mechanism ensures that resources invested in individual compliance
are wasted from the group's perspective even though they are
individually rewarded.

This appendix collects the proofs of the results stated in the main
text. Notation is as in the body: the group prior $\bar{c}_g \in [0,1]$
is the within-group mean of class outcomes; $F:[0,1]\to[0,1]$ is the
discrimination-rate function, maintained $C^1$, strictly decreasing and
convex with $F(1)=F'(1)=0$; the per-period
rate is $\phi^t = s_g F(\bar{c}_g^t)$; $\mathcal{H}(\bar{c}_g; s_g)$ is the
composed intergenerational map of equation~(\ref{eq:composed}); and
$\Psi(\bar{c}_g) = \mathcal{H}(\bar{c}_g; s_g) - \bar{c}_g$ is the residual
whose zeros are the fixed points.

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:class-closure}]
Substituting the composed map~(\ref{eq:composed}) and factoring,
\[
  \Psi(\bar{c}_g) = \mathcal{H}(\bar{c}_g; s_g) - \bar{c}_g
  = (1-\bar{c}_g)(\mu + \tilde{\gamma}\bar{c}_g)
    - s_g\delta\,\bar{c}_g F(\bar{c}_g),
\]
so $\Psi(0) = \mu > 0$ and $\Psi(1) = 0$ (as $F(1)=0$).

\emph{The integration state is stable.} Differentiating and using
$F(1)=F'(1)=0$ gives
$\Psi'(1) = -(\mu+\tilde{\gamma}) - s_g\delta F'(1) = -(\mu+\tilde{\gamma}) < 0$,
hence $\mathcal{H}'(1) = 1+\Psi'(1) = 1-(\mu+\tilde{\gamma}) \in (-1,1)$ for
$\mu+\tilde{\gamma}<2$: the boundary fixed point $\bar{c}_g = 1$ is locally
stable and $\Psi > 0$ on a left-neighbourhood of $1$. (Were $F$ to reach
zero with nonzero slope, $\Psi'(1)$ would turn positive for large $\delta$
and destabilise integration; $F'(1)=0$ is exactly what excludes this.)

\emph{An interior dip for large $\delta$.} Fix any interior
$\bar{c}_0 \in (0,1)$. As $F$ is decreasing with $F(0)>0$, the quantity
$b := s_g \bar{c}_0 F(\bar{c}_0)$ is positive while
$a := (1-\bar{c}_0)(\mu+\tilde{\gamma}\bar{c}_0) > 0$ is independent of
$\delta$; thus $\Psi(\bar{c}_0) = a - \delta b < 0$ once $\delta > a/b$
(small $\tilde{\gamma}$ enters symmetrically through $a$). Fix such a
$\delta$.

\emph{Two interior roots.} For $\bar{c}_g \in (0,1)$, $\Psi=0$ iff
$L(\bar{c}_g)=R(\bar{c}_g)$, with $\Psi \gtrless 0 \iff R \gtrless L$, where
\[
  L(\bar{c}_g) := s_g\delta F(\bar{c}_g),
  \qquad
  R(\bar{c}_g) := \frac{(1-\bar{c}_g)(\mu+\tilde{\gamma}\bar{c}_g)}{\bar{c}_g}
   = \frac{\mu}{\bar{c}_g} + (\tilde{\gamma}-\mu) - \tilde{\gamma}\bar{c}_g .
\]
$R$ is strictly decreasing and convex with $R(0^{+})=+\infty$, $R(1)=0$;
$L$ is strictly decreasing and convex with $L(0)$ finite, $L(1)=0$, and
$L = O\!\big((1-\bar{c}_g)^2\big)$ near $1$ (since $F(1)=F'(1)=0$). Hence
$R>L$ near both endpoints---so $\Psi>0$ there---while the dip
$\Psi(\bar{c}_0)<0$ places $L>R$ between, so $L$ and $R$ meet at an interior
$+\!\to\!-$ crossing $\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{low}}$
($\mathcal{H}'(\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{low}})<1$, stable) and a $-\!\to\!+$
crossing $\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$
($\mathcal{H}'(\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}})>1$, unstable), alongside the
boundary root $\bar{c}_g=1$. For the canonical $F=(1-\bar{c}_g)^2$ the count
is explicit:
$\Psi = (1-\bar{c}_g)\big[s_g\delta\,\bar{c}_g^{2}
 + (\tilde{\gamma}-s_g\delta)\bar{c}_g + \mu\big]$,
whose bracketed quadratic is positive at $\bar{c}_g\in\{0,1\}$ and, once
$\delta$ is large (positive discriminant, vertex interior), has exactly two
roots in $(0,1)$---giving precisely the three fixed points above.

\emph{Path dependence.} Since $\mathcal{H}'>0$ on
$(0,\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}})$ and on $(\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}},1)$, orbits
are monotone on each side of the tipping point and converge to
$\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{low}}$ or to $\bar{c}_g=1$ according as
$\bar{c}_g^{0}\lessgtr\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$ \citep[ch.~1]{Galor2007}.
The low fixed point is the within-group closure trap. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:bifurcation}]
At $\gamma = 0$, the closure trap exists
(Proposition~\ref{prop:class-closure}). As the mobility parameter $\gamma$ increases, the
mobility term raises $\mathcal{H}(\bar{c}_g)$ in the interior, shrinking
the region where $\Psi < 0$. At $\gamma = \gamma^*$, the region
vanishes: the low fixed point and the tipping point merge,
$\mathcal{H}'(\bar{c}_g^*) = 1$, and the system undergoes a saddle-node
bifurcation. For $\gamma > \gamma^*$, $\Psi > 0$ on all of $(0,1)$
and the only fixed point is $\bar{c}_g = 1$. The exact $\tilde{\gamma}^*$ is
found numerically by solving the bifurcation system
$\{\mathcal{H}(\bar{c}_g) = \bar{c}_g,\; \mathcal{H}'(\bar{c}_g) = 1\}$ jointly.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:salience-threshold}]
The composed map $\mathcal{H}(\bar{c}_g;\, s_g)$ is decreasing in marker salience $s_g$ on the
interior of $[0,1]$ (since $\partial G/\partial s_g = -\delta\phi
\bar{c}_g < 0$). A higher $s_g$ lowers $\mathcal{H}$ in the interior, deepening
the region where $\Psi < 0$ and requiring higher $\tilde{\gamma}$ to
restore $\Psi \geq 0$ everywhere. At $s_g = 0$, the discrimination
channel is fully shut off ($\phi^t = 0$ for all $t$), so
$\mathcal{H}(\bar{c}_g;\, 0) = \mu + \bar{c}_g(1 - \mu + \tilde{\gamma}) -
\tilde{\gamma}\bar{c}_g^2$, which satisfies $\Psi \geq 0$ for all
$\tilde{\gamma} \geq 0$. To establish strict monotonicity on
$(0,1]$, apply the implicit function theorem to the saddle-node
conditions $\Psi = 0$ and $\Psi_{\bar{c}_g} = 0$ that define
$(\bar{c}_g^*, \tilde{\gamma}^*)$ as functions of $s_g$.
Differentiating $\Psi = 0$ totally in $s_g$ and using the degeneracy
condition $\Psi_{\bar{c}_g} = 0$ at the bifurcation gives
\[
\frac{d\tilde{\gamma}^*}{ds_g} = -\frac{\Psi_{s_g}}{\Psi_{\tilde{\gamma}}}.
\]
Direct computation yields
$\Psi_{s_g} = -\delta\,\bar{c}_g F(\bar{c}_g) < 0$ and
$\Psi_{\tilde{\gamma}} = \bar{c}_g(1 - \bar{c}_g) > 0$ on $\bar{c}_g \in
(0,1)$, so $d\tilde{\gamma}^*/ds_g > 0$. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:enemy}]
In the bilateral model the map governing a group's intergenerational
prior depends only on that group's own state and marker salience,
$\bar{c}_{g_2}^{t+1} = \mathcal{H}(\bar{c}_{g_2}^t; s_g)$, with no argument
drawn from $g_1$'s prior. The dynamics of $g_2$ are therefore
autonomous, and the fixed-point structure of
Proposition~\ref{prop:class-closure} together with the bifurcation of
Proposition~\ref{prop:bifurcation} applies to $g_2$ at its own
$(\bar{c}_{g_2}, s_g)$. When $s_g$ is high enough that
$\tilde{\gamma} < \tilde{\gamma}^*(s_g)$
(Corollary~\ref{cor:salience-threshold}), the trap persists for $g_2$:
its map retains the stable low fixed point
$\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{low}}(g_2)$ and the unstable tipping point
$\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$. An outsider group entering with
$\bar{c}_{g_2}^0 < \bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$ lies in the low basin, and
monotonicity of $\mathcal{H}$ on $(0, \bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}})$ carries its
orbit to $\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{low}}(g_2)$. Since $g_1$'s map is likewise
autonomous, $g_1$'s equilibrium is unaffected. When instead $s_g$ is
low enough that $\tilde{\gamma} > \tilde{\gamma}^*(s_g)$, the saddle-node
has already eliminated the low basin
(Proposition~\ref{prop:bifurcation}), so the unique fixed point is
$\bar{c}_g = 1$ and $g_2$ converges to integration from any initial
economic position. \qed
\end{proof}

\section{Adverse Selection through Passing}
\label{app:passing}

This appendix develops the marker-suppression extension referenced in
Section~\ref{subsec:intergenerational}. Individuals in the visible
out-group may reduce their personal marker salience $s_{g,i}$ at a cost
$\kappa(c_i)$ that is decreasing in own class $c_i$, so higher-class
members find it cheaper to ``pass.'' Let within-group class be
distributed with c.d.f.\ $F_c$ on $[0,1]$ and mean $\bar{c}_g$; under the
Beta--De~Finetti structure of Appendix~\ref{app:bernoulli}, $F_c$ is the
$\mathrm{Beta}$ mixing distribution with mean $\bar{c}_g$.

\begin{proposition}[Adverse selection through passing]
\label{prop:passing}
When individuals can reduce their personal marker salience $s_{g,i}$
at cost $\kappa(c_i)$ decreasing in class, selective exit from the
visible out-group worsens the remaining group's prior:
\[
  \frac{\partial \bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{visible}}}
  {\partial (\mathrm{passing\ rate})} < 0.
\]
The individuals most able to pass---those with highest $c_i$ and
lowest natural $s_{g,i}$---are positively selected. Their departure
lowers the mean class of the visible group, which in turn raises the
discrimination rate $F(\bar{c}_g)$ against those who remain. Individual
compliance does not merely fail to improve the group prior (the
rigidity condition); it actively worsens it through composition. The
mimic man's escape harms those he leaves behind.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
An individual passes when the net private benefit of suppressing
markers exceeds the cost $\kappa(c_i)$. Because $\kappa$ is decreasing in
$c_i$ while the benefit is non-decreasing, the passing decision is
monotone in class: there is a threshold $c^\dagger$ such that exactly
the members with $c_i > c^\dagger$ exit the visible group. The visible
group is therefore the upper-truncated population $\{c_i \le c^\dagger\}$,
with mean
\[
  \bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{visible}}(c^\dagger)
  = \E\!\left[c_i \mid c_i \le c^\dagger\right],
\]
which is strictly increasing in $c^\dagger$. The passing rate is
$1 - F_c(c^\dagger)$, decreasing in $c^\dagger$; hence
$\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{visible}}$ is strictly decreasing in the passing rate,
giving $\partial \bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{visible}}/\partial(\text{passing rate}) < 0$.
Since the discrimination-rate function $F$ is decreasing in the group
prior, the induced fall in $\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{visible}}$ raises
$F(\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{visible}})$ against those who remain. The effect
operates entirely through the group prior that the rigidity condition
holds fixed under individual counter-examples, so exit does not revise
that prior upward---it lowers it. \qed
\end{proof}

\section{The Platform Channel}
\label{app:platform}

This appendix develops the platform extension summarised in
Section~\ref{sec:platform}: a profit-maximising institution that
mediates social encounters through an atmosphere vector and an
admission policy. Notation is as in the body. The bilateral mechanism
locates a first source of persistence at the individual level; the
platform identifies a second, operating at the institutional level and
independent of the first, even when bilateral encounters generate no
persistence.

For a $g_1$ client $j$, experience utility at a venue with attendee
set $\A$ is:
\begin{equation}
  V_j(\A) = \alpha \cdot q + (1 - \alpha) \cdot S_j(\A)
  \label{eq:experience}
\end{equation}
where $q$ is objective quality, $\alpha \in (0,1)$ is the weight on
objective vs.\ social quality, and:
\begin{equation}
  S_j(\A) = \frac{1}{|\A|} \sum_{i \in \A,\, i \neq j}
  \E_j[c_i \mid m_i]
  \label{eq:social-quality}
\end{equation}
$S_j(\A)$ is a function of \emph{beliefs}, not facts.

The platform produces atmosphere vector
$\mathbf{a}$ (pricing, location, d\'{e}cor, dress code,
marketing language, music, staff manner) serving two functions.
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Client self-selection.}
$\Pr(\text{visit} \mid c_j, \mathbf{a}) = h(c_j, \mathbf{a})$,
increasing in the match between $c_j$ and the class $\mathbf{a}$
signals.

\item \textbf{Contextual modulation of Jeffrey updating.}
$P'(g_k \mid m_i, \mathbf{a}) \neq P'(g_k \mid m_i)$. A strong
high-class atmosphere acts as a contextual prior, resolving marker
ambiguity against the stigmatised group.

\begin{equation}
  \max_{\mathbf{a},\, \sigma} \quad
  \Pi(\mathbf{a}, \sigma) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{D}(\mathbf{a})}
  \sigma(m_j) \cdot W_j(\mathbf{a}, \sigma) - C(\mathbf{a})
  \label{eq:platform-opt}
\end{equation}
where $\Pi$ is platform profit, $\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{a})$ is the
self-selecting set,
$\sigma: \M \to [0,1]$ is the admission policy, $W_j$ is willingness
to pay, and $C(\mathbf{a})$ is cost.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Legal constraint:} $\sigma$ cannot be an explicit function of
group membership $g$. But $\sigma$ may depend on markers $m$, which
are correlated with $g$. Result: \emph{informationally mediated,
legally invisible discrimination}.

We define the \emph{shadow information value}
$\Omega_j = (1-\alpha)\,(S_j^{*} - S_j^{\mathrm{rand}})$, the gain in
perceived social quality~(\ref{eq:social-quality}) a client secures by
using informal cues to sort into the venue whose composition matches
its preference, relative to uninformed (random) selection.

\begin{proposition}[Shadow market intensity]
\label{prop:shadow}
The shadow information value $\Omega_j$ satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item $\Omega_j$ is decreasing in $\bar{c}_g$ for $\bar{c}_g < 1/2$:
  groups with worse economic histories generate higher shadow market
  intensity.
  \item $\partial \Omega_j / \partial (1-\alpha) > 0$: clients who
  weight social quality more invest more in shadow information.
  \item $\partial \Omega_j / \partial \lambda > 0$ where $\lambda$
  indexes legal strictness: tighter anti-discrimination law raises
  the return to shadow information. The mechanism: the law constrains
  formal screening but leaves shadow cues unconstrained, degrading
  the uninformed fallback while preserving the informed outcome.
  \item $\partial \Omega_j / \partial s_g > 0$: higher marker
  salience raises shadow market intensity, because visible markers
  make group attribution easier and therefore the informational
  advantage of knowing the ``right'' venues more valuable.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $\Omega_j = (1-\alpha)(S_j^{*} - S_j^{\mathrm{rand}})$ with $S_j^*$
the social-quality term~(\ref{eq:social-quality}) under informed venue
choice and $S_j^{\mathrm{rand}}$ under uninformed choice; objective
quality $q$ cancels. (i) The gap $S_j^{*} - S_j^{\mathrm{rand}}$ measures
the dispersion in venue composition that sorting exploits; for
$\bar{c}_g < 1/2$ this gap widens as $\bar{c}_g$ falls, so
$\partial \Omega_j / \partial \bar{c}_g < 0$. (ii) $\Omega_j$ is
proportional to $(1-\alpha)$, so
$\partial \Omega_j / \partial (1-\alpha) > 0$. (iii) Let $\lambda$ index
legal strictness, which lowers the uninformed payoff
$S_j^{\mathrm{rand}}$ by $\gamma\lambda$---formal screening is
constrained while shadow cues are not---without altering the informed
payoff; then $\Omega_j = V_1 - (V_0 - \gamma\lambda)$ and
$\partial \Omega_j / \partial \lambda = \gamma > 0$. (iv) Higher marker
salience $s_g$ sharpens group attribution from cues, raising
$S_j^{*} - S_j^{\mathrm{rand}}$ and hence
$\partial \Omega_j / \partial s_g > 0$. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Platform exclusion of outsider group]
\label{prop:exclusion}
At the outsider group's entry prior $\bar{c}_{g_2}^0$, the platform
optimally excludes $g_2$ entirely:
$\sigma^*(m) = 0$ for all $m \in \M_{g_2}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The platform's first-order condition with respect to
$\sigma(m)$ for $m \in \M_{g_2}$ decomposes into two terms. The
direct revenue effect is the marginal willingness-to-pay $W(g_2)$ of
an admitted $g_2$ member. The composition externality is the
aggregated drop in social-quality perception across the $n_{g_1}$
host clients on the platform, weighted by $(1 - \alpha)$ from
equation~(\ref{eq:experience}): admitting one additional $g_2$
member shifts each host's $S_j(\mathcal{A})$ by
$(\bar{c}_{g_2} - \bar{c}_{g_1})/|\mathcal{A}| < 0$. The net
marginal profit is therefore
\[
  \frac{\partial \Pi}{\partial \sigma(m)} \;=\;
  W(g_2) \;+\; n_{g_1} (1 - \alpha)
  \cdot \frac{\bar{c}_{g_2} - \bar{c}_{g_1}}{|\mathcal{A}|}.
\]
At the entry prior $\bar{c}_{g_2}^0$, the gap
$\bar{c}_{g_1} - \bar{c}_{g_2}^0$ is large and $W(g_2)$ is bounded
above by the willingness-to-pay of a low-class admittee. The
externality term dominates and the derivative is negative, so the
platform's optimum is the corner $\sigma^*(m) = 0$.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Partial inclusion equilibrium]
\label{prop:tokenism}
The platform exclusion threshold $\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{excl}}$ satisfies
$\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{excl}} < \bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$: the platform begins
admitting $g_2$ members \emph{before} they have escaped the
high-discrimination basin, creating a partial inclusion zone
$[\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{excl}}, \bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}]$ in which $g_2$ has
platform access but not social integration.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The exclusion threshold $\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{excl}}$ is the value of
$\bar{c}_{g_2}$ at which the platform's FOC (proof of
Proposition~\ref{prop:exclusion}) just balances:
\[
  W(g_2) \;=\; n_{g_1} (1 - \alpha)
  \cdot \frac{\bar{c}_{g_1} - \bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{excl}}}{|\mathcal{A}|}
\]
This is set by the platform's local marginal calculation---the
average class signal inside the platform. The social tipping point
$\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$, by contrast, is fixed by the global
discrimination rate $F(\bar{c}_g)$ crossing the level at which the
closure basin disappears (Proposition~\ref{prop:class-closure}), a
property of the system-wide dynamic in
equation~(\ref{eq:dynamic}). The two thresholds answer to
structurally different objects, and the platform's FOC balances at a
$\bar{c}_g$ strictly below the social tipping point. \qed
\end{proof}

This equilibrium is the structural counterpart of tokenism: the
platform admits members of the stigmatised group before social
integration is achieved, producing visible inclusion without the
underlying belief revision integration would require---an emergent
outcome of the Jeffrey mechanism and profit incentives, not of any
imputed intent. These admissions are not cosmetic, however: they
alter the platform's marginal composition and can destabilise the
host equilibrium when the host group is near its own tipping point.

\begin{proposition}[Host destabilisation]
\label{prop:spillover}
If $g_1$'s equilibrium prior $\bar{c}_{g_1}^*$ is close to
$\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$, the platform's admission of $g_2$ members
at $\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{excl}}$ can push $g_1$ below its own tipping
point, triggering a class-closure episode within the host group. The
critical $g_2$ fraction $\eta_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is decreasing in the
spillover sensitivity parameter $\varepsilon$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Admitting $g_2$ members at fraction $\eta$ on the platform lowers
the local average class signal observed by $g_1$ clients by
$\eta \cdot (\bar{c}_{g_1}^* - \bar{c}_{g_2}^{\mathrm{excl}})$. The
spillover sensitivity $\varepsilon \in (0, 1]$ measures the fraction
of this local shift that transmits to $g_1$'s effective system-wide
prior through repeated platform encounters:
$\bar{c}_{g_1}^{\mathrm{eff}} = \bar{c}_{g_1}^*
- \varepsilon \eta (\bar{c}_{g_1}^* - \bar{c}_{g_2}^{\mathrm{excl}})$.
The host group crosses its tipping point when
$\bar{c}_{g_1}^{\mathrm{eff}} = \bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$, giving
\[
  \eta_{\mathrm{crit}}
  \;=\; \frac{\bar{c}_{g_1}^* - \bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}}
  {\varepsilon \cdot (\bar{c}_{g_1}^* - \bar{c}_{g_2}^{\mathrm{excl}})}
\]
whose derivative
$\partial \eta_{\mathrm{crit}} / \partial \varepsilon$ is strictly
negative when $\bar{c}_{g_1}^*$ exceeds both $\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$
and $\bar{c}_{g_2}^{\mathrm{excl}}$, the configuration of interest.
\qed
\end{proof}

\section{The Rigidity Parameter and the Bayesian Limit}
\label{app:loury}

For this appendix to be read on its own, we recall the main-text
notation used below. The group prior $\bar{c}_g \in [0,1]$ is the
within-group mean of class outcomes (\ref{eq:prior}). The
discrimination rate function $F: [0,1] \to [0,1]$ is decreasing in
$\bar{c}_g$ with $F(1) = 0$, and the per-period discrimination rate
is $\phi^t = s_g \cdot F(\bar{c}_g^t)$, where $s_g \in [0,1]$ is
marker salience. The parameter $\delta > 0$ is the economic cost of
social exclusion per unit of $\phi$; $\mu > 0$ is the baseline drift
toward integration; $\tilde{\gamma} = \gamma/(1+\nu)$ is the
effective mobility, with $\gamma$ the within-group mobility
parameter and $\nu$ the Beta concentration parameter. The composed
map $\mathcal{H}(\bar{c}_g; s_g)$ governing intergenerational belief
dynamics is given by (\ref{eq:composed}), and the residual
$\Psi(\bar{c}_g) = \mathcal{H}(\bar{c}_g; s_g) - \bar{c}_g$
characterises its fixed points.

We introduce $\rho \in [0,1]$, the \emph{rigidity parameter}, into
the dynamic system. At $\rho = 0$, individual counter-examples fully
update the group prior $F_g$---standard Bayesian updating.
At $\rho = 1$, the group prior is completely rigid---full Jeffrey
conditioning. Combined with marker salience $s_g$, the amended
dynamic system is:
\begin{equation}
  G_{\rho,s}(\bar{c}_g, \phi) = \mu + \bar{c}_g(1 - \mu - \rho\, s_g\, \delta \phi)
  + \tilde{\gamma}\,\bar{c}_g(1-\bar{c}_g)
  \label{eq:loury-rho}
\end{equation}
The discrimination channel requires both $\rho > 0$ (Jeffrey
rigidity) and $s_g > 0$ (visible markers). Either rigidity parameter $\rho = 0$ or
marker salience $s_g = 0$ eliminates the discrimination channel entirely, producing
$G = \mu + \bar{c}_g(1-\mu) + \tilde{\gamma}\bar{c}_g(1-\bar{c}_g)$
with a unique stable fixed point at $\bar{c}_g = 1$.

For the fixed-point analysis below, define
\begin{equation}
  \Psi_\rho(\bar{c}_g) \;=\; G_{\rho,s}(\bar{c}_g,\, F(\bar{c}_g)) - \bar{c}_g,
  \label{eq:psi-rho}
\end{equation}
the analogue, for the rigidity-augmented map, of the residual $\Psi$
defined above. At $\rho = 1$ and $\phi = F(\bar{c}_g)$, $G_{\rho,s}$
coincides with $\mathcal{H}$ and $\Psi_\rho$ coincides with $\Psi$.

\begin{proposition}[Bayesian nesting]
\label{prop:loury-nesting}
There exists $\rho^* \in (0,1)$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
  \item For $\rho < \rho^*$ (Bayesian regime): unique stable fixed
  point at $\bar{c}_g = 1$. Individual counter-examples erode
  stereotypes.
  \item For $\rho > \rho^*$ (Jeffrey regime): two stable fixed
  points. The closure trap exists.
\end{enumerate}
The standard-Bayesian benchmark of the statistical-discrimination
tradition \citep{Phelps1972,Arrow1973} is the $\rho \to 0$ limit; the
self-confirming, persistent stereotype---the concern of
\citet{Loury2002} and the patronising equilibrium of
\citet{CoateLoury1993}---corresponds to the closure trap at
$\rho > \rho^*$, here driven by Jeffrey rigidity rather than
belief--investment feedback.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
At $\rho = 0$, $\Psi_\rho > 0$ on all of $(0,1)$: no closure trap.
At $\rho = 1$, $\Psi_\rho$ dips negative: closure trap exists. By
continuity in $\rho$, there exists $\rho^* \in (0,1)$ where the
transition occurs (IVT). Under base parameters, $\rho^* \approx 0.31$.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The platform channel inherits the rigidity threshold]
\label{rem:platform-bayes}
The platform of Appendix~\ref{app:platform} is a \emph{distinct} source
of persistence from the bilateral mechanism---it operates even where
bilateral encounters alone would not sustain a boundary---but it is not
independent of the \emph{updating rule}: both presuppose Jeffrey
rigidity. In the Bayesian regime $\rho < \rho^*$ the dynamics have a
unique stable fixed point at $\bar{c}_g = 1$ and no interior tipping
point $\bar{c}_g^{\mathrm{mid}}$. Host destabilisation
(Proposition~\ref{prop:spillover}) then has no threshold to cross: the
critical admitted fraction $\eta_{\mathrm{crit}}$ is undefined, and no
level of out-group admission triggers a closure episode. The
shadow-market and tokenism compositions
(Propositions~\ref{prop:shadow}--\ref{prop:tokenism}), no longer
protected by a rigid $F_{g}$, unwind as counter-examples erode the
prior. What survives at $\rho < \rho^*$ is only the single-period motive
to sort by expected class---the platform still reads markers---which
produces no self-sustaining boundary. The platform's distinctive
contribution, institutionalising the trap at scale, is therefore
confined to the Jeffrey regime $\rho > \rho^*$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Individual resolution is orthogonal to rigidity]
\label{rem:resolution}
Suppose the observer additionally receives a direct, noisy signal of the
partner's class, $y = c_i + \varepsilon$ with precision $\tau \ge 0$, and
forms a precision-weighted posterior alongside the group prior. The
parameter $\tau$ indexes how far the encounter resolves the individual
rather than the category: at $\tau = 0$ the posterior is the group mean
$\bar{c}_g$ and the decision is wholesale---the group clears the
engagement threshold or none of it does---while as $\tau \to \infty$ the
partner is judged on $c_i$ alone. Crucially, $\tau$ and $\rho$ act on
different margins: resolution operates at the \emph{decision} step (whom
to engage in this encounter), rigidity at the \emph{updating} step
(whether what is observed revises $\bar{c}_g$). They are independent---an
observer may resolve the individual perfectly, engage them, and still
hold $F_g$ fixed: the exception engaged, the standing untouched. The
closure trap is therefore a property of $\rho$, not of $\tau$, and
survives any degree of resolution; raising $\tau$ rescues individuals
without correcting the group. Institutional aggregation is the
$\tau = 0$ corner---a platform that sorts on markers never meets the
person---so the channel of Appendix~\ref{app:platform} is the limit in
which no individual resolution is available, and sustained personal
contact is the $\tau > 0$ interior. The same reading micro-founds
Appendix~\ref{app:passing}: when $\tau > 0$ the resolved high-class
members are the ones engaged, and their selective exit lowers the
visible mean of those who remain, so sharper resolution can deepen the
residual penalty rather than relieve it. The correction the group needs
acts on $\rho$, not on $\tau$.
\end{remark}

\section{Intergenerational Persistence (\texorpdfstring{$T^*$}{T*})}
\label{app:Tstar}

Throughout this appendix, $\bar{c}_g$ is the group prior (within-group
mean of class outcomes), $\gamma$ is the within-group mobility
parameter, $\gamma^*$ is the saddle-node bifurcation threshold above
which the closure trap dissolves, $\bar{c}_g^*$ denotes the
fixed point of the composed map at the bifurcation, and
$\Psi(\bar{c}_g) = \mathcal{H}(\bar{c}_g; s_g) - \bar{c}_g$ is the
residual whose zeros are the fixed points.

\begin{corollary}[Critical slowing near the bifurcation]
\label{cor:Tstar}
Near $\gamma = \gamma^*$, the number of generations required to
escape the closure trap from initial position $\bar{c}_g^0$ scales as:
\begin{equation}
  T^*(\gamma) \sim C \cdot (\gamma - \gamma^*)^{-1/2}
  \quad \text{as } \gamma \to \gamma^{*+}
  \label{eq:Tstar}
\end{equation}
where $C = \pi / \sqrt{|a \cdot b|}$, with $a = \partial \Psi /
\partial \gamma$ and $b = \frac{1}{2}\partial^2 \Psi / \partial
\bar{c}_g^2$ evaluated at the bifurcation point.
\end{corollary}

The $-1/2$ exponent is the universal scaling of saddle-node ghost
passage times. It follows from the normal form of the bifurcation:
$\Psi \approx a(\gamma - \gamma^*) + b(\bar{c}_g - \bar{c}_g^*)^2$ near the
collision point. The map spends $O((\gamma - \gamma^*)^{-1/2})$
iterations in the ghost region where $|\Psi|$ is $O(\gamma - \gamma^*)$.

\textbf{Empirical prediction.} A programme raising $\gamma$ by $0.01$
above $\gamma^*$ requires approximately 65 generations to achieve
integration---multiple human lifetimes. The distribution of
convergence times across groups should follow a power law with
exponent $-1/2$ near the bifurcation. European Social Survey (ESS)
repeated cross-sections could test this at the country level.

\section{Beta--De~Finetti Structure of Within-Group Heterogeneity}
\label{app:bernoulli}

Recall that $\bar{c}_g$ denotes the within-group mean of class
outcomes for group $g$. The group prior $\bar{c}_g$ defined in
(\ref{eq:prior}) is the mean
of within-group class outcomes; the dynamic system
(\ref{eq:dynamic}) also requires a structural form for the
within-group variance. We obtain that form by treating individual
class outcomes within a group as exchangeable $[0,1]$-valued random
variables. The De~Finetti representation theorem
\citep{DeFinetti1964} then guarantees a mixing measure over possible
group means. The natural parametric family is the Beta distribution
$\mathrm{Beta}(\bar{c}_g \nu,\,(1 - \bar{c}_g)\nu)$ with mean equal
to the group prior $\bar{c}_g$ and variance
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_g^2 = \frac{\bar{c}_g(1 - \bar{c}_g)}{1 + \nu}
  \label{eq:beta-var}
\end{equation}
where $\nu > 0$ is a concentration parameter governing within-group
heterogeneity. As $\nu \to 0$, the Beta distribution concentrates
on $\{0,1\}$ and the model reduces to the Bernoulli special case
with variance $\bar{c}_g(1-\bar{c}_g)$, which is the parametrisation
adopted throughout the paper. This variance plays a central role in
the intergenerational dynamics
(Section~\ref{subsec:intergenerational}).
