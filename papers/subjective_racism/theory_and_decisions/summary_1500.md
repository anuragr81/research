# Stereotype Rigidity in Social Inferences: A "Mimic Men" Dilemma

**Executive Summary**

Anurag Srivastava · Riskcare Ltd., London · University of Reading

---

The persistence of social prejudices has become more divisive and controversial. Despite clear achievements of the decades of legal equality, group-level economic gaps do remain persistent. The stagnation of racial earnings convergence since the late 1970s despite continued individual advancement is a canonical example: conditional on parental income, African Americans receive incomes substantially below those of whites, and this intergenerational gap has not diminished appreciably over two decades (Loury 2002, Bowles et al. 2014). While members of stigmatised groups do routinely invest in host-group markers — credentials, accent, dress, consumption patterns — the group-level economic positions do not seem to change significantly. Like Naipaul's Ralph Singh, the member of the stigmatised group invests much in metropolitan markers but is only personally reclassified while his group is not. This paper delves into the reasons for this persistence and asks two questions. First, why do individual investments fail to deliver group-level mobility? Second, under what structural conditions do integration programmes or affirmative actions durably eliminate the economic gap — and when do they fail?

The model extends Loury's (2002) distinction between "discrimination in contract" (formal, labour-market) and "discrimination in contact" (informal, social). We argue that when people decide whether to engage socially with someone they have just encountered, they do not have access to that person's economic record. Instead, they read soft markers: accent, dress, name, neighbourhood, consumption patterns, phenotype. The paper argues that soft markers are non-certifiable evidence: an accent, item of dress, or phenotype does not, of itself, certify any sentence on which standard Bayesian conditioning could operate. What such evidence does instead is shift credence over the group partition while leaving the conditional class distributions untouched. Jeffrey conditioning is the appropriate updating rule for evidence of this kind. Under it, the observer reclassifies the individual without revising the group-level prior, and the equilibrium that emerges allows a high-class member of a stigmatised group to be absorbed as an exception while the group stereotype persists. The scope of the paper is normative: rather than identifying the psychological mechanism governing such behaviours, the paper investigates the welfare outcomes and, in particular, the rigidity that arises in an environment where participants operate on soft evidence.

The paper restricts its scope to cases where economic class does generate social clustering. Arguably, these environments have been created largely since legal equality has been achieved. In the market of soft signals that still indicate group membership, Jeffrey updating rule generates a self-sustaining closure trap as a stable equilibrium outcome even in a society with no racial divisions. The trap is path-dependent, compounds across generations, and is resistant to individual counter-examples by construction. Racial discrimination enters as a perturbation of this already-operating class-based equilibrium — a group whose economic history is encoded as a low prior — not as a separate phenomenon requiring separate explanation.

The first question is answered by the rigidity condition. Individual marker investment produces personal reclassification but cannot aggregate into group prior revision, regardless of volume. The compliance route is not merely ineffective at the group level — it is actively harmful. Because the individuals most able to comply are positively selected (highest class, lowest natural salience), their departure lowers the mean class of the remaining visible group through adverse selection. The compliance route is collectively destructive, not just collectively ineffective: the individually rational strategy competes for the same scarce resources as the collectively necessary one (structural mobility investment), and the mechanism ensures that resources directed toward individual compliance are wasted from the group's perspective even though they are individually rewarded.

The discrimination mechanism has two sufficient statistics. Marker salience governs the strength of the Jeffrey revision — how strongly the observer attributes the encountered individual to the out-group upon observing their markers. Not all markers are equally visible or persistent. Accent and dress are mutable: they can converge to host-group norms within a generation. Phenotypic markers — skin colour, facial structure — are immutable. The group prior is the sediment of the group's economic history across generations — what the Jeffrey revision maps to once it fires. Because these two statistics enter the mechanism at different points, the instruments that affect each are structurally different and neither can compensate for the other. Reducing marker visibility leaves the group prior untouched. Raising intergenerational mobility shifts the group prior but does not affect marker visibility.

This two-dimensional structure explains observed variation in integration speed. The Irish in England had a history of famine, colonial extraction, and armed conflict comparable to many Commonwealth immigrant groups, yet integrated within two generations because low phenotypic salience meant the Jeffrey revision fired weakly. Immigrants from far-away continents, with high phenotypic salience, remained in the closure trap under the same institutional environment. The critical mobility threshold is strictly increasing in marker salience, so groups with immutable markers need strictly more intergenerational mobility to achieve integration.

The model develops a platform channel showing how institutional factors aggregate individual biases at scale. Profit-maximising platforms — restaurants, schools, rental markets, neighbourhoods — calibrate atmosphere to attract preferred compositions using legally invisible instruments: pricing, location, social codes. Non-discriminatory social environments, when combined with profit incentives acting on soft signals, produce discriminatory compositions as an emergent outcome without any agent intending to discriminate. Anti-discrimination law addresses Loury's contracting channel directly; the model formalises why the same law cannot reach the contact channel of soft signals, where the Jeffrey-updated belief structure runs through markers that legal instruments cannot directly constrain.

The second question is answered by the bifurcation result. Prior threshold results for the contracting channel — showing that temporary intervention can shift labour-market equilibria or that sufficient integration can destabilise group inequality sustained by peer effects on human capital costs (Coate and Loury 1993, Chaudhuri and Sethi 2008, Bowles et al. 2014, Miller 2017) — do not deal with the contact channel. The current paper contributes a threshold condition on intergenerational mobility for this contact channel. There exists a critical level of intergenerational mobility above which the closure trap is permanently eliminated through a saddle-node bifurcation. Above this threshold, the group converges from any starting position without requiring integration. Below it, integration can shift a cohort above the tipping point, but the trap regenerates for subsequent cohorts. The two instruments — integration and mobility investment — are complements in the sub-threshold regime: integration manages cohort positions while mobility investment pushes toward the threshold. Above the threshold, mobility is sufficient on its own and integration is no longer required. Integration without mobility leaves the trap intact for future cohorts.

The model generates a distinctive empirical prediction: escape time from the closure trap diverges as a power law near the bifurcation threshold, producing non-linear convergence dynamics observationally distinguishable from the exponential convergence predicted by standard linear mobility models. The model does not advise the oppressed on how to conduct their struggles. Its contribution is positive-analytical: given that social interaction is soft-signal-mediated and Jeffrey conditioning is the theoretically appropriate updating rule for soft evidence, these are the structural properties of boundary persistence, these are the limits of specific formal instruments, and this is why individually rational compliance is collectively self-defeating while structural mobility investment above the threshold is the only route to permanent trap elimination.
