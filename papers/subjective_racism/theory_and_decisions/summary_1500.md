# Discrimination from Soft Signals: Dilemmas of Mimic Men

**Executive Summary**

Anurag Srivastava · Riskcare Ltd., London · University of Reading

---

Social boundaries seem to have persisted in spite of individual counter-examples, legal equality, and formal anti-discrimination instruments. A prominent economic framework for understanding this persistence is Loury (2002), which distinguishes "discrimination in contract" (formal, labour-market discrimination) from "discrimination in contact" (informal, social discrimination) and argues that the latter sustains racial inequality even after the former is removed. This paper extends Loury's framework to explain how non-discriminatory social environments amidst profit incentives could together shape social discrimination in a market of soft signals.

When people decide whether to engage socially with someone they have just encountered, they do not have access to that person's economic record. They have access to soft signals: accent, dress, name, neighbourhood, consumption patterns, phenotype. These judgments are not hard boundaries — they do not determine group membership with certainty — but they are still relevant for social interaction. The question this paper asks is: what is the correct formal inference rule for updating beliefs from soft social signals, and what does that rule imply for the persistence of social boundaries across generations?

The paper argues that soft-signal inference is better represented with Jeffrey conditioning rather than standard Bayesian updating. The argument rests on a structural distinction between two systems that interact but move at different speeds, following Cole, Mailath, and Postlewaite (1992). A slow-moving social system — cultural transmission, residential geography, historical experience — produces markers: accents, dress, names, consumption patterns. A mobile economic system determines class: income, wealth, occupation. An individual who achieves upward economic mobility does not shed the markers of their origin group. Because markers are outputs of the social system rather than diagnostic signals of current economic position, they carry no likelihood linking marker to class. The correlation between marker and class is mediated by group: a marker shifts probability mass across groups, and the class expectation follows from the group-level prior — but the marker provides no information that would revise that prior. This is the evidential structure for which Jeffrey conditioning is forced. Under Jeffrey conditioning, the statistical updating revises which group an individual is attributed to, but does not revise the group-level belief itself. A high-class member of a stigmatised group is reclassified as an exception; the group stereotype is untouched. The boundary re-asserts itself not through irrationality but through the inferential structure of soft-signal processing. The phenomenon is recognised outside economics — Naipaul's mimic man invests everything in metropolitan markers and is personally reclassified while his group is not; Fanon's colonised subject discovers that the white mask does not revise the white gaze — but what these writers identified as a dilemma, the model derives as an equilibrium outcome.

The starting point of the model is an observation, not a result: economic class generates social clustering. The model proves something different and stronger. The Jeffrey updating rule generates a self-sustaining closure trap as a stable equilibrium outcome even in a society with no racial divisions. The trap is path-dependent, compounds across generations, and is resistant to individual counter-examples by construction. Racial discrimination enters as a perturbation of this already-operating class-based equilibrium — a group whose economic history is encoded as a low prior — not as a separate phenomenon requiring separate explanation.

The discrimination mechanism has two governing parameters, corresponding to the spatial and temporal dimensions of social observation. Marker salience (the spatial dimension) governs the strength of the Jeffrey revision — how strongly the observer attributes the encountered individual to the out-group upon observing their markers. Not all markers are equally visible or persistent. Accent and dress are mutable: they can converge to host-group norms within a generation. Phenotypic markers — skin colour, facial structure — are immutable. Marker salience is a property of the marker-observer interaction, not an intrinsic property of the group. The group prior (the temporal dimension) is the sediment of the group's economic history across generations — what the Jeffrey revision maps to once it fires. Because these two parameters enter the mechanism at different points, the instruments that affect each are structurally different and neither can compensate for the other. Reducing marker visibility — whether through individual assimilation or collective policy such as anonymisation — leaves the group prior untouched. Raising intergenerational mobility shifts the group prior toward the host mean but does not affect marker visibility. Both may be necessary; neither alone is sufficient.

The model establishes a threshold level of intergenerational economic mobility below which the closure trap persists permanently and above which it is eliminated through a saddle-node bifurcation. The threshold is strictly increasing in marker salience, so groups with immutable markers need more intergenerational mobility to achieve integration than groups with mutable markers. This explains why phenotypic proximity to the host group seems to dominate conflict history in determining integration speed: the Irish in England had a history of famine, colonial extraction, and armed conflict comparable to many Commonwealth immigrant groups, yet integrated within two generations because low phenotypic salience meant the Jeffrey revision fired weakly. Indian and Kenyan immigrants, with high phenotypic salience, remained in the closure trap under the same institutional environment.

The compliance analysis goes beyond the rigidity condition. The model shows that individual "passing" — reducing one's personal marker salience through name changes, accent modification, cultural adoption — is not merely futile at the group level but actively harmful. Because the individuals most able to pass are positively selected (highest class, lowest natural salience), their departure lowers the mean class of the remaining visible group through adverse selection. The compliance route is collectively destructive, not just collectively ineffective. This sharpens the central dilemma: the individually rational strategy competes for the same scarce resources as the collectively necessary one (structural mobility investment), and the mechanism ensures that resources directed toward individual compliance are wasted from the group's perspective even though they are individually rewarded.

When authorities forcibly suppress markers — through legal restriction or institutional anonymisation — the model predicts that discrimination reallocates to unsuppressed markers rather than disappearing. Partial suppression is marker reallocation, not elimination. When suppression is lifted, the accumulated temporal pressure — the group prior has not converged during the suppression period — meets a suddenly expanded marker space. Stratification snaps back immediately unless the group prior has crossed the tipping point during suppression.

These effects play into the behaviour of profit-maximising platforms — restaurants, schools, rental markets, neighbourhoods — which calibrate atmosphere to attract preferred compositions using legally invisible instruments: pricing, location, social codes. Non-discriminatory social environments, when combined with profit incentives acting on soft signals, could produce discriminatory compositions as an emergent outcome without any agent intending to discriminate. Anti-discrimination law addresses Loury's contracting channel directly; the model formalises why the same law cannot reach the contact channel of soft signals, where the Jeffrey-updated belief structure runs through markers that legal instruments cannot directly constrain. The contact channel calls for a different instrument — mobility investment operating on the intergenerational class distribution. Law and mobility are complements: necessary in their respective channels, neither sufficient on its own. The model also identifies two further configurations of interest: language revival movements that deliberately increase marker salience, which the model predicts is rational only when the group's economic position is already above the tipping point; and anti-colonial movements as the most important historical examples of collective coordination on mobility investment rather than individual compliance.

The exercise is positive, but its payoff is a sharp statement about which policy instruments can work and which cannot. The model locates the operative margin not in the marker — which is fixed, and for phenotypic markers immutable — but in the group prior the marker points to, which evolves with intergenerational mobility. The effective lever was therefore never the marker but the economic reality it encodes, and the bifurcation result identifies the precise threshold beyond which acting on that lever is durable rather than temporary.

The model generates a distinctive empirical prediction: escape time from the closure trap diverges as a power law near the bifurcation threshold, producing non-linear convergence dynamics observationally distinguishable from the exponential convergence predicted by standard linear mobility models. The model does not advise the oppressed on how to conduct their struggles. Its contribution is positive-analytical: given that social interaction is soft-signal-mediated and Jeffrey conditioning is the theoretically natural updating rule, these are the structural properties of boundary persistence, these are the limits of specific formal instruments, and this is why non-discriminatory environments can produce discriminatory outcomes when profit incentives act on soft signals.
