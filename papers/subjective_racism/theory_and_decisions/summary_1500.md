# Discrimination from Soft Signals: Dilemmas of Mimic Men

**Executive Summary**

Anurag Srivastava · Riskcare Ltd., London · University of Reading

---

## 1. Introduction

Despite the strides made in legal equality across societies, not only do social boundaries persist, but the social classes they sustain often translate into divergent economic outcomes. This paper argues that despite the presumption that less segregated societies dissolve discrimination, the persistence of a market of soft signals in everyday social contact may be sufficient to create a self-sustaining closure trap that underpins the varied economic outcomes sustained through social boundaries. The paper shows — without relying on the contracting-channel machinery of existing models, neither discriminatory preferences nor strategic interaction through the labour market — that the inescapable processing of soft signals may be enough to maintain the closure trap, and that the conditions supposed to dissolve discrimination — integration, social mixing, removal of formal barriers — may be the very conditions that expose markers to an updating mechanism that sustains it.

The scope of the paper is limited to situations where economic stratification does create social clustering. In other words, we assume that people of different economic background are more likely to choose social connections with those in a similar economic cross-section of society. Soft signals through markers such as accent, language, or dress codes become relevant simply because income is seldom directly observed. Since the inference is soft — rather than a definite association with an economic class — the appropriate updating rule is Jeffrey conditioning rather than Bayesian updating. The paper then derives conditions for the closure trap, which is path-dependent, compounds across generations, and is resistant to individual counter-examples by construction. The participant in the market of soft signals is more likely to be in the same trap as Naipaul's Ralph Singh — who invests much in metropolitan markers but is only personally reclassified while his group is not.

Racial discrimination enters as a perturbation of this already-operating class-based equilibrium — a group whose economic history is encoded as a low prior — not as a separate phenomenon requiring separate explanation. Beyond the trap itself, the paper establishes a normative result: the welfare loss it generates contains a component — the informational loss from holding beliefs rigid — that no policy can remove without altering the soft-signal character of social interaction itself. This irreducibility is what distinguishes the contact channel from the contracting channel normatively, not merely descriptively.

## 2. Related Literature

Existing models of persistent discrimination — notably Coate and Loury (1993) — derive self-confirming stereotypes from strategic feedback under Bayesian employers on the labour-market contracting channel: employer beliefs lower the targeted group's incentive to invest in skills, which confirms the beliefs. Loury's (2002) Du Bois lectures distinguish "discrimination in contract" (formal, labour-market) from "discrimination in contact" (informal, social); his formal work with Coate addresses the contracting channel, but the contact channel remains without a formal model.

The mechanism here is embedded in a family indexed by the rigidity of belief updating. At the lower limit, updating is fully Bayesian and the model reduces to the standard statistical-discrimination benchmark (Phelps 1972, Arrow 1973): individual counter-examples erode stereotypes and no closure trap exists. Once rigidity passes a critical level, the closure trap emerges, producing the same self-confirming persistence as Coate and Loury's patronising equilibrium, but through Jeffrey rigidity on the contact channel rather than strategic feedback on the contracting channel.

Chaudhuri and Sethi (2008) ask a closely related question — whether integration eliminates stereotypes — within a contracting-channel model where neighbourhood peer effects shift the costs of human capital acquisition; in their setting integration can eliminate the discriminatory equilibrium but with ambiguous welfare consequences, raising or lowering skill levels depending on the curvature of peer effects. Their signal is a noisy but likelihood-bearing test of qualification, for which Bayesian updating is appropriate. The present model differs in signal type, not just channel: soft markers carry no direct likelihood linking them to class, which is precisely why Jeffrey conditioning rather than Bayes is the operative rule, and why the contact-channel trap persists even where their contracting-channel conditions for eliminating stereotypes are met.

## 3. The Base Model

### 3.1 Primitives

Distance between people of different economic standing has two structural sources, neither of which requires anyone to assess another's economic position. The first is economic: budget constraints filter *sustained* association rather than initial contact. A person of limited means can enter an expensive venue and strike up a conversation with anyone present — both have engaged — but cannot sustain that association as the budget runs out. Sorting by economic standing operates through the affordability of continued interaction, not through anyone reading anyone's economic record. The second source is social, and follows Cole, Mailath, and Postlewaite (1992): soft markers — accent, dress, name, neighbourhood, consumption patterns, phenotype — generate distance independently of economics.

The social system operates through a structural distinction between two systems that interact but move at different speeds. A slow-moving social system — cultural transmission, residential geography, historical experience — produces markers. At the population level, endogenous parental socialisation sustains heterogeneous trait distributions across groups (Bisin and Verdier 2001); at the individual level, observable markers are durable dispositions resistant to economic mobility (Bourdieu 1984). A mobile economic system determines class: income, wealth, occupation. An individual who achieves upward economic mobility does not shed the markers of their origin group — the predicament of the "mimic man" of the title.

### 3.2 Social engagement and the closure equilibrium

Because markers are outputs of the social system rather than diagnostic signals of current economic position, their evidential channel runs through group membership rather than directly to class. At a networking event, you hear a regional accent. It shifts your belief about where the speaker is from. You cannot observe their economic position directly, so your willingness to engage depends on what you believe about the economic profile of people from that region — and the accent itself tells you nothing that would revise that belief. The marker shifts probability mass across groups, and the class expectation follows from the group-level prior held fixed. This is the evidential structure for which Jeffrey conditioning is the unique coherent updating rule (Diaconis and Zabell 1982).

Under Jeffrey conditioning, a high-class member of a stigmatised group is reclassified as an exception; the group stereotype is untouched. Under standard Bayesian updating, the same counter-example would revise the group prior upward — each successful individual would incrementally erode the stereotype, and over time the boundary would dissolve. The difference between the two rules is not a modelling preference: it is forced by the evidential structure of soft signals. The result is a self-sustaining closure trap, stable even in a society with no racial divisions.

### 3.3 Comparative statics and the mobility threshold

The discrimination mechanism has two governing parameters, corresponding to the spatial and temporal dimensions of social observation. Marker salience (the spatial dimension) governs the strength of the Jeffrey revision — how strongly the observer attributes the encountered individual to the out-group. Accent and dress are mutable and can converge to host-group norms within a generation; phenotypic markers — skin colour, facial structure — are immutable. Marker salience is a property of the marker–observer interaction, not an intrinsic property of the group. The group prior (the temporal dimension) is the sediment of the group's economic history across generations — what the Jeffrey revision maps to once it fires. Because these two parameters enter at different points, they require structurally different instruments and neither can compensate for the other.

The model establishes a threshold level of intergenerational mobility below which the closure trap persists permanently and above which it is eliminated through a saddle-node bifurcation. The threshold is strictly increasing in marker salience, so groups with immutable markers require strictly more mobility to escape than groups with mutable markers. This resolves the puzzle of why phenotypic proximity to the host group dominates severity of historical conflict in determining integration speed: migrants from Ireland as well as India may have had a history of famine, colonial extraction, and armed conflict, but different phenotypic salience means a differential rate of assimilation of the respective migrant communities.

## 4. Institutional Aggregation: The Second Source of Persistence

### 4.1 Platform model

The base model locates the first source of persistence at the individual level: the rigidity condition ensures that compliance does not aggregate into group prior revision. But there is also a second, prominent and independent source operating at the institutional level. Profit-maximising platforms — restaurants, schools, neighbourhoods — calibrate atmosphere to attract preferred compositions using legally invisible instruments: pricing, location, dress codes, social network filtering. The same Jeffrey mechanism that operates in bilateral encounters can thus operate at institutional scale, producing discriminatory compositions as an emergent outcome without any agent holding discriminatory preferences. The platform's profit-maximising admission policy generates a tokenism equilibrium: members of the stigmatised group are admitted before social integration is achieved, producing visible inclusion without underlying belief revision. When anti-discrimination law constrains formal screening, a shadow information market emerges whose intensity rises with legal strictness — the law constrains the contracting channel but leaves the contact channel unconstrained, and activity reorganises accordingly. The two sources are analytically independent: even if bilateral encounters did not generate rigidity, institutional aggregation of soft-signal inference would produce discriminatory outcomes on its own.

### 4.2 Welfare analysis

The welfare consequences are not uniform across the sources of persistence. The loss the trap generates can be decomposed, but its decisive feature is a single component: the informational loss that arises because Jeffrey rigidity holds the group prior fixed where Bayesian updating would have revised it. This component is structurally irreducible — no policy can remove it without changing the soft-signal character of social interaction itself. The allocative loss from foregone beneficial encounters, the dynamic loss from intergenerational compounding, and the loss from the platform shadow market can in principle be targeted by instruments aimed at each; the informational loss cannot. This is the sense in which the contact channel sets a floor on what any contracting-channel remedy can achieve.

## 5. Discussion and Worked Examples

The discussion develops worked examples in both causal directions — economic incentives that produce social conflict, and social conflict reproducing economic outcomes — and then turns to strategic action over the parameters. The compliance analysis shows that individual "passing" — reducing one's personal marker salience — is not merely futile at the group level but actively harmful: because the individuals most able to pass are positively selected, their departure lowers the mean class of the remaining visible group through adverse selection. The individually rational compliance strategy competes for the same scarce resources as the collectively necessary one (structural mobility investment), and the Jeffrey mechanism ensures that resources directed toward individual compliance are wasted from the group's perspective even though they are individually rewarded. When authorities forcibly suppress markers, the model predicts that discrimination reallocates to unsuppressed markers rather than disappearing; when suppression is lifted, stratification snaps back immediately unless the group prior has crossed the tipping point during suppression.

## 6. Conclusion

Coate and Loury's policy instruments — affirmative action, employer and worker subsidies — address the contracting channel; the closure trap identified here operates on a more general contact channel, encompassing any social encounter where soft signals are read, and requires structural mobility above the critical threshold as a complement. Because the informational loss is irreducible, the two channels are not substitutes: contracting-channel remedies, however effective, cannot reach the floor that soft-signal processing sets.
