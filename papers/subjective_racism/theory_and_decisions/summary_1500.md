# Discrimination from Soft Signals: Dilemmas of Mimic Men

**Executive Summary**

Anurag Srivastava · Riskcare Ltd., London · University of Reading

---

Social boundaries persist despite individual counter-examples, legal equality, and formal anti-discrimination instruments. Existing formal models of self-confirming stereotypes — notably Coate and Loury (1993) — derive persistent discrimination from strategic belief–investment feedback under Bayesian employers: the discriminatory equilibrium is sustained because employer beliefs lower the targeted group's incentive to invest in skills, which confirms the beliefs. Loury (2002) identifies a broader conceptual programme, distinguishing "discrimination in contract" (formal, labour-market) from "discrimination in contact" (informal, social), and argues that the latter sustains inequality even after the former is removed — but does not formalise the inferential mechanism through which contact-channel discrimination persists. This paper provides that microfoundation.

When people decide whether to engage socially with someone they have just encountered, they do not have access to that person's economic record. They have access to soft signals: accent, dress, name, neighbourhood, consumption patterns, phenotype. These signals do not determine group membership with certainty, but they are relevant for social interaction. The question the paper addresses is: what is the correct formal inference rule for updating beliefs from soft social signals, and what does that rule imply for the persistence of social boundaries across generations?

The mechanism rests on a structural distinction between two systems that interact but move at different speeds, following Cole, Mailath, and Postlewaite (1992). A slow-moving social system — cultural transmission, residential geography, historical experience — produces markers: accents, dress, names, consumption patterns. At the population level, endogenous parental socialisation sustains heterogeneous trait distributions across groups (Bisin and Verdier 2001); at the individual level, observable markers are durable dispositions resistant to economic mobility (Bourdieu 1984). A mobile economic system determines class: income, wealth, occupation. An individual who achieves upward economic mobility does not shed the markers of their origin group — the predicament of the "mimic man" of the title. The phenomenon is recognised outside economics: Naipaul's Ralph Singh invests everything in metropolitan markers and is personally reclassified while his group is not; Fanon's colonised subject discovers that the white mask does not revise the white gaze. What these writers identified as a dilemma, the model derives as an equilibrium outcome.

Because markers are outputs of the social system rather than diagnostic signals of current economic position, they carry no likelihood linking marker to class. The correlation between marker and class is mediated by group: a marker shifts probability mass across groups, and the class expectation follows from the group-level prior — but the marker provides no information that would revise that prior. This is the evidential structure for which Jeffrey conditioning is the unique coherent updating rule (Diaconis and Zabell 1982). Under Jeffrey conditioning, the statistical updating revises which group an individual is attributed to, but does not revise the group-level belief itself. A high-class member of a stigmatised group is reclassified as an exception; the group stereotype is untouched. The boundary re-asserts itself not through irrationality but through the inferential structure of soft-signal processing.

The starting point of the model is an observation, not a result: economic class generates social clustering. The model proves something stronger: the Jeffrey updating rule generates a self-sustaining closure trap as a stable equilibrium outcome even in a society with no racial divisions. The trap is path-dependent, compounds across generations, and is resistant to individual counter-examples by construction. Racial discrimination enters as a perturbation of this already-operating class-based equilibrium — a group whose economic history is encoded as a low prior — not as a separate phenomenon requiring separate explanation.

The discrimination mechanism has two governing parameters, corresponding to the spatial and temporal dimensions of social observation. Marker salience (the spatial dimension) governs the strength of the Jeffrey revision — how strongly the observer attributes the encountered individual to the out-group upon observing their markers. Not all markers are equally visible or persistent. Accent and dress are mutable and can converge to host-group norms within a generation; phenotypic markers — skin colour, facial structure — are immutable. Marker salience is a property of the marker–observer interaction, not an intrinsic property of the group. The group prior (the temporal dimension) is the sediment of the group's economic history across generations — what the Jeffrey revision maps to once it fires. Because these two parameters enter the mechanism at different points, they require structurally different instruments and neither can compensate for the other.

The model establishes a threshold level of intergenerational mobility below which the closure trap persists permanently and above which it is eliminated through a saddle-node bifurcation. The threshold is strictly increasing in marker salience, so groups with immutable markers require strictly more intergenerational mobility to escape the trap than groups with mutable markers. This resolves the puzzle of why phenotypic proximity to the host group dominates severity of historical conflict in determining integration speed: the Irish in England had a history of famine, colonial extraction, and armed conflict comparable to many Commonwealth immigrant groups, yet integrated within two generations because low phenotypic salience meant the Jeffrey revision fired weakly; Indian and Kenyan immigrants, with high phenotypic salience, remained in the closure trap under the same institutional environment.

The base model locates the first source of persistence at the individual level: the rigidity condition ensures that compliance does not aggregate into group prior revision. The paper identifies a second, independent source operating at the institutional level. Profit-maximising platforms — restaurants, schools, neighbourhoods — calibrate atmosphere to attract preferred compositions using legally invisible instruments: pricing, location, dress codes, social network filtering. The same Jeffrey mechanism that operates in bilateral encounters now operates at institutional scale, producing discriminatory compositions as an emergent outcome without any agent holding discriminatory preferences. The platform's profit-maximising admission policy generates a tokenism equilibrium: members of the stigmatised group are admitted before social integration is achieved, producing visible inclusion without underlying belief revision. When anti-discrimination law constrains formal screening, a shadow information market emerges whose intensity rises with legal strictness — the law constrains the contracting channel but leaves the contact channel unconstrained, and activity reorganises accordingly. The two sources of persistence are analytically independent: even if bilateral encounters did not generate rigidity, institutional aggregation of soft-signal inference would produce discriminatory outcomes on its own.

The mechanism is embedded in a one-parameter family indexed by a rigidity parameter ρ. At ρ = 0, the model reduces to the standard-Bayesian benchmark of the statistical-discrimination tradition (Phelps 1972, Arrow 1973): individual counter-examples erode stereotypes and no closure trap exists. At ρ > ρ*, the closure trap emerges — the formal analogue of the self-confirming, patronising equilibrium of Coate and Loury (1993), generated here by Jeffrey rigidity in the updating rule rather than by their belief–investment feedback. The two models share the phenomenon (persistent self-confirming stereotypes) but differ in the micro-mechanism: theirs operates through strategic investment incentives on the contracting channel; ours operates through the structure of evidence on the contact channel.

The compliance analysis shows that individual "passing" — reducing one's personal marker salience — is not merely futile at the group level but actively harmful: because the individuals most able to pass are positively selected, their departure lowers the mean class of the remaining visible group through adverse selection. The individually rational compliance strategy competes for the same scarce resources as the collectively necessary one (structural mobility investment), and the Jeffrey mechanism ensures that resources directed toward individual compliance are wasted from the group's perspective even though they are individually rewarded. When authorities forcibly suppress markers, the model predicts that discrimination reallocates to unsuppressed markers rather than disappearing; when suppression is lifted, stratification snaps back immediately unless the group prior has crossed the tipping point during suppression.

The welfare loss from the two sources of persistence decomposes into four components: allocative (foregone beneficial encounters), informational (Jeffrey rigidity vs. Bayesian counterfactual), dynamic (intergenerational compounding), and platform (shadow information market). The informational component is structurally irreducible within the class of policies that leave the soft-signal character of social interaction intact. As a downstream consequence, anti-discrimination law on the contracting channel and mobility investment on the contact channel are complements: necessary in their respective domains, neither sufficient on its own.
