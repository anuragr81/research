## The study, from its own primitives

1. **The speech community** (pp. 30 to 32). The coherent system belongs to the
   community, not the individual. New York City is "a single speech community,
   united by mutual agreement on the social meaning of linguistic variables, but
   divided by increasingly divergent use of these variables in speech" (p. 30). A
   working class speaker's forms signify that "they are not the forms used by
   middle class speakers" (p. 32).
2. **Linguistic variables and indexes** (pp. 73 to 80). Five variables, chosen as
   frequent, partly immune to conscious suppression and measurable on a linear
   scale. (r) is the percentage of constricted tokens among determinate ones
   (p. 75). (eh) and (oh) are ten times the average value on six-point height
   scales (pp. 76 to 78). (th) and (dh) are 100 times the average value, less 1,
   on a three-point scale from fricative to stop (pp. 79 to 80). The (r-1)
   variant and the fricative are the prestige forms; for (eh) and (oh) low index
   values are high vowels (p. 295), which listeners stigmatise (pp. 438 to 448).
3. **Contextual styles** (pp. 114 to 136). Style A, casual speech, needs one of
   five contexts and one of five channel cues. Style B is careful speech in the
   interview. Style C is reading, D word lists, D′ minimal pairs. In a ten per
   cent sample of interviews, casual speech was 29 per cent of the speech
   recorded (p. 136).
4. **Social class** (pp. 236 to 248). Social stratification is "social
   differentiation and social evaluation" (pp. 87 and 236). The independent
   variable is the Mobilization for Youth socio-economic index (SEC): occupation,
   education and income each ranked I to IV, given equal weight and summed into
   ten classes 0 to 9 (pp. 241 to 244, Table 1). It measures the productive side
   of class, not consumption or style of life (p. 240). Classes 0 to 1 or 0 to 2
   are called lower class, 3 to 4 working class, 6 to 8 lower middle class and 9
   upper middle class (p. 247). A second index, SC, uses the respondent's own
   occupation and education only (pp. 305 to 306).
5. **The samples.** (a) The department store survey of November 1962. 264 sales
   staff in Saks, Macy's and Klein's were asked for a department on the fourth
   floor (pp. 94 to 96). (b) The Lower East Side survey of 1963, a secondary
   survey of the 1961 Mobilization for Youth sample of 988 adults. 122 full
   interviews, 33 short television interviews, 81 adult New Yorkers in the core
   sample (pp. 182 to 233).
6. **Regular structure** (pp. 258 to 263). A regular structure is a regular array
   of order relations between linear sets (styles, classes, index values). Its
   deviation is the share of expected relations that fail. Figure 3 has nine of
   thirty, a deviation of .30, and .50 or more is "not a structure worth
   considering" (p. 260).
7. **Apparent time** (pp. 345 to 358). Age levels are read as real time under
   stated conditions. Case I is a stigmatised feature, Case II a prestige feature,
   Case III change from below, each with and without change in progress.
8. **The subjective reaction test** (pp. 427 to 440, 590). Listeners hear 22
   sentences read by five women from the Lower East Side. They rate each on a
   scale of jobs the speaker could hold "as far as her speech was concerned",
   from television personality down to factory worker and "none of these"
   (LV-Q6, form on p. 433). They are told they are not judging the job the
   speaker holds (LV-Q7). A response is (oh)-negative, (eh)-negative,
   (r)-positive or (th)-sensitive by comparing a speaker's rating on the sentence
   loaded with a variable to her rating elsewhere.
9. **The index of linguistic insecurity** (pp. 177, 473 to 474, 592 to 593). For
   eighteen words heard in two pronunciations, the respondent circles the one he
   calls correct and checks the one he uses. The index is the number of words on
   which the two differ (LV-Q11).
10. **Hypercorrection** (p. 540). The lower middle class outdoes the upper middle
    class in prestige forms in formal styles (LV-Q12, LV-D6).

## Derivation chain of the main results

Every result is an empirical finding from the samples. None is derived from a
model of choice or of equilibrium.

1. **Department stores** (pp. 87 to 113). The stores are ranked by location,
   newspaper advertising, prices and floor space (pp. 90 to 92), not by wages,
   which run Saks below Macy's (p. 92). All or some (r-1) is used by 62, 51 and
   20 per cent of the staff of Saks, Macy's and Klein's (LV-Q2). The order holds
   in each word position, for native white saleswomen alone (n = 141, p. 103),
   for occupations within Macy's (p. 104) and for the upper floors of Saks against
   its ground floor (all (r-1) 34 against 23 per cent, p. 105). The (th) stops in
   "fourth" run 0, 4 and 15 per cent (p. 105). Labov's note gives two routes to
   the result, the store's selection of staff by speech (LV-Q3) and the staff's
   adjustment, after Mills's remark that salespeople borrow prestige from
   customers (pp. 89, 112).
2. **Class stratification** (pp. 248 to 281, Table 4 on p. 249). With classes
   0 to 2, 3 to 5 and 6 to 9, the (r) index in styles A to D′ is
   2.5, 10.5, 14.5, 23.5, 49.5; 4.0, 12.5, 21.0, 35.0, 55.0; and 12.5, 25.0,
   29.0, 55.5, 70.0. (th) in styles A to C is 78.0, 65.0, 43.5; 68.0, 53.5, 27.0;
   and 25.5, 16.5, 10.0. (r) is finely stratified into at least six strata, and
   (th) and (dh) are sharply stratified (pp. 271, 276). Class 9 casual speech has
   (r)-18, "enough to distinguish class 9 speech in every-day life" (p. 269).
3. **Lower middle class cross-over** (pp. 270 to 290). The lower middle class
   goes from (r)-04 in casual speech to (r)-78 in isolated words and passes class 9
   in styles D and D′ (pp. 270, 374). The same cross-over appears for (eh) and
   (oh) (pp. 283 to 286), not for (th) (p. 289), and is read as a sign of change in
   progress and of linguistic insecurity (p. 286).
4. **The inference from speech to class** (pp. 237 to 238, 311 to 313). The
   second of two approaches reads the class of a speaker from his speech (LV-Q4).
   From the distribution of (dh) in careful speech, no stops means some high
   school and an even chance of a professional, and much use means, in two cases
   of three, no high school (LV-Q5, pp. 312 to 313). This is the author's
   inference from his sample, not a measurement of listeners.
5. **Ethnicity and sex** (pp. 320 to 344). Ethnic group separates (eh) and (oh)
   more than class (p. 333). Italians lead in high (eh) and Jews in high (oh).
   Negro respondents do not take part in the (eh) and (oh) structure. Italians
   and Jews follow the same class patterns at different levels (p. 336). Women
   show the wider stylistic shift (p. 339).
6. **Apparent time** (pp. 345 to 402). The stigmatised /Ay/ of "bird" fits Case
   I-B, and (r) fits Case II-B with a break around the Second World War. (eh) and
   (oh) fit change from below, and (th) and (dh) are stable. The department store
   age pattern (p. 107) matches the Lower East Side pattern point by point
   (pp. 377 to 379).
7. **Subjective evaluation** (pp. 427 to 476). The zero pattern ranks the five
   speakers alike for all classes, more steeply for the middle class (LV-Q8,
   Table 1 on p. 437). (oh)-negative response peaks in the lower middle class
   (100 per cent in class 6, p. 441). Every respondent aged 18 to 39 is
   (r)-positive on the two-choice test, against 62 per cent of those over 40
   (Table 11, p. 454, LV-Q9). Those who use a stigmatised form most are most
   sensitive to it (p. 463). The test measures conformity to the common ranking,
   not identification with one's own group (LV-Q10, p. 434).
8. **Linguistic insecurity** (pp. 473 to 475). Index scores of 3 to 7 are held by
   12, 25, 58 and 10 per cent of the SEC groups 0 to 2, 3 to 5, 6 to 8 and 9, and
   the lower middle class "outdistances all others" (LV-Q11, Table 16). Most
   respondents had tried to change their speech and "would consider it a
   compliment to be told that they did not sound like New Yorkers" (p. 475).
9. **Limits** (pp. 146, 554). No New Yorker reached 100 per cent (r-1) in
   conversation, even when trying (LV-Q13). Predictability holds for small groups
   of speakers, not for the individual (LV-Q15).

## What the Lean file formalises

The indexes, the SEC sum and its configurations, the index of linguistic
insecurity, the department store ordering against the wage ordering, the
deviation measure, the cross-over as a failure of class order across styles, and
our reading of the inference from speech to class as Bayes' rule with two
classes. The last is our reading of a stated inference, not a model in the
source.
