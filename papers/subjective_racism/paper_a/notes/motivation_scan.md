# Motivation scan, 15 October 2026

The author asked what research problem Paper A1 solves, and how class matters
in social interaction in the existing literature. Three searches were run on
the web, and two papers were then read in full by hand. Nothing here is a
`lit/` record. Before a manuscript row rests on any source below, that source
needs a full read and a record under `lit/`.

Access marks: **[F]** full text read, **[P]** the quoted passage read in the
source, **[A]** abstract or summary only, **[S]** quoted through a secondary
source only.

Kim and Loury (2012), verbatim: "when group membership is endogenous then the
logic of individuals' identity choices leads there to be a positive selection of
higher ability individuals into the group with a better reputation." Their
setting is wages, so row M14 is a special case of theirs in a contact setting.

Mailath and Postlewaite (2002 WP): agents see income and match on it. An
attribute such as accent gains value through the matching equilibrium, not by
signalling unseen income. The 2006 published version is not read.

## The empirical hook

Chetty et al. (2022, Social Capital II) [F]: "The other half is explained by
friending bias—the tendency for low-SES people to befriend high-SES people at
lower rates even conditional on exposure." And: "Friending bias is also higher
in more racially diverse schools." Their mechanism for friending bias is open.
Their only cost evidence is a suggestion about fraternity dues.

McPherson et al. (2001) [F]: "areas where African Americans are a larger part of
the population also show larger African-American/Anglo differences in
education, income and other social class variables."

Evidence on whether the racial penalty in contact is inferred class is split.
For it are Crabtree et al. (2022) [F] on names read as class, and Laouénan and
Rathelot (2022) [A] on Airbnb. Against it are Hitsch, Hortaçsu and Ariely
(2006 WP) [F] on dating, May and Goldsmith (2018) [A] on nightclub doors, and
Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004) [F] on names in hiring.

## Candidate contributions, none yet checked for novelty

1. **Group avoidance derived from class homophily.** In expected class distance
   the cost of a group is the squared gap between its mean and the observer's
   own class, plus its within-group variance. Avoidance of a group therefore
   depends on the observer's class. Low-class members of the high-standing
   group may prefer outsiders, and a group with more spread is avoided by
   everyone. No source found has this dependence. Pęski and Szentes, Eeckhout,
   Mailath and Postlewaite 2006, and Charles, Hurst and Roussanov need full
   reads before this is claimed.
2. **Budget compatibility as the reason class matters.** No formal model was
   found in which a one-off encounter is affordable but a relationship is not.
   The empirical support is thin: Armstrong and Hamilton (2013) [A], Adams and
   Allan (1998) [S], and the fraternity-dues remark in Chetty et al.
3. **Venue exclusion as the aggregate of client inference, and price screening
   as class certification.** Venue discrimination was not searched directly.
4. **A test of the class channel.** The group penalty in contact should vanish
   when class is shown if and only if inference about class drives it.

## Not new

- Row M14, passing lowers the visible mean, is in Kim and Loury (2012).
- "Soft evidence" and "soft marker" have no formal content once Jeffrey's
  rule is dropped. What remains is a noisy group marker under group
  sufficiency.
