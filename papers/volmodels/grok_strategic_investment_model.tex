\section{Overview and Motivation}

This document presents a complete, self-contained model of strategic investment between two asymmetrically informed players. Each player owns a risky stochastic process and may invest in the other's process for a fee. Players market themselves to attract investment, and beliefs are updated via Jeffrey conditioning applied to a Beta-Binomial model of returns.

The model features a clean microeconomic mechanism for endogenous volatility: when player \(i\) receives investment from player \(j\), the incoming capital multiplicatively augments the growth (drift) base of \(i\)'s process without increasing the volatility borne by \(i\). Player \(i\) bears risk only on their own retained capital \(C_i\), while capturing fee income on the larger asset base. This decoupling of growth benefits from risk costs generates rich strategic feedback into realised portfolio volatility through Jeffrey belief updating and marketing.

The key outputs are:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
  \item Equilibrium allocations \(\alpha_i^*\) and marketing spends \(m_i^*\) in closed form.
  \item Realised portfolio variance derived from the strategic interaction.
  \item A structural GARCH(1,1) representation with endogenous GJR-type asymmetry.
\end{enumerate}

All objects are defined explicitly to permit full symbolic solution in SymPy.

\section{Primitives and Notation}

\subsection{Players and Budgets}

There are two players, indexed \(i \in \{1, 2\}\), with \(j = 3 - i\).

\begin{definition}[Budgets]
Player \(i\) begins with initial wealth \(W_i > 0\) and chooses marketing spend \(m_i \in [0, W_i]\), leaving effective wealth
\[
W_i^{\mathrm{eff}} = W_i - m_i.
\]
\end{definition}

\subsection{Return Processes}

Each period, player \(i\)'s process generates return \(r_i^t = u_i\) with true probability \(p_i\) (known only to \(i\)) or \(d_i\) otherwise, where \(u_i > d_i\). The true mean and variance are
\[
\mu_i = p_i u_i + (1-p_i) d_i, \qquad \sigma_i^2 = p_i(1-p_i)(u_i - d_i)^2.
\]

\subsection{Public History and Beliefs}

Both players observe the same history of \(n\) returns with \(k_i\) ups. The Bayesian posterior is \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_i^* = \alpha_i^0 + k_i, \beta_i^* = \beta_i^0 + n - k_i)\). Marketing by \(i\) generates a Jeffrey shift \(\delta(m_i) = m_i/(m_i + \gamma_i)\), yielding Jeffrey-shifted parameters \(\alpha_i^{**} = \alpha_i^* + \delta(m_i)\), \(\beta_i^{**} = \beta_i^*\) and perceived probability \(\hat{p}_i^j = \alpha_i^{**}/(\alpha_i^{**} + \beta_i^{**})\).

Perceived moments for player \(j\) about \(i\)'s process are
\[
\mu_i^j = \hat{p}_i^j u_i + (1 - \hat{p}_i^j) d_i, \qquad \sigma_i^{j2} = (u_i - d_i)^2 \hat{p}_i^j (1 - \hat{p}_i^j).
\]

\subsection{Player's Investment Choices}

Each player has exactly three uses for their capital and no others:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Own process} (Choice 1): Retain fraction \((1-\alpha_i)\) of \(W_i^{\mathrm{eff}}\) in own process. This capital alone bears true volatility \(\sigma_i^2\).
  \item \textbf{Marketing} (Choice 2): Spend \(m_i\) to shift opponent's beliefs upward, attracting more incoming capital without changing true \(\mu_i\) or \(\sigma_i^2\).
  \item \textbf{Opponent's process} (Choice 3): Allocate fraction \(\alpha_i\) of \(W_i^{\mathrm{eff}}\) to \(j\)'s process, receiving net-of-fee return based on own Jeffrey beliefs.
\end{enumerate}

Incoming investment is accepted passively.

\subsection{Why Jeffrey Conditioning?}

Standard Bayesian updating requires \emph{hard evidence} — an observation that becomes certain once received. Jeffrey conditioning, by contrast, is designed for \emph{soft evidence}: the agent revises their degree of belief without any event being assigned probability one. This distinction is essential for modelling marketing in the present framework.

Jeffrey conditioning therefore provides the precise mechanism needed: it allows marketing to be a \emph{costly, dissipative, soft-evidence signal} that manipulates the opponent's perceived drift and volatility without altering the true stochastic process. This generates the rich endogenous volatility dynamics that are the central contribution of the model.

\begin{definition}[Jeffrey Conditioning — Mathematical Derivation]
Let \(H\) be the hypothesis of interest (here: ``the true up-probability is \(p_i$'') and let the evidence partition the space into \(\{E, \neg E\}\), where \(E\) represents ``favourable evidence for high \(p_i\)''.

Jeffrey's rule of conditioning (Jeffrey, 1965) states that if the agent revises their credence in the evidence from the prior \(P(E)\) to a new value \(q \in (0,1)\), the updated probability of \(H\) is
\[
P'(H) = P(H \mid E) \cdot q + P(H \mid \neg E) \cdot (1-q).
\]
No event is treated as certain; only the weight on \(E\) changes.

In the Beta-Binomial setting of this model:
- The prior/posterior after public history is \(\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha_i^*,\, \beta_i^*)\).
- The mean is \(\hat{p}_i^j = \frac{\alpha_i^*}{\alpha_i^* + \beta_i^*}\).

Marketing supplies soft evidence equivalent to raising the credence in ``up'' by the amount \(\delta(m_i) \in (0,1)\). This is mathematically identical to adding a fractional pseudo-count \(\delta(m_i)\) **only to the up-count** while leaving the down-count unchanged:
\[
\alpha_i^{**} = \alpha_i^* + \delta(m_i), \qquad \beta_i^{**} = \beta_i^*.
\]
The new perceived up-probability becomes
\[
\hat{p}_i^j = \frac{\alpha_i^{**}}{\alpha_i^{**} + \beta_i^{**}} = \frac{\alpha_i^* + \delta(m_i)}{\alpha_i^* + \delta(m_i) + \beta_i^*}.
\]

To see that this is exactly Jeffrey's rule, note that the Beta mean is a weighted average of the conditional means under \(E\) and \(\neg E\). Adding \(\delta(m_i)\) to \(\alpha_i^*\) is equivalent to setting \(q = \frac{\alpha_i^* + \delta(m_i)}{\alpha_i^* + \beta_i^* + \delta(m_i)}\) in the Jeffrey formula above, confirming that the update is a convex combination that shifts credence without treating any new observation as certain.

This derivation holds because the conditional likelihood \(P(r^t = u_i \mid p_i) = p_i\) is unaffected by marketing (rigidity condition).
\end{definition}

\section{Terminal Wealth and Preferences}

\begin{definition}[Exposures]
Define
\begin{align}
  C_i &= (1 - \alpha_i) W_i^{\mathrm{eff}} && \text{(own retained capital that bears risk)}, \\
  A_i &= C_i + f \alpha_j W_j^{\mathrm{eff}} && \text{(drift/growth exposure)}, \\
  B_i &= \alpha_i W_i^{\mathrm{eff}} (1-f) && \text{(risk exposure to opponent's process)}.
\end{align}
Terminal wealth is
\[
W_i(T) = \mu_i A_i + \mu_j^i B_i + C_i \cdot \varepsilon_i + B_i \cdot \tilde{\varepsilon}_j.
\]
\end{definition}

Mean-variance utility:
\[
\mathcal{V}_i = \E[W_i(T)] - \frac{\rho_i}{2} \Var[W_i(T)],
\]
with
\[
\E[W_i(T)] = \mu_i A_i + \mu_j^i B_i, \qquad \Var[W_i(T)] = C_i^2 \sigma_i^2 + B_i^2 \sigma_j^{i2}.
\]

\section{Stackelberg Equilibrium}

Player 1 (leader) moves first choosing \((m_1, \alpha_1)\); player 2 (follower) responds with \((m_2=0, \alpha_2)\).

Useful shorthand:
\[
\Delta_i = \mu_j^i(1-f) - \mu_i, \quad \Sigma_i = \sigma_i^2 + (1-f)^2 \sigma_j^{i2}.
\]

Optimal allocations:
\[
\boxed{\alpha_2^* = \frac{\Delta_2 + \rho_2 \sigma_2^2 C_2}{\rho_2 W_2^{\mathrm{eff}} \Sigma_2}}, \qquad
\boxed{\alpha_1^* = \frac{\Delta_1 + \rho_1 \sigma_1^2 C_1}{\rho_1 W_1^{\mathrm{eff}} \Sigma_1}}.
\]

The leader's optimal marketing \(m_1^*\) retains the square-root closed form derived from \(\partial \mathcal{V}_1 / \partial m_1 = 0\), with the new variance plugged in (explicit expression obtainable symbolically).

Follower sets \(m_2^* = 0\).

\section{Realised Portfolio Volatility}

At equilibrium,
\[
\mathcal{R}_i = C_i^2 \sigma_i^2 + B_i^2 \sigma_j^{i2},
\]
evaluated at \((m_1^*, \alpha_1^*, \alpha_2^*)\). Marketing tends to reduce \(\mathcal{R}_i\) via the protected risk exposure \(C_i\).
