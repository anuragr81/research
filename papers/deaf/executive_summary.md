Financial dealer balance sheets are known to be asymmetric: expansions are fast, turbulent, and high-variance but contractions are slow and compressed. Adrian and Shin (2010, 2014) document this pattern in primary dealer data and attribute it to procyclical leverage under Value at Risk (VaR) constraints. Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) connect it to funding liquidity spirals. Li et al. (2025) confirm it in granular desk-level regulatory data. While the near-permanent volatility persistence — known as the Integrated Generalised Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity (IGARCH) phenomenon (Engle and Bollerslev, 1986) — as well as a higher duration of crises in low-growth environments (Reinhart and Rogoff, 2009) are both well-documented and captured by forecasting models — GARCH variants, Heterogeneous Autoregressive Realised Variance (HAR-RV), stochastic volatility — the literature does not underpin strong structural explanations for what drives this persistence. Existing structural approaches all require something external: reduced-form GARCH needs an auxiliary estimate of the trend growth rate (denoted g) to separate persistence from other sources; rational belief equilibrium models require distributional assumptions on agent beliefs; prospect theory calibrations identify the loss aversion parameter (denoted λ) against macroeconomic moments — equity premia, risk-free rates, consumption processes — that must be taken as given. The result is that λ and g are never jointly identified from the same time series without conditional dependence on untested auxiliary structure. The current paper uses insights on loss aversion from behavioural models to address the uncertainty on how long dealer balance sheet shocks last while identifying the factors that determine the degree of asymmetry in the two regimes.

Using a simple model for loss aversion and a reference level — the economy-wide trend growth benchmark — we find that while persistence is driven independently by trend growth g alone, asymmetry is a direct result of loss aversion levels λ. More specifically, the persistence coefficient β (which measures how quickly balance sheet shocks fade — a β close to one means shocks persist for many quarters, while a β close to zero means they fade quickly) is determined by trend growth through the structural relationship β ≈ 1/(1+g)². The degree of asymmetry is captured by the ratio |γ|/α, where α is the ARCH coefficient (governing the overall sensitivity of current volatility to past shocks in the expansion regime) and γ is the asymmetry coefficient (governing how much that sensitivity differs between expansion and contraction). This ratio is determined by loss aversion through |γ|/α = (λ⁴−1)/λ⁴. As a central result, this independence transforms the variance ratio — defined as the ratio of expansion innovation variance to contraction innovation variance, measuring how many times more volatile expansion is than contraction — into a direct reading of institutional loss aversion, and transforms persistence into a direct reading of the growth environment. Each is identifiable without external instruments. In practitioner terms, the risk committee's loss aversion and the economy's growth rate are two independent dials — one institutional and inside the risk committee's control, the other macroeconomic and outside it — that can be read off the same balance sheet data separately.

To elaborate the loss aversion channel, the paper identifies the risk-limits channel recognised in practitioner literature (Li et al., 2025; Stulz, 2016; Ellul and Yerramilli, 2013). More particularly, we rely on the observation that the risk committee encodes loss aversion in pre-committed position limits while the desk optimises within them. A market maker optimising a standard piecewise linear loss-averse value function generates no equilibrium asymmetry: the loss aversion scalar λ multiplies the objective in the loss region but cancels exactly in the first-order condition, leaving the optimal price impact coefficient unchanged regardless of which side of the reference point the dealer occupies. This means λ is observationally inert in unconstrained optimisation. The model resolves this through pre-committed, regime-contingent constraints on the price impact schedule — the institutional analogue of VaR limits and balance sheet benchmarks. These constraints are the endogenous form that loss aversion must take to remain consequential in a competitive market. The Stackelberg structure of the market — in which the dealer sets prices before the speculator acts — gives them binding force. In expansion, the binding limit posts low price impact, attracting large speculator positions and generating high balance sheet variance. In contraction, the binding limit posts high price impact, compressing both. The limits are log-symmetric around the Kyle (1985) benchmark by exactly log λ, encoding loss aversion as equal proportional departure in each direction.

Separately, we assume a bilateral zero-sum structure between dealer and speculator (Assumption 1), which forces them to opposite sides of the trend benchmark simultaneously — a derived result guaranteeing that the two agents always occupy opposite regimes. This is the engine of the variance asymmetry: regime-specific position-scaling coefficients differ by a factor of λ², producing a variance ratio of λ⁴ between expansion and contraction. Together with the risk-limits channel, trend growth provides deterministic mean-reversion at rate 1/(1+g), determining how quickly shocks fade independently of λ. Asymmetry — how many times more volatile expansion is than contraction — identifies loss aversion. Theorem 1 formalises this as a Threshold-GARCH recursion for the conditional variance of detrended dealer balance sheet deviations, with the ARCH coefficient α and asymmetry coefficient γ identified exactly, and the persistence coefficient β ≈ 1/(1+g)² identified to leading order.

The empirical analysis draws on the Federal Reserve Z.1 Financial Accounts, Table L.130 (Security Brokers and Dealers) — the standard source for aggregate US broker-dealer balance sheet dynamics. The dataset records total financial assets and proprietors' equity of US security brokers and dealers at quarterly frequency from 1952 to 2025 (292 quarters). From these two series we construct leverage (assets divided by equity) and the detrended balance sheet deviation — the state variable of the model. This is the same data source used by Adrian and Shin (2010, 2014) and the intermediary asset pricing literature, and provides the longest available time series for testing the model's structural predictions across multiple growth regimes and one major regulatory break.

The empirical design uses two detrending methods by design, each the theoretically correct specification for its parameter. The variance ratio identifies λ through the difference in the typical size of unexpected balance sheet shocks across regimes: after removing the predictable component of balance sheet movements (the mean-reversion toward trend), the residual shock in each period has a different magnitude depending on whether the dealer is expanding or contracting. In expansion, these shocks are roughly twice as large as in contraction — it is their ratio that identifies loss aversion. The model's reference point is a locally smooth trend — the benchmark against which the risk committee evaluates performance — correctly operationalised by the Hodrick-Prescott (HP) filter (Hodrick and Prescott, 1997; Ravn and Uhlig, 2002 justify the smoothing parameter λ_HP = 1600 for quarterly data). The HP filter is used here for regime classification — identifying whether the dealer is above or below the local trend at each point — not for extracting cycle dynamics, which addresses the concerns raised by Hamilton (2018). Persistence identifies g through the secular deflation factor 1/(1+g) with a quarterly half-life of approximately 350 quarters; the HP filter absorbs this signal entirely, making β unidentifiable under HP detrending. Deterministic detrending of log(equity) at the literature Total Factor Productivity (TFP) growth rate preserves it. Each method recovers one structural channel and is blind to the other. Together they provide the complete identification that no single detrending method can.

The model makes three testable claims. The first is that asymmetry exists and is determined by loss aversion — the answer to the second research question. The second is that persistence is structurally linked to growth — the answer to the first research question. The third is that the two channels are independent — the separation property itself. Each claim is tested by a pair of findings. If the first claim fails, the model's core prediction is wrong. If the second fails, the growth-persistence link is wrong. If the third fails, the two channels are entangled and separate identification is impossible.

*Claim 1 — Asymmetry exists and loss aversion determines it.* Finding 1 establishes that expansion variance exceeds contraction variance with high statistical significance (variance ratio R̂ = 2.07, p < 0.00001). This is the model's most basic prediction: the kink, operating through risk limits, must produce a variance ratio above one. Finding 3 then tests whether asymmetry depends on loss aversion alone or is contaminated by growth: the pre-Global Financial Crisis (GFC) asymmetry ratio is stable across growth regimes (0.42–0.45) despite threefold variation in trend TFP growth (from g = 0.5% in the productivity slowdown to g = 1.5% in the IT boom). If asymmetry depended on g, it would vary with growth; it does not. Together, these two findings establish the asymmetry channel and confirm that the variance ratio reads loss aversion, not growth. The implied loss aversion is λ̂ = 1.20 — meaning the risk committee weights a £1 loss at £1.20, a modest but persistent asymmetry in loss-gain evaluation. This is the first structural market-based estimate of dealer loss aversion, below the Tversky-Kahneman laboratory value of 2.25, consistent with institutional attenuation — the aggregation of individual loss aversion through layers of organisational governance, risk oversight, and competitive pressure. Despite its modest magnitude, this degree of loss aversion is sufficient, channelled through pre-committed VaR limits, to generate the full observed Threshold-GARCH structure.

*Claim 2 — Persistence is structurally linked to growth.* Finding 2 confirms that persistence under deterministic detrending matches the structural prediction within 0.4 percentage points: the estimated autoregressive coefficient φ̂ (phi-hat, measuring the quarter-to-quarter carry-over of balance sheet deviations) is 0.994 against a predicted value of 0.998. A caveat: the HP filter absorbs this signal entirely (φ̂_HP ≈ 0.66), which is why the dual detrending design is necessary — the HP filter is the correct tool for Claim 1 but the wrong tool for Claim 2, and vice versa. Finding 5 tests the model's most extreme prediction: as g approaches zero, β approaches one and shocks become effectively permanent — the IGARCH boundary. The secular stagnation period (g = 0.3%) produces φ̂ = 1.016, at or slightly above unity. The point estimate exceeding one is a finite-sample artefact: in a 60-quarter window near the unit-root boundary, standard Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) estimation is biased upward. The qualitative prediction — the stagnation period exhibits the most persistent dynamics of all subperiods — is confirmed.

*Claim 3 — The two channels are independent.* Finding 4 tests whether a known exogenous shock to one channel leaves the other undisturbed. Post-GFC regulation — Basel III leverage ratios, the Volcker Rule (Dodd-Frank Section 619), and the Comprehensive Capital Analysis and Review / Dodd-Frank Act Stress Testing (CCAR/DFAST) programmes — selectively binds in the expansion regime. Both variances fell post-GFC due to balance sheet deleveraging, but expansion variance compressed more than contraction variance (93% vs 78%, differential 15 percentage points, p < 0.05), consistent with regulation selectively targeting the expansion regime. Under deterministic detrending, persistence takes comparable values across all regimes (φ = 0.974–1.016) while |γ|/α shifts from +0.44 to −0.38. Regulation changed one channel without affecting the other. The paper models this through a one-parameter regulatory floor extension — a price-impact floor that, when binding, dominates the dealer's own loss-aversion-driven limit in expansion. The model predicts that such a floor should affect the asymmetry channel without disturbing the persistence channel; the data confirms exactly this. The pre-GFC estimate (λ̂ = 1.22) matches the full-sample estimate to two decimal places, confirming the structural parameter is stable across the regulatory regime change. Finding 6 provides the strongest test: the model estimated on pre-GFC data alone correctly predicts all three structural features of the post-GFC regime — asymmetric compression, stagnation-period persistence, and the variance ratio sign flip — with zero post-GFC parameters. The model has structural predictive content: it generalises beyond the estimation sample to a regulatory regime it was not designed to explain.

The independence tells regulators and risk managers which toolkit to reach for. Rising asymmetry with stable persistence is institutional: leverage ratios, position limits, and stress testing are the instruments. Falling asymmetry signals dealers underweighting losses — a systemic risk indicator addressable with the same macroprudential toolkit. Rising persistence with stable asymmetry is macroeconomic: monetary policy, fiscal investment, and structural reforms are the instruments — an area where risk limits cannot help. The IGARCH boundary adds a further diagnostic: when estimated persistence approaches one, the model identifies this as the structural signature of near-zero trend growth — a real-time indicator of secular stagnation regimes in which macroprudential tightening alone cannot restore normal balance sheet dynamics.
