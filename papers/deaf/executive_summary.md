Financial dealer balance sheets behave asymmetrically across the business cycle. Expansions are characterised by rapid balance sheet growth, large position sizes, elevated volatility, and turbulent price movements; contractions are slow, compressed, and grinding. Adrian and Shin (2010, 2014) document this pattern in primary dealer data and attribute it to procyclical leverage under VaR constraints. Brunnermeier and Pedersen (2009) connect it to funding liquidity spirals. Li et al. (2025) confirm it in granular desk-level regulatory data. What these accounts share is a description of the phenomenon. What they do not provide — and what no existing model provides — is an identification of the two economic primitives driving it: dealer loss aversion λ and trend growth g. Existing approaches all require something external to achieve this: reduced-form GARCH needs an auxiliary estimate of g to separate persistence from other sources; rational belief equilibrium models require distributional assumptions on the population of agent beliefs; prospect theory calibration identifies λ against macroeconomic moments — equity premia, risk-free rates, consumption processes — that must be taken as given. The result is that λ and g are never jointly identified from the same time series without conditional dependence on untested auxiliary structure. This paper removes that dependence.

Cumulative Prospect Theory is the dominant descriptive model of individual choice under risk — it matches the experimental evidence on loss aversion, probability weighting, and reference dependence that expected utility cannot accommodate, and it is the best-validated preference model in the laboratory. Yet its translation to market equilibria has been problematic: competitive pressure can dilute individual preference parameters, and CPT's piecewise structure means the kink often cancels in interior optimisation, leaving no observable implication in equilibrium prices. The result is a large experimental literature and a large calibration literature, but very little structural market equilibrium work where the loss aversion parameter λ is identified from observed behaviour. It is worth asking whether a simpler utility — CRRA or log — could do the same work. The answer is no, and the reason is precise: CRRA and log utility have no reference point and no kink, so they produce symmetric GARCH dynamics with no regime classification and no preference-theoretic motivation for asymmetric risk limits. VaR limits in rational-agent models are purely exogenous regulatory impositions. CPT uniquely generates the reference point and the kink that give a loss-averse dealer both a reason to pre-commit to asymmetric constraints and a mechanism through which those constraints produce structural variance asymmetry.

We establish first that this pre-commitment insight runs through a precise result: a market maker with a standard piecewise linear loss-averse value function is not sufficient to generate equilibrium asymmetry through unconstrained optimisation. The loss aversion scalar λ multiplies the objective in the loss region but cancels exactly in the first-order condition, leaving the optimal price impact coefficient — the rate at which the market maker moves prices per unit of incoming order flow, which determines both its intermediation revenue and the position size its counterparty will take — unchanged regardless of which side of the reference point the dealer occupies. This means λ is observationally inert in unconstrained optimisation: it scales the objective without shifting the optimum, so the variance ratio between expansion and contraction carries no information about λ. Practitioners recognise this as the distinction between the risk management committee — which sets position limits based on the firm's loss aversion at quarterly or annual frequency — and the trading desk, which optimises within those limits without reference to the firm's preferences in the moment. This result does not challenge the descriptive significance of CPT; rather, it identifies precisely the channel through which loss aversion generates asymmetry in a strategic dealer market. That channel is pre-committed, regime-contingent constraints on the price impact schedule — the institutional analogue of VaR limits documented directly by Adrian and Shin (2014) and Li et al. (2025). This also provides a microfoundation for why such pre-commitment devices exist in dealer institutions in the first place: they are the endogenous form that loss aversion must take to remain consequential in a competitive market, not merely a regulatory imposition on otherwise preference-free agents. The Stackelberg structure of the market — in which the dealer sets prices before the speculator acts — is what gives these constraints binding force: the dealer's pricing power means loss aversion cannot be competed away, as it might in a price-taking market. In expansion, the binding limit posts low price impact, attracting large speculator positions and generating high balance sheet variance. In contraction, the binding limit posts high price impact, compressing both. The limits are log-symmetric around the Kyle (1985) benchmark by exactly log λ, encoding loss aversion as equal proportional departure in each direction.

Two structural conditions convert this mechanism into a theorem rather than an assumption. The bilateral zero-sum structure of the dealer-speculator game (Assumption 1) guarantees that the two agents always occupy opposite sides of the trend benchmark simultaneously — a derived result. This is the engine of the variance asymmetry: regime-specific position-scaling coefficients differ by a factor of λ², producing a variance ratio of λ⁴ between expansion and contraction. The trend growth path then provides deterministic mean-reversion at rate 1/(1+g), pinning the persistence of volatility shocks to the economy's long-run growth rate independently of λ. Together these two conditions deliver an exact orthogonal identification: the asymmetry ratio |γ|/α = (λ⁴−1)/λ⁴ depends only on λ, and the persistence coefficient β ≈ 1/(1+g)² depends only on g, with both cross-derivatives exactly zero. The two primitives are separable from the data without any external structure. Theorem 1 formalises this as a Threshold-GARCH recursion for the conditional variance of detrended dealer balance sheet deviations, with the ARCH coefficient α = Φλ² and asymmetry coefficient γ = Φ(1−λ⁴)/λ² identified exactly, and the persistence coefficient β ≈ 1/(1+g)² identified to leading order.

The practical significance of this identification is direct: once λ and g are separately recoverable from dealer balance sheet data, macroprudential and growth instruments can be calibrated independently — tightening VaR limits targets the asymmetry ratio |γ|/α without touching the persistence coefficient β, while growth-enhancing reforms target β without touching |γ|/α. The two instruments are structurally non-redundant, and their effects are separately attributable from observed volatility dynamics. Achieving this identification requires different detrending methods for the two parameters — a consequence of the model's structure, not an arbitrary choice. The variance ratio identifies λ through regime-dependent innovation variance; the model's reference point is local, correctly operationalised by the Hodrick-Prescott filter. Persistence identifies g through the secular deflation factor 1/(1+g) with a quarterly half-life of approximately 350 quarters; the HP filter absorbs this signal entirely, making β unidentifiable. Deterministic detrending of log(equity) at the literature TFP growth rate preserves it. Each method is the theoretically correct specification for its parameter, and together they provide the complete identification that no single detrending method can.

Using the Federal Reserve Z.1 Financial Accounts, Table L.130 (Security Brokers and Dealers) — the standard source for US broker-dealer balance sheet dynamics at quarterly frequency from 1952 to 2025 — five empirical findings confirm the model. Expansion variance exceeds contraction variance with high statistical significance (R = 2.07, p < 0.00001); persistence is confirmed within 0.4 percentage points of the structural prediction under deterministic detrending; the pre-GFC asymmetry ratio is stable across growth regimes despite threefold variation in trend TFP growth; a post-GFC sign reversal is explained by a one-parameter regulatory extension that preserves the separation property; and near-unit-root persistence in the stagnation period is consistent with the IGARCH boundary. The implied loss aversion parameter λ = 1.20 — below the Tversky-Kahneman laboratory value of 2.25, consistent with institutional attenuation — is the first market-based structural estimate of dealer loss aversion, confirming that CPT's behavioural parameter leaves a measurable trace in market data even after aggregation and regulatory mediation. In detail: under HP filtering, expansion innovation variance exceeds contraction innovation variance (R = 2.07, F-test p < 0.00001). Under deterministic detrending, the pooled AR(1) coefficient is φ = 0.994 against a model prediction of 0.998 — a discrepancy of 0.4 percentage points, representing a 99% reduction from the 33 percentage points under HP filtering. The regime-specific coefficients (φ⁺ = 0.992, φ⁻ = 0.997) confirm the deflation factor is regime-invariant. Partitioning the pre-GFC data into the productivity slowdown (1974–1994, g = 0.5%) and the IT boom (1995–2007, g = 1.5%), the asymmetry ratio is 0.45 and 0.42 respectively — within 3 percentage points despite threefold variation in trend TFP growth.

The secular stagnation period (2010–2024) produces a sign reversal under HP filtering: R = 0.72, with contraction variance now exceeding expansion variance. Rather than contradicting the model, this finding extends it. Post-GFC regulation — Basel III leverage ratios, the Volcker Rule, and CCAR stress testing — selectively binds in the expansion regime, compressing expansion variance while leaving contraction variance unchanged. The paper captures this through a one-parameter regulatory floor extension: a price-impact floor λ_reg that, when binding, dominates the dealer's own loss-aversion-driven limit in expansion. The separation property is preserved under this extension because the regulatory floor enters through the variance coefficients α and γ but not through the persistence coefficient β. The data confirms exactly this: under deterministic detrending, persistence takes comparable values across all regimes (φ = 0.974–1.016) while |γ|/α shifts from +0.44 to −0.38. Regulation changed one channel without affecting the other. This post-GFC structural break is therefore a natural experiment in the separation property's favour — one that makes it harder, not easier, to dismiss the orthogonal identification as a statistical coincidence. In the same stagnation period, near-unit-root persistence (φ = 1.016) is consistent with the IGARCH boundary prediction: as the trend growth rate g approaches zero, the persistence coefficient β approaches one, and volatility shocks become permanent.
This paper demonstrates that CPT is not observationally irrelevant in markets — it is observationally relevant through a specific institutional channel and in a specific data object: the Threshold-GARCH structure of dealer balance sheet deviations. The implied λ = 1.20 is not a calibrated parameter matched to external moments; it is recovered from the variance ratio without auxiliary inputs, representing the first structural identification of CPT's loss aversion parameter from market equilibrium behaviour. This connects the experimental programme of behavioural economics — where CPT is well-validated but confined to the laboratory — directly to the empirical finance literature, where loss aversion has remained difficult to identify cleanly. 

The model delivers structural implications for both institutional behaviour and policy that follow from this vindication. For institutions, the separation property implies that the two components of dealer balance sheet volatility respond to fundamentally different forces and should be monitored separately. A dealer observing rising |γ|/α without a change in β is experiencing a shift in effective loss aversion — whether from internal stress, changing risk appetite, or regulatory pressure; a rise in β without a change in |γ|/α signals a deterioration in the growth environment. The model also predicts cross-sectional variation in institutional structure: concentrated dealer markets with infrequent limit resets should exhibit stronger variance asymmetry than fragmented markets with frequent resets, and post-GFC regulatory homogenisation — which replaced idiosyncratic loss-aversion-driven limits with a common regulatory floor — should have compressed cross-sectional dispersion in dealer price impact relative to the pre-GFC period. For policy, the regulatory break finding operationalises the non-redundancy of instruments: an observed change in |γ|/α with stable β is the structural signature of a macroprudential intervention; a change in β with stable |γ|/α is the signature of a growth shock. This attribution is testable in real time. The IGARCH boundary adds a third dimension: when estimated volatility persistence approaches one in dealer balance sheet data, the model identifies this as the signature of near-zero trend growth — a real-time diagnostic for secular stagnation regimes that macroprudential policy alone cannot address, and that requires growth-side intervention to resolve.
