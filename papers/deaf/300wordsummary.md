Financial dealer balance sheets are known to be asymmetric: expansions are fast, turbulent, and high-variance but contractions are slow and compressed. While the near-permanent volatility persistence as well as a higher duration of crises in low-growth environments are both well-documented and captured by forecasting models, the literature does not underpin strong structural explanations for what drives this persistence. The current paper uses insights on loss aversion from behavioural models to address the uncertainty on how long dealer balance sheet shocks last while identifying the factors that determine the degree of asymmetry in the two regimes.

Using a simple model for loss aversion and reference level, we find that while the persistence is driven independently by trend growth alone, asymmetry is a direct result of loss aversion levels. As a central result, this independence transforms the variance ratio into a direct reading of institutional loss aversion, and persistence into a direct reading of the growth environment — each identifiable without external instruments.

To elaborate the loss aversion channel, the paper identifies the risk-limits channel recognised in practitioner literature. More particularly, we rely on the observation that the risk committee encodes loss aversion in pre-committed position limits while the desk optimises within them. Separately, we assume a zero-sum structure between dealer and speculator, which forces them to opposite sides of the benchmark. Together with the risk-limits channel, this means trend growth determines how quickly shocks fade, and asymmetry — how many times more volatile expansion is than contraction — identifies loss aversion.

Six empirical findings confirm these predictions using Federal Reserve dealer data, including an out-of-sample test with zero post-crisis parameters. Implied loss aversion — a one-pound loss weighted at one pound twenty — is modest but generates the full observed structure.

The independence tells regulators which toolkit to reach for. Rising asymmetry with stable persistence is institutional: leverage ratios, position limits, and stress testing are the instruments. Falling asymmetry signals dealers underweighting losses — addressable with the same toolkit. Rising persistence with stable asymmetry is macroeconomic: monetary, fiscal, and structural reforms are the instruments — an area where risk limits cannot help.
