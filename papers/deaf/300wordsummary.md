Financial dealer balance sheets behave asymmetrically: expansions are fast, turbulent, and high-variance; contractions are slow, compressed, and grinding. This asymmetry is well documented but despite the success of CPT, such phenomena are seldom explained using dealer preferences. This paper intends to fill this gap in the literature and arrives at a structural identification of dealer loss aversion and trend growth that is exact, orthogonal, and requires no external instruments.

We retain the single empirically robust element of Cumulative Prospect Theory — the kink in the value function at the reference point — as the natural starting point. The goal is to find the minimal structure with CPT-like properties that reproduces the asymmetry, working around CPT's complications rather than applying it wholesale. Simpler alternatives such as CRRA or log utility cannot do this: without a reference point and a kink, they produce only symmetric GARCH dynamics with no regime classification and no preference-theoretic motivation for asymmetric risk limits. However, the kink itself creates an obstacle: the loss aversion parameter cancels exactly in the first-order condition, leaving it observationally inert in unconstrained optimisation — the distinction practitioners recognise between the risk management committee, which encodes loss aversion in position limits, and the trading desk, which optimises within those limits without reference to the firm's preferences. The model identifies the appropriate channel: pre-committed, regime-contingent constraints on the price impact coefficient — the rate at which the market maker moves prices per unit of order flow — implied by the endogenous institutional form loss aversion must take to remain consequential in a competitive market. The Stackelberg structure gives them binding force: the dealer's pricing power prevents loss aversion from being competed away.

Two structural conditions make this work as a theorem. A bilateral zero-sum structure guarantees dealer and speculator always occupy opposite sides of the benchmark simultaneously. The trend growth path provides mean-reversion, pinning persistence to the growth rate. The result is a Threshold-GARCH recursion for detrended dealer balance sheet deviations. The asymmetry ratio |γ|/α depends only on loss aversion λ; the persistence coefficient β depends only on trend growth g. Both cross-derivatives are exactly zero — the separation property. This identifies both primitives from the same time series without external instruments, and carries immediate policy content: macroprudential and growth instruments are structurally non-redundant, each targeting a different coefficient.

Using Federal Reserve Z.1 data (quarterly, 1952–2025), five findings confirm the model: expansion variance exceeds contraction variance (R = 2.07, p < 0.00001); persistence is confirmed within 0.4 percentage points of the structural prediction; the pre-GFC asymmetry ratio is stable across growth regimes; a post-GFC sign reversal is explained by a regulatory extension that preserves the separation property; and near-unit-root persistence in the stagnation period confirms the IGARCH boundary. The implied λ = 1.20 is the first structural market estimate of dealer loss aversion — confirming that the kink mechanism survives translation from laboratory to market data.
