% ABSTRACT
% ============================================================

\begin{abstract}
Financial markets exhibit a well-documented asymmetry between expansions and
contractions: boom periods are volatile, turbulent, and fast; bust periods
compressed, slow, and grinding. This asymmetry is most sharply visible in
\emph{dealer balance sheet dynamics}: intermediary leverage expands rapidly
and with high variance when dealers are above their funding benchmarks, and
contracts slowly with compressed variance when approaching their limits.
This paper derives the asymmetry from first principles using the minimal
behavioural structure sufficient to deliver it as an equilibrium theorem
rather than a calibration choice.

We retain the single empirically robust element of Cumulative Prospect
Theory --- the kink in the value function at a reference point ---
as the minimal structure capable of generating boom-bust asymmetry in
a dealer market equilibrium. The reference point is the economy-wide
trend growth benchmark, publicly observed, embedded in a Stackelberg market
scoped to liquid exchange-traded settings where dealer risk management is
the primary source of price impact asymmetry.

Two structural conditions are jointly necessary. First, the bilateral zero-sum
structure of the dealer--speculator game (Assumption~1) guarantees as a theorem
that both agents always sit on opposite sides of the kink simultaneously,
generating asymmetric price impact. Second, the trend growth path pins
volatility persistence to the growth rate through $\beta \approx 1/(1+g)^2$.
The paper derives a Threshold-GARCH recursion for the conditional variance of
detrended dealer balance sheet deviations, with the asymmetry
coefficients $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ identified exactly and $\beta$ identified
as a leading-order approximation with explicit error bounds. The asymmetry
ratio $|\gamma|/\alpha = (\lambda^4-1)/\lambda^4$ is determined by loss
aversion alone and $\beta$ by the growth rate alone --- a separation property
orthogonal by construction that allows exact within-model identification of
both structural primitives without external instruments. Empirical analysis
using US dealer balance sheet data (Federal Reserve Z.1, 1952--2025) confirms
the model: expansion variance exceeds contraction variance
($\hat{\mathcal{R}} = 2.07$, $p < 0.00001$), the pre-GFC asymmetry ratio
is stable across growth regimes ($0.42$--$0.45$), and deterministic
detrending confirms persistence within 0.4 percentage points of the
structural prediction. A post-GFC sign reversal is explained by a
one-parameter regulatory floor extension that preserves the separation
property. The implied $\hat{\lambda} = 1.20$ is the first structural
market-based estimate of dealer loss aversion.\end{abstract}


\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} loss aversion; boom-bust asymmetry; Threshold-GARCH; market microstructure; Stackelberg equilibrium; trend growth; dealer balance sheets

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Risk managers at primary dealer institutions and financial stability
departments at central banks face a common measurement challenge:
dealer balance sheet volatility has two distinct components --- a
boom-bust asymmetry between expansion and contraction regimes, and a
persistence of shocks across regimes --- but standard models conflate
them. \citet{li2025} establish that primary dealers behave as if
risk-averse even in essentially risk-free markets, driven by internal
VaR limits that are predetermined, costly to breach, and
counter-cyclical by design. A risk-neutral dealer with unlimited
capital would not behave differently based on its balance sheet position;
the fact that dealers do --- as documented by \citet{adrian2010,adrian2014}
and \citet{brunnermeier2009} --- is direct evidence of risk-sensitive
behaviour operating through institutional constraints, not through
unconstrained preference optimisation. This raises a natural question:
what preference structure underlies these constraints, and can it be
structurally identified from observed dealer balance sheet dynamics?

Existing approaches all require something external. Reduced-form GARCH
estimation needs an auxiliary estimate of the trend growth rate $g$ to
separate the persistence coefficient $\beta$ from other persistence
sources; rational belief equilibrium models \citep{brock1998,kurz2001}
require distributional assumptions on agent beliefs; prospect theory
calibration \citep{barberis2001} identifies the loss aversion parameter
$\lambda$ against macroeconomic moments that must be taken as given.
The result is that $\lambda$ and $g$ are never jointly identified from
the same time series without conditional dependence on untested
auxiliary structure (Table~\ref{tab:identification_comparison}). This
paper removes that dependence.

Cumulative Prospect Theory --- the dominant descriptive model of
individual choice under risk, best-validated in the laboratory --- is
the natural starting point. We retain its single empirically robust
element: the kink in the value function at a reference point. The goal
is to find the minimal structure with CPT-like properties that
reproduces boom-bust asymmetry, working around CPT's complications ---
probability weighting, time-inconsistency, intractable endogenous
reference points --- rather than applying it wholesale. Simpler
alternatives such as CRRA or log utility cannot serve as the starting
point: without a reference point and a kink, they produce only symmetric
GARCH dynamics with no regime classification and no preference-theoretic
motivation for asymmetric risk limits.

The kink itself, however, creates an obstacle in equilibrium. A market
maker optimising a standard piecewise linear loss-averse value function
generates no asymmetry: the loss aversion parameter $\lambda$ multiplies
the objective in the loss region but cancels exactly in the first-order
condition, leaving the optimal price impact coefficient unchanged
regardless of regime. This is the distinction practitioners draw between
the risk management committee, which encodes loss aversion in position
limits at quarterly or annual frequency, and the trading desk, which
optimises within those limits without reference to the firm's aggregate
preferences. The cancellation therefore identifies the channel through
which the kink must operate to generate equilibrium asymmetry:
pre-committed, regime-contingent constraints on the price impact
schedule. These constraints are the endogenous form that loss aversion
takes to remain consequential in a competitive market --- precisely what
\citet{li2025} document empirically at the desk level. The Stackelberg
structure of the market gives them binding force: the dealer's pricing
power prevents loss aversion from being competed away.

Two structural conditions make this work as a theorem. The bilateral
zero-sum structure of the dealer-speculator game guarantees that the two
agents always occupy opposite sides of the trend benchmark simultaneously
--- a derived result, not an assumption. The trend growth path provides
mean-reversion, pinning volatility persistence to the economy's growth
rate. Together they deliver the result: a Threshold-GARCH recursion for
detrended dealer balance sheet deviations with an exact orthogonal
identification. The ratio of the asymmetry coefficient $\gamma$ to the
ARCH coefficient $\alpha$ --- measuring the relative magnitude of the
boom-bust variance asymmetry --- depends only on the loss aversion
parameter $\lambda$; the persistence coefficient $\beta$ depends only
on the trend growth rate $g$; both cross-derivatives are exactly zero.
This separation property provides exact within-model identification of
both structural primitives without external instruments, exclusion
restrictions, or auxiliary calibration targets.

The paper makes five contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The derivation of boom-bust dealer balance sheet volatility
    asymmetry as an equilibrium theorem from the minimal CPT-like
    structure.
  \item The structural identification result with its exact orthogonal
    separation property: $|\gamma|/\alpha$ identifies $\lambda$ and
    $\beta$ identifies $g$ from the same time series, with zero
    cross-derivatives.
  \item The IGARCH boundary: as the trend growth rate approaches zero,
    volatility persistence approaches one, providing the first
    preference-based microfoundation for integrated volatility processes.
  \item A testable cross-sectional prediction: persistence should
    co-vary with trend growth, and asymmetry ratios with loss aversion
    measures, with zero cross-effects.
  \item Empirical confirmation using US dealer balance sheet data,
    including a post-GFC regulatory structural break that provides a
    natural experiment for the separation property.
\end{enumerate}

The paper is postdictive in intent: it explains why the boom-bust
asymmetry exists rather than predicting that it will. Its value lies in
providing structural micro-foundations for a pattern that is well
described but not well explained, and in connecting the experimental
programme of behavioural economics --- where the kink is well-validated
but confined to the laboratory --- to the structural programme of market
microstructure, where loss aversion has remained difficult to identify
cleanly. The remainder of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:literature} reviews the related literature.
Section~\ref{sec:model} develops the model.
Section~\ref{sec:empirical} presents the empirical evidence.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Literature}
\label{sec:literature}

This paper connects three literatures: the microstructure of dealer
markets, the microfoundations of GARCH dynamics, and the application of
behavioural preferences to financial equilibrium.

The asymmetry in dealer balance sheet dynamics is documented empirically
by \citet{adrian2010,adrian2014}, who show that broker-dealer leverage is
procyclical and VaR-constrained, with balance sheets expanding rapidly in
booms and compressing in busts. \citet{brunnermeier2009} provide a
theoretical framework connecting market liquidity to funding liquidity
through margin spirals, producing amplification but not a structural
identification of the underlying preference parameter. \citet{li2025}
provide the most granular evidence, documenting at the desk level that
US Treasury dealers reduce position changes and require higher
compensation as they approach their internal VaR limits --- precisely the
contraction regime of the present model.

GARCH-type volatility clustering has been derived from heterogeneous
beliefs by \citet{brock1998} and from endogenous uncertainty propagated
through belief heterogeneity by \citet{kurz2001}. \citet{follmer2005}
show that interacting agent models can generate ARCH effects.
\citet{watanabe2008} connects belief dynamics to conditional
heteroskedasticity. These frameworks produce volatility persistence from
the belief channel, and some generate asymmetric shock responses.
What they do not deliver is the specific structural identification this
paper targets: that expansion variance exceeds contraction variance by a
factor tied to a single preference parameter ($\lambda^4$), with
persistence tied to the growth rate through a separate and orthogonal
mapping ($\beta \approx 1/(1+g)^2$). The contribution here is
complementary: the preference channel delivers a structural result the
belief channel does not, and their interaction is a direction for future
work.

The application of Cumulative Prospect Theory to asset pricing begins
with \citet{barberis2001}, who show that loss aversion can explain the
equity premium, and extends through the reference-dependent preference
framework of \citet{koszegi2006,koszegi2007}. The present paper is
distinct from these contributions in three respects. First, it retains
only the kink, discarding probability weighting, rank-dependence, and
the S-shaped curvature. Second, the reference point is a social
benchmark (the economy-wide trend growth path), not an individual wealth
history or rational-expectations fixed point. Third, the identification
is within-model rather than requiring calibration against external
macroeconomic moments.

The connection between dealer balance sheet dynamics and volatility
is also explored by \citet{danielsson2008}, who model feedback between
VaR constraints and market volatility, and by \citet{ang2005}, who
document regime-switching in aggregate market volatility correlated with
macroeconomic states. The linear price impact structure used in the
present model takes a form similar to \citet{kyle1985}; its economic
content --- inventory-based rather than information-based --- is discussed
in Section~\ref{sec:setup}. The present paper provides what none of
these contributions delivers: a closed-form structural identification
of both dealer loss aversion and trend growth from the same time series,
without external instruments, through the exact orthogonal mappings of
the separation property.

\section{The Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The model targets liquid, transparent dealer markets --- US Treasuries,
agency MBS, major FX pairs, interest rate swaps, and equity index
futures --- where dealer pricing power is established, macro-relevant
information is approximately simultaneously public, and VaR-constrained
balance sheet management is the primary source of price impact variation
\citep{li2025,adrian2014}. In these markets, empirical evidence
consistently shows that price impact is driven by dealer balance sheet
capacity and inventory risk rather than adverse selection against
privately informed counterparties
\citep{fleming1999,brandt2004,li2025}. Markets where adverse selection
dominates --- high-yield credit, small-cap equities, structured products
--- are outside the model's direct scope.

\noindent\textbf{Agents and timing.}
Three agents trade a single risky asset over discrete periods
$t = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$\,: a market maker (MM), a speculator (S), and a
continuum of liquidity-motivated traders. The market operates as a
Stackelberg game. At the start of each period, the MM --- the
Stackelberg leader --- commits to a \emph{price impact schedule} before
observing any order flow. A price impact schedule is a rule mapping the
total quantity demanded in the market to the transaction price: it
specifies, for each possible net order size, the price at which the MM
will clear the market. By committing to this schedule before trading
begins, the MM acts as a price-setter. The speculator --- the follower
--- then observes a public signal and chooses how large a position to
take, knowing the price impact schedule in advance.

\noindent\textbf{The public signal and the speculator's information.}
In the liquid, transparent markets this model targets, macro-relevant
information --- interest rate decisions, employment releases, earnings
announcements --- becomes simultaneously public to all participants. The
model captures this through a publicly observable return innovation
$\mu_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_\mu)$, where $\sigma^2_\mu > 0$ is
the variance of the signal. Both the MM and the speculator observe
$\mu_t$ at the same time; the speculator has no private information
advantage. Its role is as a capacity-constrained position-taker
responding to common knowledge signals, not as a Kyle-style informed
trader exploiting private information.

\noindent\textbf{Order flow and liquidity-motivated traders.}
Order flow is the net signed quantity submitted to the market in a
given period: a positive quantity means net buying pressure, a negative
quantity means net selling pressure. It is measured in asset units, not
in value terms. Liquidity-motivated traders --- hedgers, index
rebalancers, and participants with exogenous needs to buy or sell ---
submit random order flow $u_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_u)$,
independently across periods, where $\sigma^2_u > 0$ is the variance
of their aggregate demand. These traders have no strategic motive: they
trade for reasons unrelated to the signal $\mu_t$. Their presence
reflects the empirical reality that in exchange-traded markets, the MM
observes total order flow but cannot decompose it into its speculator
and liquidity-trader components.

\noindent\textbf{The pricing rule and price impact.}
The MM sets prices according to the linear rule:
\begin{equation}
  p_t = p_{t-1} + \lambda_p(m_t + u_t)
  \label{eq:pricing_rule}
\end{equation}
where $m_t$ is the speculator's signed position (quantity, in asset
units), $u_t$ is liquidity-trader order flow, and $\lambda_p > 0$ is
the \emph{price impact coefficient}. The sum $m_t + u_t$ is total order
flow --- the aggregate net quantity the MM must absorb. The coefficient
$\lambda_p$ is the rate at which the price moves per unit of net order
flow absorbed: if $\lambda_p = 0.5$ and total order flow is $+10$
units, the price rises by 5. A higher $\lambda_p$ means the MM moves
prices more aggressively for a given quantity, reflecting tighter
balance sheet capacity; a lower $\lambda_p$ means the MM accommodates
larger quantities with less price movement, reflecting ample capacity.
In this sense, $\lambda_p$ is the MM's strategic variable: by choosing
it before trading begins, the MM determines the terms on which it will
intermediate between buyers and sellers.

This linear price impact structure is consistent with the
inventory-based market making literature \citep{stoll1978,grossman1988}
and takes a form similar to \citet{kyle1985}, though in our setting
$\lambda_p$ reflects balance sheet capacity constraints rather than
adverse selection against privately informed traders. Note that
$\lambda_p$ (the price impact coefficient) is distinct from $\lambda$
(the loss aversion parameter); we use $\lambda_p$ throughout for the
former to avoid ambiguity.

The liquidity-motivated traders generate a steady source of
intermediation income for the MM. Since the MM absorbs their random
order flow $u_t$ and prices move by $\lambda_p u_t$ per unit, the
expected revenue from this flow per period is $\lambda_p \sigma^2_u$
--- the product of the price impact coefficient and the variance of
liquidity demand, which measures how much uninformed order flow the MM
expects to turn over. The noise trader variance $\sigma^2_u$ also
determines the benchmark price impact level
$\lambda^0_p = \sigma_\mu/(2\sigma_u)$ --- the value of $\lambda_p$
at which the MM's unconstrained profit is maximised (derived in
Section~\ref{sec:equilibrium}). The paper's structural results ---
the variance ratio, the asymmetry mechanism, and the separation
property --- are invariant to $\sigma^2_u$: scaling liquidity demand
up or down rescales $\lambda^0_p$ but leaves the ratios that identify
$\lambda$ and $g$ unchanged.

\noindent\textbf{Return innovation and position closing.}
After the speculator takes position $m_t$ and liquidity traders submit
$u_t$, the asset's fundamental value is realised. The observed return
innovation is:
\begin{equation}
  \varepsilon^r_t = \tfrac{1}{2}\mu_t + \varepsilon_t
  \label{eq:return_innovation}
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2_\varepsilon)$ is
idiosyncratic noise with variance $\sigma^2_\varepsilon > 0$,
independent of $\mu_t$. The factor of $\tfrac{1}{2}$ arises because
the speculator's optimal position (derived in
Section~\ref{sec:equilibrium}) passes half the signal through to
prices. All agents observe $\varepsilon^r_t$ at the end of each
period. Within-period position closing then ensures that both the MM
and the speculator enter each new period with zero inventory: all
asymmetric effects operate through accumulated wealth, not through
carried positions.

The MM evaluates wealth against a publicly observed reference point
$\bar{W}_t = \bar{W}_0(1+g)^t$, the economy-wide trend wealth benchmark
growing at constant rate $g > 0$, where $\bar{W}_0 > 0$ is the initial
benchmark level. This benchmark requires no individual wealth histories,
no rational-expectations fixed point, and no peer-group comparison. It
connects to the social comparison literature \citep{festinger1954} and
the catching-up-with-the-Joneses tradition \citep{abel1990,campbell1999}.
The loss aversion parameter $\lambda > 1$ is treated as a constant
structural parameter of the dealer population, reflecting the view that
institutional loss aversion is determined by risk culture, regulatory
requirements, and competitive selection, and changes on timescales
longer than the business cycle.

\noindent\textbf{State variable and detrending.}
Define the detrended wealth deviation for each agent $i \in \{S, MM\}$
as the agent's actual wealth deflated by the trend benchmark, minus the
initial benchmark level:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\Delta}^i_t = \frac{W^i_t}{(1+g)^t} - \bar{W}_0
  \label{eq:deviation_def}
\end{equation}
and let $D_t = \tilde{\Delta}^S_t$ denote the speculator's deviation.
The speculator's per-period profit is the gain from its position $m_t$
against the realised return innovation $\varepsilon^r_t$, net of the
price impact cost it pays the MM. Under the pricing rule
\eqref{eq:pricing_rule} and within-period position closing, this yields
the wealth recursion $W^S_t = W^S_{t-1} + \lambda^{\rm eff}_p m^2_t +
m_t\varepsilon_t$, where $\lambda^{\rm eff}_p m^2_t$ is the price
impact cost (quadratic in position size, paid to the MM as the price
moves against the speculator's trade) and $m_t\varepsilon_t$ is the
speculator's revenue from the idiosyncratic component of the return
innovation.

\begin{assumption}[Bilateral zero-sum subsystem]
\label{ass:zerosum}
We track the bilateral wealth subsystem $\{S, MM\}$ only. Noise trader
transfers --- the income $\lambda^{\rm eff}_p u^2_t$ accruing to the
MM from uninformed order flow --- exit the system at each date via
consumption or external hedging demands. Under this assumption,
$W^S_t + W^{MM}_t = 2\bar{W}_0(1+g)^t$, so that
$D_t + \tilde{\Delta}^{MM}_t = 0$ for all $t$.
\end{assumption}

\noindent
The assumption is substantive but standard in the microstructure
literature when the object of interest is dealer--speculator dynamics
rather than noise trader welfare.


\subsection{Equilibrium}
\label{sec:equilibrium}

Under Assumption~\ref{ass:zerosum}, the bilateral zero-sum condition
places the two agents on opposite sides of the trend benchmark
simultaneously.

\begin{proposition}[Regime Separation]
\label{prop:regime_separation}
Under the bilateral zero-sum condition, for all $t$:
\begin{align}
  \text{Expansion:} & \quad D_t \geq 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\;
    \tilde{\Delta}^{MM}_t \leq 0 \\
  \text{Contraction:} & \quad D_t < 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\;
    \tilde{\Delta}^{MM}_t > 0
\end{align}
The speculator's expansion is the MM's contraction and vice versa.
\end{proposition}

\noindent
This is the engine of the variance asymmetry. When the speculator is in
expansion ($D_{t-1} \geq 0$), the MM is in its loss region (below the
trend benchmark) and posts low price impact to attract order flow and
recover; when the speculator is in contraction ($D_{t-1} < 0$), the MM
is in its gain region and posts high price impact to protect accumulated
wealth.

\noindent\textbf{The MM's problem.}
The MM earns profit from two sources: revenue from absorbing
liquidity-trader order flow (which generates $\lambda_p\sigma^2_u$ in
expectation, since it absorbs random quantity $u_t$ at price impact
$\lambda_p$), and bears a cost from the speculator's informed trading
against it (which costs $\sigma^2_\mu/(4\lambda_p)$ in expectation,
since the speculator exploits the signal $\mu_t$ optimally given the
price impact it faces). The MM's per-period profit from intermediation
is therefore:
\begin{equation}
  \pi^{MM}(\lambda_p) = \lambda_p\sigma^2_u - \frac{\sigma^2_\mu}{4\lambda_p}
  \label{eq:mm_profit}
\end{equation}
The unconstrained maximum coincides with the Kyle zero-profit benchmark
$\lambda^0_p = \sigma_\mu/(2\sigma_u)$ regardless of regime: the loss
aversion scalar $\lambda$ cancels in the first-order condition.%
\footnote{For a piecewise linear value function $v(\pi) = \pi$ in the
gain region and $v(\pi) = \lambda\pi$ in the loss region, the FOC in
each region is $\partial\pi^{MM}/\partial\lambda_p = 0$ scaled by $1$
or $\lambda$. The optimum is the same interior point $\lambda^0_p$ in
both cases.}
This cancellation explains why unconstrained loss aversion has no
empirical content in this setting and motivates the risk-limit
formulation.

Consistent with the VaR-constrained dealer behaviour documented by
\citet{adrian2014} and \citet{li2025}, we model the MM's loss aversion
as operating through pre-committed, regime-contingent risk limits.
These limits are set at a lower frequency than individual trading
periods, reflecting the institutional reality that VaR limits and balance
sheet benchmarks are updated infrequently. The MM's loss aversion
parameter $\lambda > 1$ calibrates two log-symmetric risk limits around
the Kyle benchmark:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda^{\rm eff}_p =
  \begin{cases}
    \lambda^0_p/\lambda
      & D_{t-1} \geq 0 \quad\text{(expansion: limit acts as floor)} \\[4pt]
    \lambda\lambda^0_p
      & D_{t-1} < 0 \quad\text{(contraction: limit acts as cap)}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:risk_limits}
\end{equation}
The log-symmetry $\log(\lambda\lambda^0_p) - \log\lambda^0_p =
\log\lambda^0_p - \log(\lambda^0_p/\lambda) = \log\lambda$ encodes loss
aversion as equal proportional departure in each direction.

The MM's effective behaviour under these limits is not risk-neutral,
even though the unconstrained interior optimum coincides with the
risk-neutral benchmark $\lambda^0_p$. The risk limits make the MM's
price impact regime-dependent: lower in expansion
($\lambda^0_p/\lambda < \lambda^0_p$) and higher in contraction
($\lambda\lambda^0_p > \lambda^0_p$). This regime-dependence is the
structural foundation of the procyclical leverage cycle documented by
\citet{adrian2010,adrian2014}. In expansion, the accommodating risk
limit supports large positions and high leverage with low VaR
utilisation; in contraction, the tight risk limit forces small positions
and low leverage with high VaR utilisation --- the negative
leverage--VaR relationship established empirically by \citet{adrian2014}.
\citet{li2025} confirm this pattern at the desk level: dealers reduce
position changes and require higher compensation as they approach their
internal VaR limits, precisely the contraction regime of the model.

\noindent\textbf{The speculator's position.}
Taking the price impact schedule $\lambda^{\rm eff}_p$ as given, the
speculator chooses position size $m_t$ to maximise expected profit. Its
profit from position $m_t$ given signal $\mu_t$ is $m_t(\mu_t -
2\lambda^{\rm eff}_p m_t)$: the first term $m_t\mu_t$ is the gross
revenue from trading in the direction of the signal (buying when $\mu_t
> 0$, selling when $\mu_t < 0$), and the second term $-2\lambda^{\rm
eff}_p m^2_t$ is the price impact cost, which is quadratic in position
size because a larger position moves the price further against the
speculator. The speculator therefore maximises:
\[
  \mathbb{E}[m_t(\mu_t - 2\lambda^{\rm eff}_p m_t) \mid \mu_t]
\]
yielding the optimal position $m^*_t = \mu_t / (4\lambda^{\rm eff}_p)$.
The speculator trades in the direction of the signal and scales its
position down as price impact rises: a higher $\lambda^{\rm eff}_p$
(tighter MM capacity) means the speculator takes a smaller position to
avoid paying excessive price impact.

\begin{remark}[MM assignment of loss aversion]
\label{rem:mm_assignment}
Loss aversion in the speculator's own objective cancels by the same
scalar argument as in the MM's unconstrained problem: $\lambda^S$
scales the objective in the loss region but does not shift the interior
optimum. The mechanism therefore requires loss aversion to reside in the
price-setter (the MM), not the quantity-setter (the speculator). Under
heterogeneous loss aversion $\lambda^{MM} \neq \lambda^S$, only
$\lambda^{MM}$ enters the variance asymmetry; $\lambda^S$ is
observationally irrelevant.
\end{remark}

\noindent\textbf{The deviation recursion.}
Substituting the speculator's optimal position into the wealth dynamics
under Assumption~\ref{ass:zerosum}, the detrended deviation evolves as:
\begin{equation}
  D_t = \frac{D_{t-1}}{1+g} + A^r\sigma^2_\mu + B^r\mu_t\varepsilon_t
  \label{eq:deviation_recursion}
\end{equation}
where the regime-specific coefficients are:
\begin{align}
  A^+ &= \frac{\lambda^2}{4\lambda^{0\,2}_p}, \quad
  A^- = \frac{1}{4\lambda^2\lambda^{0\,2}_p}
  \label{eq:Ar_coeff} \\
  B^+ &= \frac{\lambda}{2\lambda^0_p}, \quad
  B^- = \frac{1}{2\lambda\lambda^0_p}
  \label{eq:Br_coeff}
\end{align}
with $r = +$ when $D_{t-1} \geq 0$ and $r = -$ when $D_{t-1} < 0$. The
deflation factor $1/(1+g)$ is regime-invariant --- the structural source
of the separation between persistence and asymmetry.

\noindent\textbf{The cancellation result.}
The one-period return variance $\text{Var}(\varepsilon^r_t) =
\sigma^2_\mu/4 + \sigma^2_\varepsilon$ is identical in both regimes:
the return innovation has the same distribution regardless of whether
the dealer is in expansion or contraction.%
\footnote{Within the model's maintained assumption of
regime-independent signal variance $\sigma^2_\mu$, return variance
$\text{Var}(\varepsilon^r_t) = \sigma^2_\mu/4 + \sigma^2_\varepsilon$
is identical across regimes --- bounded below by
$\sigma^2_\varepsilon > 0$. Empirically, implied volatility (VIX) is
countercyclical, suggesting $\sigma^2_\mu$ may be higher in contraction.
Relaxing the constant $\sigma^2_\mu$ assumption would reduce the
observed variance ratio relative to $\lambda^4$, making the empirical
estimate $\hat{\mathcal{R}} = 2.07$ a conservative lower bound on the
loss-aversion-driven asymmetry in wealth dynamics.}
The boom-bust asymmetry therefore operates through the wealth dynamics
--- through the coefficients $B^+$ and $B^-$ --- not through the return
process. The ratio of expansion to contraction innovation variance in
the wealth process is:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{(B^+)^2}{(B^-)^2} = \lambda^4
  \label{eq:variance_ratio}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Threshold-GARCH Result}
\label{sec:tgarch_result}

The deviation recursion~\eqref{eq:deviation_recursion} implies a
regime-dependent conditional variance for $D_t$. Linearising the regime
probability around the stationary point
(Proposition~\ref{prop:linearisation}, with approximation error bounded
below 6\%) yields:

\begin{theorem}[Threshold-GARCH for Detrended Dealer Wealth Deviations]
\label{thm:tgarch}
The conditional variance $V_t = \operatorname{Var}(D_t \mid
\mathcal{F}_{t-1})$ satisfies the Threshold-GARCH recursion:
\begin{equation}
  V_t = \omega + \alpha(\varepsilon^r_t)^2
      + \gamma(\varepsilon^r_t)^2 \cdot \mathbf{1}[D_{t-1} < 0]
      + \beta V_{t-1}
  \label{eq:tgarch}
\end{equation}
with exact structural identifications for the ARCH coefficient $\alpha$,
the asymmetry coefficient $\gamma$, and the intercept $\omega$, and a
leading-order identification for the persistence coefficient $\beta$:
\begin{align}
  \alpha &= \Phi\lambda^2 > 0
    \label{eq:alpha} \\
  \gamma &= \Phi\,\frac{1-\lambda^4}{\lambda^2} < 0
    \quad \text{for all } \lambda > 1
    \label{eq:gamma} \\
  \beta  &\approx \frac{1}{(1+g)^2} \in (0,1)
    \quad \text{for } g > 0 \quad \text{(leading-order)}
    \label{eq:beta} \\
  \omega &= \frac{2\lambda^2\sigma^4_\varepsilon\sigma^4_\mu}
             {\lambda^{0\,2}_p(4\sigma^2_\varepsilon+\sigma^2_\mu)^2}
    \label{eq:omega}
\end{align}
where $\Phi = 2\sigma^4_\varepsilon\sigma^2_\mu /
[\lambda^{0\,2}_p(4\sigma^2_\varepsilon + \sigma^2_\mu)^2]$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The exact identifications for the ARCH coefficient $\alpha$ and
asymmetry coefficient $\gamma$ follow from Proposition~\ref{prop:var_asym}
in the appendix: for each regime $r$,
$V^r_t = (B^r)^2\Sigma[H_0 + H_2(\varepsilon^r_t)^2]$, where
$\Sigma$, $H_0$, $H_2$ are functions of the signal and noise variances.
Setting $\alpha = c_2^+$, $\gamma = c_2^- - c_2^+$, $\omega = c_0^+$
gives \eqref{eq:alpha}--\eqref{eq:omega} exactly. The leading-order
identification $\beta \approx 1/(1+g)^2$ arises from linearising
$\Phi(z_{t-1})$ around the stationary point; the approximation error
is bounded below 6\% for moderate deviations
(Proposition~\ref{prop:linearisation}).
Signs and the $\beta$ limit follow from direct differentiation.
\end{proof}

The sign of the asymmetry coefficient $\gamma$ is negative for all
$\lambda > 1$: expansion-regime conditional variance exceeds
contraction-regime conditional variance. This is the correct sign for
boom-bust asymmetry in dealer balance sheet dynamics --- distinct from
the leverage effect in equity returns, where the sign convention is
reversed.

\begin{corollary}[The Separation Property]
\label{cor:separation}
The asymmetry ratio and persistence coefficient satisfy:
\begin{align}
  \frac{|\gamma|}{\alpha} &= \frac{\lambda^4 - 1}{\lambda^4}
    \quad \text{(depends on $\lambda$ only)} \\
  \frac{\partial\beta}{\partial\lambda} &= 0
    \quad \text{(persistence independent of loss aversion)} \\
  \frac{\partial(|\gamma|/\alpha)}{\partial g} &= 0
    \quad \text{(asymmetry independent of growth)}
\end{align}
As $g \to 0$, $\beta \to 1$: the Threshold-GARCH approaches
Threshold-IGARCH, providing the first preference-based microfoundation
for integrated volatility. The asymmetry coefficient $\gamma$ is
independent of $g$: a stagnant economy exhibits the same boom-bust
asymmetry as a growing one but with permanently persistent shocks.
\end{corollary}

The implied structural loss aversion parameter is recovered from
the asymmetry ratio: $\hat{\lambda} = [(1 - |\hat{\gamma}|/
\hat{\alpha})^{-1}]^{1/4}$. The implied variance ratio between
expansion and contraction regimes is $\lambda^4$.

\section{Empirical Evidence}
\label{sec:empirical}

The model's predictions concern the dynamics of $D_t$ --- the book-value
deviation of dealer wealth from the trend benchmark. Translating this to
data requires identifying the empirical counterpart of $D_t$ among
available dealer balance sheet series. This is a conceptual choice, not
just a data availability question, and getting it wrong inverts the
model's predictions entirely.

Two candidate series are available. The Federal Reserve Z.1 Financial
Accounts, Table L.130 (Security Brokers and Dealers), provides
book-value leverage --- total financial assets divided by proprietors'
equity --- at quarterly frequency from 1952 to 2025. The He-Kelly-Manela
(2017) intermediary capital ratio provides market-value leverage ---
market equity divided by market equity plus book debt --- for primary
dealer holding companies from 1970. Our model's state variable $D_t$ is
a book-value wealth deviation: the market maker's balance sheet position
relative to the trend benchmark. The choice of series matters because
market-value leverage inverts the variance asymmetry: equity prices
collapse during contractions, causing market-value leverage to spike and
producing high contraction-regime variance --- the opposite of our
model's prediction that expansion variance exceeds contraction
variance.\footnote{We confirmed this empirically: the HKM series
produces $\hat{\mathcal{R}} < 1$ in all three growth regimes tested,
with the wrong sign in every case.}
We therefore use the Z.1 L.130 book-value series throughout. The
methodological discipline established here carries through to the
detrending choices that follow: each empirical decision is determined by
the model's structure rather than by convention.

\subsection{Detrending methodology}
\label{sec:detrending}

The model's two structural parameters --- loss aversion $\lambda$ and
trend growth $g$ --- require different empirical objects for
identification, and therefore different detrending methods. This is not
a post hoc choice but a direct consequence of the model's structure.

\noindent\textbf{HP filter for $\lambda$ identification.} The variance
ratio $\hat{\mathcal{R}} = \hat{V}^+/\hat{V}^-$ identifies $\lambda$
through the regime-dependent innovation variance. The model's regime
classification depends on whether the market maker's wealth is above or
below its \emph{current} reference point --- a local benchmark. The
Hodrick-Prescott filter ($\lambda_{HP} = 1600$) provides the
appropriate local trend, adapting to structural shifts in dealer
leverage (notably around 2008). We apply it to log-leverage and classify
regimes by the lagged deviation: expansion ($D_{t-1} \geq 0$) or
contraction ($D_{t-1} < 0$).

\noindent\textbf{Deterministic detrending for $g$ identification.} The
persistence coefficient $\beta \approx 1/(1+g)^2$ arises from the
secular deflation factor $1/(1+g)$ in the deviation recursion. The
model's quarterly AR(1) coefficient is $\phi \approx 1/(1+g/4) \approx
0.998$ --- a half-life of approximately 350 quarters. The HP filter
removes low-frequency variation at cycles longer than approximately
$2\pi\sqrt{\lambda_{HP}/4} \approx 40$ quarters, absorbing the entire
persistence signal into the estimated trend. The residual HP cycle has
much lower persistence ($\hat{\phi}_{\rm HP} \approx 0.66$) than the
model predicts, making $\beta$ unidentifiable from HP-filtered data.

Deterministic detrending --- $d_t = \log(\text{equity}_t) - (g_{\rm
lit}/4) \cdot t - c$ --- removes only the secular growth trend and
preserves all business-cycle and medium-run variation, including the
persistence the model predicts. We use log-equity (the dealer wealth
claim) rather than log-leverage, and source $g_{\rm lit}$ from
\citet{fernald2015} and the Congressional Budget Office (2013) rather
than estimating it from the leverage series (which has no long-run TFP
trend). For the full 1952--2025 sample, $g_{\rm lit} = 0.8\%$ p.a.

\subsection{Finding 1: Variance asymmetry (HP filter)}
\label{sec:finding1}

The detrended deviation is stationary (ADF statistic $-6.70$,
$p < 0.001$). Expansion and contraction regimes are approximately
balanced (149 vs 144 quarters). The expansion innovation variance
exceeds the contraction innovation variance with the correct sign:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathcal{R}} = \frac{\hat{V}^+}{\hat{V}^-} = 2.07, \quad
  \text{95\% bootstrap CI: } [1.35, 3.17], \quad
  \text{F-test } p < 0.00001.
  \label{eq:test1_R}
\end{equation}
The implied structural loss aversion parameter is
$\hat{\lambda} = \hat{\mathcal{R}}^{1/4} = 1.20$, with 95\% CI
$[1.08, 1.33]$. This is below the Tversky-Kahneman laboratory value
of 2.25, consistent with institutional attenuation of individual loss
aversion through VaR-limit mechanisms. It is also below the
Adrian-Shin (2010) range of $\lambda \in [1.4, 1.7]$, reflecting the
broader Z.1 population (which includes non-primary-dealer firms).

The separation property check --- testing whether the AR(1)
coefficient $\phi$ is equal across regimes ($H_0\!: \phi^+ = \phi^-$,
$t = -1.28$, $p = 0.20$) --- fails to reject equality, consistent
with $\partial\beta/\partial\lambda = 0$.

\noindent\textbf{Robustness.} The result is stable across the pre-GFC
subsample (1963--2007: $\hat{\mathcal{R}} = 2.19$) and positive
threshold perturbations ($+0.05$: $2.39$; $+0.10$: $2.43$). Linear
detrending produces a sign reversal ($\hat{\mathcal{R}} = 0.72$),
reflecting post-2008 deleveraging: a rigid linear trend misclassifies
this volatile period as contraction, inflating contraction-regime
variance. The HP filter, which adapts to time-varying trends, avoids
this artefact.

\subsection{Finding 2: Persistence confirmed (deterministic detrending)}
\label{sec:finding2}

Under deterministic detrending of log-equity at $g_{\rm lit} = 0.8\%$
p.a., the pooled AR(1) coefficient is:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\phi}_{\rm det} = 0.994, \quad
  \text{model prediction } 1/(1+g_{\rm lit}/4) = 0.998, \quad
  \text{discrepancy } = 0.004.
  \label{eq:phi_det}
\end{equation}
This represents a 99\% reduction in the persistence identification
error relative to HP filtering ($\hat{\phi}_{\rm HP} = 0.664$,
discrepancy $= 0.334$). The model's prediction that dealer wealth
reverts to trend at rate $1/(1+g/4)$ is empirically confirmed within
0.4 percentage points.

The regime-specific AR(1) coefficients under deterministic detrending
are $\hat{\phi}^+_{\rm det} = 0.992$ (expansion) and
$\hat{\phi}^-_{\rm det} = 0.997$ (contraction), both within 0.6
percentage points of the model prediction and within 0.5 percentage
points of each other. This confirms that the deflation factor $1/(1+g)$
is regime-invariant, as the model requires: persistence does not depend
on which side of the reference point the dealer occupies.

\subsection{Finding 3: Pre-GFC asymmetry ratio stability (HP filter)}
\label{sec:finding3}

The separation property predicts that across subperiods with different
$g$ but the same dealer population, $|\gamma|/\alpha$ should be stable.
We partition the pre-GFC Z.1 data into two growth regimes based on
\citet{fernald2015}: the productivity slowdown (1974--1994,
$g = 0.5\%$) and the IT boom (1995--2007, $g = 1.5\%$). In both
regimes, the asymmetry ratio is remarkably stable:
\begin{align}
  |\hat{\gamma}|/\hat{\alpha}
    &= 0.45 \quad \text{(productivity slowdown, } g = 0.5\%) \nonumber \\
  |\hat{\gamma}|/\hat{\alpha}
    &= 0.42 \quad \text{(IT boom, } g = 1.5\%)
  \label{eq:asym_stability}
\end{align}
The two values differ by less than 3 percentage points despite a
threefold difference in trend TFP growth. Both regimes have the correct
sign on the variance ratio ($\hat{\mathcal{R}} = 1.81$ and $1.73$).
This stability is evidence that the loss aversion parameter $\lambda$
was approximately constant across the two pre-GFC regimes: since
$|\gamma|/\alpha = (\lambda^4-1)/\lambda^4$ depends only on $\lambda$,
stability of the asymmetry ratio across different growth environments
can only arise if $\lambda$ is stable. The alternative --- that $\lambda$
varied with $g$ in just the right way to keep $|\gamma|/\alpha$ constant
--- is a knife-edge coincidence the data does not require us to invoke.

The persistence channel provides a complementary check under
deterministic detrending. The subperiod AR(1) coefficients are
$\hat{\phi}_{\rm det} = 0.988$ (slowdown) and $\hat{\phi}_{\rm det} =
0.974$ (IT boom), against model predictions of $0.999$ and $0.996$.
The directional finding is correct: higher growth produces lower
persistence ($0.988 > 0.974$ as $g$ increases from $0.5\%$ to $1.5\%$).
The quantitative discrepancy (1--2 percentage points) is consistent
with finite-sample estimation error in 52--84 quarter windows.

\subsection{Finding 4: Post-GFC regulatory structural break}
\label{sec:finding4}

The secular stagnation period (2010--2024) produces a sign reversal
under HP filtering: $\hat{\mathcal{R}} = 0.72 < 1$ and
$|\hat{\gamma}|/\hat{\alpha} = -0.38$. This reflects a structural
change in dealer balance sheet dynamics driven by post-GFC regulation.

\noindent\textbf{The regulatory floor mechanism.} Basel III leverage
ratio requirements, the Volcker Rule (Dodd-Frank Section 619), the
Supplementary Leverage Ratio on safe assets, and CCAR/DFAST stress
testing all impose constraints that \emph{selectively bind in the
expansion regime}. We introduce a regulatory price-impact floor
$\lambda^{\rm reg} > 0$:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda^{\rm eff}_p =
  \begin{cases}
    \max\!\left(\dfrac{\lambda^0_p}{\lambda},\; \lambda^{\rm reg}\right)
      & D_{t-1} \geq 0 \quad\text{(expansion)} \\[8pt]
    \lambda\lambda^0_p
      & D_{t-1} < 0 \quad\text{(contraction, unchanged)}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:reg_floor}
\end{equation}
When $\lambda^{\rm reg} > \lambda^0_p/\lambda$, the regulatory
constraint dominates in expansion, compressing expansion variance
without affecting contraction variance. The variance ratio becomes
$\mathcal{R}^{\rm reg} = \lambda^2\lambda^{0\,4}_p /
\lambda^{\rm reg\,2}$, which can fall below 1 when regulation is
sufficiently tight.

\noindent\textbf{Separation property under regulation.} The regulatory
floor enters through $\alpha$ and $\gamma$ but not through $\beta$.
The separation property is therefore preserved: regulation changes the
asymmetry channel while leaving the persistence channel unchanged.
The data confirms this. Under deterministic detrending, the stagnation
subperiod gives $\hat{\phi}_{\rm det} = 1.016$, comparable to the
pre-GFC values ($0.974$--$0.988$), while $|\hat{\gamma}|/\hat{\alpha}$
shifts from $+0.44$ to $-0.38$. The regulatory intervention changed
one channel without affecting the other, exactly as the separation
property predicts.

\subsection{Finding 5: IGARCH boundary}
\label{sec:finding5}

The secular stagnation period ($g_{\rm lit} = 0.3\%$) provides a test
of the IGARCH boundary prediction: as $g \to 0$, $\beta \to 1$. Under
deterministic detrending, $\hat{\phi}_{\rm det} = 1.016$ and
$\hat{\beta}_{\rm det} = \hat{\phi}^2_{\rm det} = 1.032$ in this
subperiod --- at or slightly above unity. The model predicts
$\beta = 1/(1+0.003)^2 = 0.994$; the observed value exceeds this and,
strictly speaking, lies outside the model's stationary region. This
should be read as a finite-sample estimation artefact rather than a
structural prediction: in a 60-quarter window with $g \approx 0$, the
AR(1) coefficient is estimated close to the unit-root boundary where
standard OLS is biased upward. The qualitative finding stands: the
stagnation period exhibits the most persistent dealer balance sheet
dynamics of all subperiods, consistent with the IGARCH boundary
prediction for a near-zero growth environment.

\subsection{Limitations and future work}
\label{sec:empirical_limits}

The within-country approach tests $\partial(|\gamma|/\alpha)/\partial
g = 0$ (confirmed in the pre-GFC sample) but cannot test
$\partial\beta/\partial\lambda = 0$, which requires cross-country
variation in $\lambda$. The full cross-sectional test --- matching
dealer balance sheet GARCH estimates to Penn World Table TFP growth and
\citet{wang2017} experimental loss aversion estimates across countries
--- is deferred to the companion empirical paper. The implied
$\hat{\lambda} = 1.20$ is below the Adrian-Shin range $[1.4, 1.7]$,
reflecting the broader Z.1 population; estimation on primary dealer
data specifically may produce higher values. The variance ratio result
is robust to HP filtering and positive threshold perturbations but
sensitive to linear detrending, an honest limitation.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper derives boom-bust asymmetry in dealer balance sheet volatility
as an equilibrium theorem from a single behavioural primitive: the kink
in the value function at a reference point. Two structural conditions
are jointly necessary. The bilateral zero-sum structure
(Assumption~\ref{ass:zerosum}) places dealer and speculator on opposite
sides of the kink simultaneously --- a derived result, not an assumption
--- generating a variance ratio of $\lambda^4$ between expansion and
contraction. The trend growth path provides mean-reversion at rate
$1/(1+g)$, pinning persistence to the economy's growth rate. Together
they deliver the separation property: $|\gamma|/\alpha$ identifies loss
aversion $\lambda$ and $\beta$ identifies trend growth $g$, with zero
cross-derivatives and no external instruments required
(Table~\ref{tab:identification_comparison}). Empirical analysis confirms
all five structural predictions using Federal Reserve Z.1 data
(1952--2025), and the implied $\hat{\lambda} = 1.20$ is the first
structural market-based estimate of dealer loss aversion.

The IGARCH boundary ($\beta \to 1$ as $g \to 0$) provides the first
preference-based microfoundation for integrated volatility processes.
Near-unit-root volatility persistence in low-growth episodes is not an
arbitrary statistical feature but the structural regime of a stagnant
economy. Importantly, the asymmetry coefficient $\gamma$ is independent
of $g$: a stagnant economy exhibits the same boom-bust asymmetry as a
growing one but with permanently persistent shocks.

The model delivers structural implications for institutional behaviour
and policy. For institutions, the separation property implies that the
two components of dealer balance sheet volatility --- the boom-bust
asymmetry and the persistence of shocks --- respond to fundamentally
different forces and should be monitored and managed separately. A
dealer observing rising $|\gamma|/\alpha$ without a change in $\beta$ is
experiencing a shift in effective loss aversion --- whether from internal
stress, changing risk appetite, or regulatory pressure; a rise in
$\beta$ without a change in $|\gamma|/\alpha$ signals a deterioration in
the growth environment. The model also predicts cross-sectional
variation in institutional structure: concentrated dealer markets with
infrequent limit resets should exhibit stronger variance asymmetry than
fragmented markets with frequent resets, because Stackelberg pricing
power --- which prevents loss aversion from being competed away --- is
stronger in concentrated settings. Post-GFC regulatory homogenisation,
which replaced idiosyncratic loss-aversion-driven limits with a common
regulatory floor, should have compressed cross-sectional dispersion in
dealer price impact relative to the pre-GFC period. Both predictions
are testable against desk-level regulatory data.

For policy, the separation property provides a structural basis for
non-redundant calibration of macroprudential and growth instruments.
Tightening VaR limits (reducing effective $\lambda$) reduces the
asymmetry ratio $|\gamma|/\alpha$ without affecting the persistence
coefficient $\beta$; growth-enhancing structural reforms reduce $\beta$
without affecting $|\gamma|/\alpha$. The two instruments operate on
orthogonal structural targets, cannot substitute for each other, and
their effects are separately attributable from observed volatility
dynamics. An observed change in $|\gamma|/\alpha$ with stable $\beta$ is
the structural signature of a macroprudential intervention; a change in
$\beta$ with stable $|\gamma|/\alpha$ is the signature of a growth
shock --- attributable in real time from dealer balance sheet data.
This is a sharper conclusion than the conventional view that financial
stability and growth policy involve fundamental trade-offs. The
IGARCH boundary adds a third dimension: when estimated volatility
persistence approaches one, the model identifies this as the signature
of near-zero trend growth --- a real-time diagnostic for secular
stagnation regimes that macroprudential policy alone cannot address.
Whether reducing $|\gamma|/\alpha$ is unambiguously desirable requires a
welfare function the model does not specify; some boom-bust asymmetry
reflects rational intermediary caution and the high-variance expansion
that enables price discovery. The normative question of the optimal
$\lambda$ and $g$ is deferred; what the model delivers is the structural
result that the two policy choices are orthogonal by construction.

Four limitations should be stated clearly. First, the persistence
identification is a leading-order approximation with error bounded below
6\% for moderate deviations (Proposition~\ref{prop:linearisation}); all qualitative results
and the separation property hold exactly. Second, the paper derives a
recursion for the conditional variance of detrended \emph{wealth
deviations}, not equity return variance; the cancellation result
establishes that return variance is regime-invariant, so the structural
asymmetry operates through wealth dynamics rather than return dynamics.
Third, the within-country approach tests $\partial(|\gamma|/\alpha)/
\partial g = 0$ but cannot test $\partial\beta/\partial\lambda = 0$,
which requires cross-country variation in $\lambda$. The full
cross-sectional test --- matching dealer balance sheet GARCH estimates
to Penn World Table TFP growth and \citet{wang2017} experimental loss
aversion estimates across countries --- is the primary agenda for the
companion empirical paper. Fourth, the loss aversion parameter $\lambda$
is treated as constant within each regulatory regime. In practice,
$\lambda$ may vary with balance sheet state --- experimental evidence
suggests loss aversion intensifies under stress \citep{cohn2015} ---
or with regulatory regime shifts (Basel III may have permanently changed
effective institutional $\lambda$, not merely imposed a binding floor).
Market structure evolution also matters: the growing share of
non-loss-averse intermediaries such as principal trading firms likely
reduces the effective $\lambda$ of the broader dealer population
\citep{baron2019}, compressing the cross-sectional variance asymmetry
in fragmented markets. The pre-GFC asymmetry ratio stability
($0.42$--$0.45$ across two decades) provides evidence that $\lambda$
was approximately constant in that era, but a two-state extension
allowing $\lambda^+ \neq \lambda^-$ across regimes is a natural
direction for future work. The model's scope is limited to liquid, transparent dealer
markets where VaR-constrained balance sheet management drives price
impact; markets with significant information asymmetry require the
adverse selection channel that is outside the present model's scope.

\section{Robustness: Verifying the Necessary Structural Conditions}
\label{app:robustness}

\subsection*{Effect of each design choice on the structural results}

\noindent\textbf{Choice 1 --- Linear price impact.} Nonlinear price
impact breaks the exact cancellation result: the product
$\lambda^{\rm eff}_p \cdot m^*_t$ is no longer $\mu_t/2$ in both regimes.
The variance recursion becomes a Power ARCH (PARCH) specification.
The boom-bust variance asymmetry survives because the regime-dependent
bound structure is preserved; both comparative static directions are
maintained.

\noindent\textbf{Choice 2 --- Quadratic cost (price impact).} Without
price impact ($\lambda^0_p \to 0$), the speculator's problem has no
finite interior solution and the $\beta$ channel collapses. Result:
Threshold-ARCH(1). The trend mean-reversion channel --- the
structural source of $\beta = f(g)$ --- is disrupted. The
$\partial|\gamma|/\partial\lambda > 0$ direction is preserved. This
choice is \emph{a necessary condition for the persistence identification}.

\noindent\textbf{Choice 3 --- Position closing.} Carry-over positions
introduce additional lags, extending the recursion to Threshold-GARCH
$(n,1)$. Both comparative static directions are preserved in each lag.

\noindent\textbf{Choice 4 --- Zero-sum condition.} Spread income $s > 0$
breaks zero-sum and admits a third regime in which both agents can
simultaneously be on the same side of the kink. Result: three-regime
asymmetric variance recursion. The $\partial\beta/\partial g < 0$
direction is preserved; the loss aversion effect is enriched with three
separate $\lambda$-dependent asymmetry terms.

\noindent\textbf{Choice 5 --- Regime structure.} The regime structure is
inherited from Choices 3, 4, and 6. When the shared reference point is
intact, regime separation holds and the variance asymmetry is preserved
regardless of the number of lags or the presence of spread income.

\noindent\textbf{Choice 6 --- Shared reference point.} Replacing the
shared trend growth path with own-history reference points eliminates
the trend deflation from the deviation recursion. The
persistence parameter becomes $\beta = \rho^2$ where $\rho$ is the
own-history mean-reversion coefficient, severing the structural link
between $\beta$ and $g$. This choice is \emph{a necessary condition for the
persistence identification}: removing it eliminates the paper's second
main result.

\noindent\textbf{Choice 7 --- Identical $\lambda$.} Heterogeneous loss
aversion $\lambda^{MM} \neq \lambda^S$: the variance asymmetry is driven
by $\lambda^{MM}$ alone, since $\lambda^S$ cancels in the speculator's
first-order condition (Remark~\ref{rem:mm_assignment}). Both comparative
static directions are preserved.

\noindent\textbf{Choice 8 --- Constant $g$.} Time-varying $g_t$
delivers $\beta_t = 1/(1+g_t)^2$ period by period, yielding a
Markov-Switching Threshold-GARCH with growth cycles as the state variable.
The separation property holds period by period: $\beta_t$ depends only
on $g_t$ and the asymmetry ratio depends only on $\lambda$.

\noindent\textbf{Choice 9 --- MM zero-profit benchmark.} A monopolist MM
yields an identical $\lambda^0_p$ in the linear Kyle framework. Both
comparative static directions are fully preserved.

\noindent\textbf{Choice 10 --- Public signal.} Under private information,
adverse selection scales both $\alpha$ and $|\gamma|$ uniformly by
$1/(1+\delta)$ where $\delta$ is the adverse selection premium. The
ratio $|\gamma|/\alpha = (\lambda^4-1)/\lambda^4$ is preserved
exactly, as is the separation property.

\noindent\textbf{Choice 11 --- Stationarity ($g > 0$).} As $g \to 0$,
$\beta \to 1$ (IGARCH boundary, Proposition~\ref{prop:igarch}). The
$\partial\beta/\partial g$ derivative diverges at $g = 0$, reflecting
that the persistence effect is strongest precisely in stagnant economies.
The $\partial|\gamma|/\partial\lambda > 0$ direction is preserved, and
$|\gamma|$ is independent of $g$ (verified symbolically).

\section{Technical Appendix}
\label{app:proofs}

This appendix collects all proofs omitted from the main text. The
structure mirrors the main text: Lemmas A.1--A.4 establish the
conditional distribution machinery; Propositions A.1--A.5 prove
the main results; Proposition A.5 states the linearisation
approximation with explicit error bounds. All symbolic results
have been verified symbolically.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Standing assumptions throughout the appendix.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(A1)] \textit{Joint Gaussianity.} $\mu_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,
    \sigma^2_\mu)$ and $\varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0,
    \sigma^2_\varepsilon)$ are mutually independent. The return
    innovation is $\varepsilon^r_t = \frac{1}{2}\mu_t +
    \varepsilon_t$.
  \item[(A2)] \textit{Deviation recursion.} $D_t = D_{t-1}/(1+g) +
    A^r\sigma^2_\mu + B^r\mu_t\varepsilon_t$, where $r \in \{+,-\}$
    denotes the regime determined by $\operatorname{sign}(D_{t-1})$,
    and $A^r$, $B^r$ are the regime-specific coefficients of
    equations~\eqref{eq:Ar_coeff}--\eqref{eq:Br_coeff}.
  \item[(A3)] \textit{Isserlis.} For any zero-mean bivariate Gaussian
    $(X,Y)$: $\mathbb{E}[X^2Y^2] = \operatorname{Var}(X)
    \operatorname{Var}(Y) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(X,Y)^2$.
\end{enumerate}

% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\subsection{Conditional distribution lemmas}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\begin{lemma}[Conditional distribution of $(\mu_t, \varepsilon_t)$
  given $\varepsilon^r_t$]
\label{lem:cond_dist}
Under Assumption~(A1), the conditional distribution is bivariate
Gaussian with moments:
\begin{align}
  \mathbb{E}[\mu_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t]
    &= \rho_\mu\,\varepsilon^r_t, \qquad
  \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t]
    = \rho_\varepsilon\,\varepsilon^r_t
    \label{eq:cond_means} \\
  \operatorname{Var}(\mu_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t)
    &= \frac{4\sigma^2_\varepsilon\sigma^2_\mu}
       {4\sigma^2_\varepsilon + \sigma^2_\mu}, \qquad
  \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t)
    = \frac{\sigma^2_\varepsilon\sigma^2_\mu}
       {4\sigma^2_\varepsilon + \sigma^2_\mu}
    \label{eq:cond_vars} \\
  \operatorname{Cov}(\mu_t, \varepsilon_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t)
    &= \frac{-2\sigma^2_\varepsilon\sigma^2_\mu}
       {4\sigma^2_\varepsilon + \sigma^2_\mu}
    \label{eq:cond_cov}
\end{align}
where $\rho_\mu \equiv \sigma^2_\mu/(2\bar\sigma^2)$,
$\rho_\varepsilon \equiv \sigma^2_\varepsilon/\bar\sigma^2$, and
$\bar\sigma^2 \equiv \sigma^2_\mu/4 + \sigma^2_\varepsilon$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By standard Gaussian conditioning. Since $(\mu_t, \varepsilon_t)$
are jointly Gaussian and independent, and $\varepsilon^r_t =
\frac{1}{2}\mu_t + \varepsilon_t$ is a linear combination,
$(\mu_t, \varepsilon_t, \varepsilon^r_t)$ is multivariate Gaussian.
The cross-covariances are $\operatorname{Cov}(\mu_t, \varepsilon^r_t)
= \sigma^2_\mu/2$ and $\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_t,
\varepsilon^r_t) = \sigma^2_\varepsilon$. Dividing by
$\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon^r_t) = \bar\sigma^2$ gives
\eqref{eq:cond_means}. The conditional variances and covariance
follow from the Schur complement formula applied to the joint
covariance matrix. \textit{Consistency check:}
$\frac{1}{2}\rho_\mu + \rho_\varepsilon =
\sigma^2_\mu/(4\bar\sigma^2) + \sigma^2_\varepsilon/\bar\sigma^2
= \bar\sigma^2/\bar\sigma^2 = 1$, confirming
$\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^r_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t] = \varepsilon^r_t$.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Conditional expectation of $\mu_t\varepsilon_t$]
\label{lem:cond_mueps}
Under Assumption~(A1):
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[\mu_t\varepsilon_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t]
  = \rho_\mu\bigl[\rho_\varepsilon(\varepsilon^r_t)^2
    - \sigma^2_\varepsilon\bigr]
  \label{eq:cond_mueps}
\end{equation}
This expression is \emph{even} in $\varepsilon^r_t$: it depends only
on $(\varepsilon^r_t)^2$, not on the sign of $\varepsilon^r_t$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Decompose $\mu_t\varepsilon_t = \operatorname{Cov}(\mu_t,
\varepsilon_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t) + \mathbb{E}[\mu_t \mid
\varepsilon^r_t]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t]$.
Substituting \eqref{eq:cond_cov} and \eqref{eq:cond_means}:
\[
\mathbb{E}[\mu_t\varepsilon_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t]
= -\frac{2\sigma^2_\varepsilon\sigma^2_\mu}
       {4\sigma^2_\varepsilon + \sigma^2_\mu}
  + \rho_\mu\varepsilon^r_t \cdot \rho_\varepsilon\varepsilon^r_t
= -\rho_\mu\sigma^2_\varepsilon
  + \rho_\mu\rho_\varepsilon(\varepsilon^r_t)^2
= \rho_\mu\bigl[\rho_\varepsilon(\varepsilon^r_t)^2
  - \sigma^2_\varepsilon\bigr].
\]
Evenness follows immediately since the right-hand side contains only
$(\varepsilon^r_t)^2$.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[$\operatorname{Var}(\mu_t\varepsilon_t \mid
  \varepsilon^r_t)$ via Isserlis]
\label{lem:var_mueps}
Under Assumptions~(A1) and (A3):
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(\mu_t\varepsilon_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t)
  = \Sigma\bigl[H_0 + H_2(\varepsilon^r_t)^2\bigr]
  \label{eq:var_mueps}
\end{equation}
where $\Sigma \equiv \sigma^2_\varepsilon\sigma^2_\mu /
(4\sigma^2_\varepsilon + \sigma^2_\mu)^2$,
$H_0 \equiv 8\sigma^2_\varepsilon\sigma^2_\mu$, and
$H_2 \equiv 4(16\sigma^4_\varepsilon + \sigma^4_\mu) /
(4\sigma^2_\varepsilon + \sigma^2_\mu)$. In particular
$\operatorname{Var}(\mu_t\varepsilon_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t) > 0$
and both $H_0 > 0$ and $H_2 > 0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $u \equiv \mu_t - \mathbb{E}[\mu_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t]$ and
$v \equiv \varepsilon_t - \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_t \mid
\varepsilon^r_t]$ be the conditional residuals, with
$(u,v) \sim \mathcal{N}(0, V)$ where $V$ is the $2\times2$
conditional covariance matrix of Lemma~\ref{lem:cond_dist}. Denote
conditional means $m_\mu = \rho_\mu\varepsilon^r_t$ and
$m_\varepsilon = \rho_\varepsilon\varepsilon^r_t$. Then
$\mu_t\varepsilon_t = (u+m_\mu)(v+m_\varepsilon)$. Expanding:
\[
\mathbb{E}[(\mu_t\varepsilon_t)^2 \mid \varepsilon^r_t]
= \mathbb{E}[u^2v^2] + m^2_\varepsilon\operatorname{Var}(u)
  + m^2_\mu\operatorname{Var}(v)
  + (m_\mu m_\varepsilon)^2
  + 2m_\mu m_\varepsilon\operatorname{Cov}(u,v),
\]
where all odd moments of $(u,v)$ vanish by Gaussianity. Applying
Assumption~(A3): $\mathbb{E}[u^2v^2] = \operatorname{Var}(u)
\operatorname{Var}(v) + 2\operatorname{Cov}(u,v)^2$. Subtracting
$(\mathbb{E}[\mu_t\varepsilon_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t])^2$ and
collecting terms in $(\varepsilon^r_t)^0$ and $(\varepsilon^r_t)^2$
yields \eqref{eq:var_mueps}. Positivity of $H_0$ and $H_2$ is
immediate from the positivity of all parameters.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Slope cancellation]
\label{lem:slope_cancel}
The partial derivative of the standardised threshold $z_t =
-\mu^r_{D_t}/\psi^r_t$ with respect to $(\varepsilon^r_t)^2$ is
regime-invariant:
\[
\frac{\partial z_t}{\partial(\varepsilon^r_t)^2}
= -\frac{\rho_\mu\rho_\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\Sigma H_0}}
  \qquad \text{for both } r \in \{+,-\}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
From Lemma~\ref{lem:cond_mueps}, $\delta^r \equiv \partial
\mu^r_{D_t}/\partial(\varepsilon^r_t)^2 = B^r\rho_\mu\rho_\varepsilon$.
The conditional standard deviation of $D_t$ at $\varepsilon^r_t = 0$
is $\psi^r_0 = B^r\sqrt{\Sigma H_0}$. Therefore
$\partial z_t/\partial(\varepsilon^r_t)^2 = -\delta^r/\psi^r_0 =
-B^r\rho_\mu\rho_\varepsilon/(B^r\sqrt{\Sigma H_0}) =
-\rho_\mu\rho_\varepsilon/\sqrt{\Sigma H_0}$,
where $B^r$ cancels exactly in both regimes.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Asymmetric drift]
\label{prop:asym_drift}
Define the regime-specific stationary points $D^*_r = A^r\sigma^2_\mu(1+g)/g$.
Then $D^*_+/D^*_- = \lambda^2$ and both are strictly positive. The
process $\{D_t\}$ satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] In contraction ($D_{t-1}<0$): $\mathbb{E}[D_t \mid
    D_{t-1}] > D_{t-1}$ unconditionally (submartingale).
  \item[(ii)] In expansion ($D_{t-1}\geq0$): $\mathbb{E}[D_t \mid
    D_{t-1}] \gtrless D_{t-1}$ as $D_{t-1} \lessgtr D^*_+$.
  \item[(iii)] $D^*_+ > D^*_- > 0$: the economy has a positive bias.
  \item[(iv)] The deflation factor $1/(1+g)$ is identical in both
    regimes; asymmetry enters only through the drift $A^r\sigma^2_\mu$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Assumption~(A1), $\mathbb{E}[\mu_t\varepsilon_t] = 0$ (independence
and zero means), so $\mathbb{E}[D_t \mid D_{t-1}] = D_{t-1}/(1+g) +
A^r\sigma^2_\mu$. The fixed point $\mathbb{E}[D_t \mid D_{t-1}] = D_{t-1}$
gives $D^*_r = A^r\sigma^2_\mu(1+g)/g > 0$ since $A^r, \sigma^2_\mu,
g > 0$. The ratio $D^*_+/D^*_- = A^+/A^- = \lambda^2$ follows from
equations~\eqref{eq:Ar_coeff}--\eqref{eq:Br_coeff}.

\textit{Part (i).} For $D_{t-1} < 0$: $\mathbb{E}[D_t \mid D_{t-1}]
- D_{t-1} = -D_{t-1}g/(1+g) + A^-\sigma^2_\mu > 0$, since both
terms are strictly positive.

\textit{Part (ii).} For $D_{t-1} \geq 0$: the same expression has sign
equal to $\operatorname{sign}(D^*_+ - D_{t-1})$.

\textit{Parts (iii) and (iv).} Both follow directly from the definitions
and $A^+/A^- = \lambda^2 > 1$.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Variance asymmetry]
\label{prop:var_asym}\label{prop:Vt_ratio}
Under Assumptions (A1)--(A3), the conditional variance of $D_t$
given $(\varepsilon^r_t, D_{t-1})$ satisfies:
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{Var}(D_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t, D_{t-1})
  = (B^r)^2\,\Sigma\bigl[H_0 + H_2(\varepsilon^r_t)^2\bigr]
  \label{eq:VarDt}
\end{equation}
and the ratio across regimes is:
\[
\frac{\operatorname{Var}(D_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t, D_{t-1},\,
  D_{t-1}\geq0)}
     {\operatorname{Var}(D_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t, D_{t-1},\,
  D_{t-1}<0)}
= \frac{(B^+)^2}{(B^-)^2} = \lambda^4.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
From Assumption~(A2), $D_t - D_{t-1}/(1+g) - A^r\sigma^2_\mu =
B^r\mu_t\varepsilon_t$. Since $D_{t-1}$ and $\varepsilon^r_t$ are
$\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$-measurable or determined by the current period's
shock distribution, and $B^r$ is $\mathcal{F}_{t-1}$-measurable:
\[
\operatorname{Var}(D_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t, D_{t-1})
= (B^r)^2\operatorname{Var}(\mu_t\varepsilon_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t).
\]
Substituting Lemma~\ref{lem:var_mueps} gives \eqref{eq:VarDt}. The
ratio $(B^+)^2/(B^-)^2 = [\lambda/(2\lambda^0_p)]^2 /
[1/(2\lambda\lambda^0_p)]^2 = \lambda^4$ follows directly.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Stationarity condition]
\label{prop:stationarity}
The Threshold-GARCH process for $V_t$ is covariance-stationary if
and only if:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi \;<\; \Phi_{\max} \;\equiv\;
  \frac{2g\lambda^2(g+2)}{(1+g)^2(\lambda^4+1)}
  \label{eq:Phi_max_proof}
\end{equation}
Three corollaries: (a) $\Phi_{\max} \to 0$ as $g \to 0$, recovering
the IGARCH boundary; (b) at $\lambda = 1$, $\Phi_{\max} =
g(g+2)/(1+g)^2 > 0$; (c) $\Phi_{\max}$ is strictly decreasing
in $\lambda$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The standard covariance-stationarity condition for a Threshold-GARCH
process is $\alpha + \gamma\pi^* + \beta < 1$, where $\pi^*$ is the
ergodic probability of contraction. At the symmetric stationary point
$\pi^* = 1/2$, this becomes $\alpha + \gamma/2 + \beta < 1$. From
Theorem~1: $\alpha + \gamma/2 = \Phi(\lambda^4+1)/(2\lambda^2)$.
Substituting $\beta = 1/(1+g)^2$ and solving for $\Phi$ gives
\eqref{eq:Phi_max_proof}. Corollary~(a): substitute $g=0$.
Corollary~(b): substitute $\lambda=1$ and simplify. Corollary~(c):
$\partial\Phi_{\max}/\partial\lambda = 2g(g+2)(1+g)^{-2}
\cdot\partial[\lambda^2/(\lambda^4+1)]/\partial\lambda$;
the bracketed expression has derivative
$\lambda(2-2\lambda^4)/(\lambda^4+1)^2 < 0$ for $\lambda > 1$.
\qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[IGARCH boundary]
\label{prop:igarch}
As $g \to 0$, $\beta = 1/(1+g)^2 \to 1$ and the process converges
to a Threshold-IGARCH with $\alpha + \gamma/2 + \beta = 1$. The
asymmetry coefficient $\gamma$ satisfies $\partial\gamma/\partial g
= 0$: it is independent of the growth rate. A stagnant economy
therefore exhibits the same leverage asymmetry as a growing one,
but with permanently persistent variance shocks.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$\beta = 1/(1+g)^2 \to 1$ as $g \to 0$ is immediate. That
$\partial\gamma/\partial g = 0$ follows because $\gamma =
\Phi(1-\lambda^4)/\lambda^2$ and $\Phi$ does not depend on $g$
(it contains only $\sigma_\mu$, $\sigma_\varepsilon$, $\lambda^0_p$,
and $\lambda$).
\qed
\end{proof}

\subsection{Linearisation and error bounds}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\begin{proposition}[Linearisation approximation and error bound]
\label{prop:linearisation}
\textbf{Exact part.} For each realised regime $r \in \{+,-\}$,
the conditional variance of $D_t$ is:
\begin{equation}
  V^r_t \equiv \operatorname{Var}(D_t \mid \varepsilon^r_t,
  D_{t-1},\, \text{regime}\,=r)
  = (B^r)^2\,\Sigma\bigl[H_0 + H_2(\varepsilon^r_t)^2\bigr].
  \label{eq:Vt_exact}
\end{equation}
This holds exactly under Assumptions (A1)--(A3).

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Approximate part.} The expected conditional
variance $\mathbb{E}[V_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]$ equals
$\pi_{t-1}V^-_t + (1-\pi_{t-1})V^+_t$, where $\pi_{t-1} =
\Phi(z_{t-1})$ and $z_{t-1}$ is the standardised threshold.
Define $\bar{V} \equiv \Sigma(H_0 + H_2\bar\sigma^2) =
\sigma^2_\varepsilon\sigma^2_\mu$ and expand $\Phi(z_{t-1})$ to
first order around $(D_{t-2} = D^*_r,\, \varepsilon^r_{t-1} = 0)$:
\begin{equation}
  \pi_{t-1} \approx \pi^* +
  \phi(z^*)\left[-\frac{D_{t-2} - D^*_r}{(1+g)\psi^r_0}
  - \frac{\rho_\mu\rho_\varepsilon}{\sqrt{\Sigma H_0}}
    (\varepsilon^r_{t-1})^2\right]
  \label{eq:pi_linearised}
\end{equation}
where $\psi^r_0 = B^r\sqrt{\Sigma H_0}$. Substituting
\eqref{eq:pi_linearised} into $\mathbb{E}[V_t \mid
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]$ and identifying $V_{t-1} \propto
\pi_{t-2} \cdot [(B^-)^2 - (B^+)^2]\bar{V}$ yields a
recursion of the form:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[V_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
  \approx \omega + \alpha(\varepsilon^r_{t-1})^2
  + \gamma(\varepsilon^r_{t-1})^2\mathbf{1}[D_{t-2}<0]
  + \beta V_{t-1}
  \label{eq:Vt_recursion_approx}
\end{equation}
with $\beta = 1/(1+g)^2$ arising from the $D_{t-2}/(1+g)$ term
in $z_{t-1}$, which propagates through two lags to $V_{t-1}$.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Error bound.} The error in approximating
$\pi_{t-1}$ by \eqref{eq:pi_linearised} satisfies, by the
Taylor remainder theorem:
\[
|\pi_{t-1} - \pi^{\,\mathrm{approx}}_{t-1}|
\;\leq\;
\frac{\phi(0)}{2\sqrt{e}}\,(z_{t-1} - z^*)^2.
\]
For deviations $|D_{t-2} - D^*_r| \leq \delta\,\psi^r_0(1+g)$,
the implied error in $\mathbb{E}[V_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]$ is:
\[
\left|\mathbb{E}[V_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
  - \mathbb{E}[V_t \mid \mathcal{F}_{t-1}]^{\,\mathrm{approx}}\right|
\;\leq\;
\bar{V}\,\bigl|(B^-)^2 - (B^+)^2\bigr|
\,\frac{\phi(0)}{2\sqrt{e}}\,\delta^2.
\]
At $\lambda = 2.25$, $\lambda^0_p = 0.8$, $\sigma_\mu = 1$,
$\sigma_\varepsilon = 0.5$, and $\delta = 0.5$, the relative
error is $5.8\%$. The approximation is accurate when the
economy is within $O(\psi^r_0)$ of the stationary point ---
the empirically relevant regime for moderate deviations from
trend.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\textit{Exact part.} Follows directly from Proposition
\ref{prop:var_asym} (Lemma~\ref{lem:var_mueps} and
Assumption~(A2)).

\textit{Error bound.} The function $\Phi: \mathbb{R} \to (0,1)$
is twice continuously differentiable with $\Phi'' =
-z\phi(z)$. By Taylor's theorem with remainder:
$|\Phi(z) - \Phi(z^*) - \phi(z^*)(z-z^*)| \leq
\sup_\xi|\Phi''(\xi)| \cdot (z-z^*)^2/2$. Since
$|\Phi''(z)| = |z|\phi(z) \leq \phi(0)/\sqrt{e}$ (attained at
$z = \pm 1$), the bound follows. The remainder maps to the
$V_t$ recursion by multiplying through by $|(B^-)^2 -
(B^+)^2|\cdot\bar{V}$.
\qed
\end{proof}
