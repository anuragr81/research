Loss Aversion, Endogenous Reference Points, and
Boom-Bust Asymmetry in Financial Wealth Dynamics
================================================================

Financial markets exhibit a well-documented asymmetry between expansions and
contractions: boom periods are volatile, turbulent, and fast; bust periods are
compressed, slow, and grinding. The GARCH structure of volatility dynamics has
been derived from diverse agent beliefs in rational belief equilibrium models
and from heterogeneous agent switching. Bayesian approaches with regime
uncertainty explore the belief channel — how learning and uncertainty generate
asymmetric dynamics. This paper explores a complementary channel: the
preference channel. Even when agents are certain about their positions — as
dealers in liquid markets are, observing their P&L relative to a shared
benchmark in real time — loss aversion generates boom-bust asymmetry through
the price-setting mechanism. The contribution is to isolate this channel cleanly
and derive its exact structural consequences.

Cumulative Prospect Theory has the value function kink, reference dependence,
and asymmetric loss weighting that should generate boom-bust dynamics, with
extensive experimental support across cultures. The difficulty is that its
full apparatus creates tractability problems in dynamic market equilibrium.
In the spirit of Koszegi and Rabin (2006, 2007), this paper retains the value
function kink and replaces the rational expectations reference point with the
economy-wide Schumpeterian wealth trend, publicly known from national accounts
data and growing at a constant rate. The paper is scoped to liquid
exchange-traded settings where dealer risk management is the primary source of
price impact asymmetry. Unlike Barberis, Huang and Santos (2001), who generate
time-varying risk aversion from individual performance histories in a
representative agent consumption CAPM, this paper derives asymmetric dynamics
from fixed dealer loss aversion against a shared social benchmark in a
strategic market equilibrium with exact closed-form mappings.

Two structural conditions are jointly necessary. First, the zero-sum trading
game guarantees as a derived theorem — not an assumption — that the market
maker and speculator always sit on opposite sides of the kink simultaneously,
generating the boom-bust variance asymmetry. Second, the Schumpeterian trend
pins volatility persistence to the growth rate through the exact mapping
beta = 1/(1+g)^2. The separation property follows: d(beta)/d(lambda) = 0
and d(|gamma|/alpha)/dg = 0 — persistence and asymmetry are separately
identified from different economic primitives without auxiliary assumptions.

Three structural implications follow from these mappings, independent of
empirical calibration. The asymmetry ratio |gamma|/alpha = (lambda^4-1)/lambda^4
provides a structural estimator for market-level loss aversion from dealer
balance sheet data once g is measured from trend TFP growth. The IGARCH
boundary — beta -> 1 as g -> 0 — is a structural early warning indicator:
when estimated persistence approaches one, the model says trend growth is
approaching zero, reframing financial stability monitoring as a forward-looking
structural assessment rather than a backward-looking description. And because
beta and |gamma|/alpha are orthogonal by construction, macroprudential and
growth policy optimisation problems are structurally independent — neither
instrument substitutes for the other and neither contaminates the other's target.

The paper isolates the preference channel cleanly: loss aversion alone, without
belief dynamics, is sufficient to generate boom-bust asymmetry with structurally
identified parameters. Testing whether the separation property holds in dealer
balance sheet data — whether beta co-varies with trend TFP growth and |gamma|/alpha
co-varies with experimental loss aversion estimates across markets, with zero
cross-effects — is the paper's primary empirical direction.
