\section{Model claims}
\subsection*{The model in plain English}
The economy is a ladder of a fixed number of jobs, one worker in each, with income rising in rank. Incomes follow the Pareto pattern, and a lower tail index means a steeper ladder and more inequality. Each worker belongs to one of two status groups throughout a working life.

Each period creative destruction displaces every job holder with the same chance, whatever the rank, and each displaced worker takes one of three routes before holding a job again. The downgrade pays the bottom income. The return buys the former job back at an upskilling cost that rises with the income climbed. Displaced workers who choose the lottery are its entrants, and each pays the lottery cost and receives the former job of a partner, another entrant drawn at random. Closure lowers the chance that the partner comes from the other status group.

The lottery moves income between entrants without creating any, so the pool loses the lottery costs. When no partner's former income less the lottery cost falls below the bottom income, every entrant does at least as well in the lottery as in the downgrade, whatever the shape of utility. A richer entrant values the lottery no more than a poorer one, and the gap shrinks as the pool grows.

When displaced workers value income at its average, those who choose the return form one band of ranks. Routes are compared by lifetime value, so the return beats the downgrade exactly when its one-time upskilling cost is at most the income climbed times the lifetime multiplier. More inequality keeps in the lottery the entrants above the rank where the return starts to lose value, and can draw lower entrants back to the return when the upskilling cost is nearly proportional.

The scale of the upskilling cost and the shape of utility are not yet fixed.

\subsection*{Base case, two displaced workers}
Two displaced workers with former incomes $x_i<x_j$ form the pool. The table gives each entrant’s income on each route, with $\kappa$ the scale of the upskilling cost, which M7 and M38 set to one and issue 1 leaves open.

downgrade & $x_0$ & $x_0$ & M32 \\ \midrule

With two entrants the lottery is the exchange of their former jobs, so neither faces any risk.

The richer entrant gives up income in the exchange, while the poorer gains whenever the lottery cost is below the gap between their former incomes. The poorer entrant's lottery cost goes to the job holders above its former rank, the people whose favour the lottery stands for, and the exchange lifts it into the richer entrant's former job. The richer entrant still prefers the exchange to its other routes when its upskilling cost is at least the income it climbs and the poorer entrant's former income less the lottery cost is at least the bottom income, because the downgrade pays only the bottom income. The poorer entrant prefers the exchange when its former income is at least the lottery cost below the richer entrant's, whatever its upskilling cost. Both conditions hold for every nondecreasing utility, so the transfer from the richer entrant to the poorer rests on the bottom income rather than on a taste for risk.

Had the downgrade kept a share of former income, the richer entrant would refuse every partner paying less than that share plus the lottery cost, closing the path from the bottom to the top. Closure cannot act with two entrants, nor with two from one status group and one from the other, so it first acts with two in each.

\section{Idea summaries}
Each idea summary states one idea of the model in plain words. Every sentence records in \texttt{claims.yaml} the rows, Lean theorems or source premise it rests on, and uses the terms of the terminology table. \texttt{check.py} fails when a row or theorem statement a summary cites changes, until the summary is reviewed and re-stamped, so the summaries move with the claims.

\subsection{Why displaced workers choose the lottery}
A worker chooses the lottery only after losing a job to creative destruction. Job holders never sustain a pool, because the lottery creates no income and the richest entrant does better outside the pool.

For a displaced worker, the downgrade pays the bottom income, the lowest on the ladder. When no partner's former income less the lottery cost falls below the bottom income, the lottery does at least as well as the downgrade, whatever the shape of utility. Under that condition even a displaced worker who dislikes risk has no reason to take the downgrade, so the bottom income, not the source's S-shaped utility, drives the choice between the lottery and the downgrade.

Valuing income at its average, a displaced worker whose upskilling cost is at least the income climbed times the lifetime multiplier does no better on the return than on the downgrade, nor, under the condition on partners' former incomes, than in the lottery. With a cost exponent of at least one, the cost per unit of income climbed rises with rank, so this case, once it holds at a rank, holds at every higher rank.

The firm prefers upskilling to the lottery, because upskilling spending flows back to the firm whereas lottery spending is lost to the firm.

\subsection{What the lottery cost does to each player}
The lottery cost is the price of competing through connections, the one channel of mobility the firm does not control, and nobody in the model sets it. It is paid once by every entrant, to the job holders above the entrant's former rank, so it stays among workers and flows up the ladder.

For a displaced worker, a higher lottery cost lowers the worth of the lottery for every shape of utility, so some who would have entered return instead, and the firm receives their upskilling cost. The lottery cost sets the smallest gap in former income that an exchange can bridge with a gain to the poorer entrant. It also excludes the poorest, whose former income less the lottery cost falls below the bottom income, because the top prefers the downgrade to their job, and it excludes the top as a prize for anyone within the lottery cost of it, so the window of ranks that can exchange with the top narrows from both ends.

A job holder receives a share of the lottery cost of every entrant below, rising with rank, and is otherwise untouched by it until displaced. The two status groups face the same lottery cost, since closure acts on who can be a partner, not on the price. The firm never receives the lottery cost, but gains from a higher one, because more displaced workers upskill.

\section{Frequently asked questions}
Each answer rests sentence by sentence on the rows, Lean theorems or source premises recorded in \texttt{claims.yaml}, and is stamped like the summaries, so the answers move with the claims.

\paragraph{What does it mean that every entrant receives exactly one former job?} The lottery hands out exactly the former jobs of its entrants, one to each entrant, and no entrant receives their own former job back. So every job keeps exactly one worker, and each status group holds as many jobs after the lottery as before, though not the same ranks.

\paragraph{Can a pool have only one entrant, and what happens then?} Displacement strikes each job holder with the same chance, so a period can leave exactly one displaced worker in the lottery. The lottery is then unavailable, because the only former job in the pool is the entrant's own, and the entrant chooses between the return and the downgrade. Handing the lone entrant their own former job back at the lottery cost would let the lottery undercut the return whenever the lottery cost is below the upskilling cost, and the firm would then receive nothing.

\paragraph{What does the handling of a pool of one imply for larger pools?} The chance that fewer than two workers enter the lottery falls to zero as the number of workers grows, for any chance of entry strictly between zero and one. A displaced worker who enters and finds no partner takes the route it would otherwise have chosen, so the chance of an empty pool does not change who enters, and choices in pools of every size are as if every pool had two entrants or more. Handing back the lone entrant's own former job would instead give the lottery a guaranteed minimum that the return lacks, and draw more workers into the lottery in pools of every size.

\paragraph{Does a job holder expect to lose the job, and can the job holder protect it?} A job holder is displaced with the same chance in every period, whatever the rank, and nothing the job holder does changes that chance. The lottery never takes a job from a job holder, because only the former jobs of entrants are handed out.

\paragraph{Who sets the lottery cost, and does the firm's preference for upskilling survive it?} Nobody in the model sets the lottery cost, which is the price of competing through connections, the one channel of mobility the firm does not manage. The firm never receives the lottery cost, which goes to the job holders above the entrant, and a higher lottery cost leads more displaced workers to return and pay the firm the upskilling cost, so the firm's preference for upskilling over the lottery holds.

\paragraph{How does the choice between the lottery and upskilling look to the firm, to the displaced worker and to workers taken together?} The displaced worker compares the former income less the upskilling cost with the expected prize less the lottery cost, and so weighs the difference between the two costs against the difference between the prize and the former income. The firm receives the upskilling cost from a returner and nothing from an entrant, so it prefers the return whatever the two costs are. The job holders above the entrant receive the lottery cost, so they gain when the displaced worker enters, and workers taken together recover it and prefer the lottery whenever the displaced worker does.

\paragraph{How does a status group benefit from the lottery?} Through the lottery cost a status group gains only as far as its members hold the ranks above the entrants, because the lottery cost goes up the ladder to the job holders above the entrant whatever their group. The group benefits through ranks instead, because the lottery can move higher ranks from one group to the other, and with two entrants from each group the expected gap between a group's share of their former income and half the total shrinks with every lottery unless closure is complete. So a group holding the lower ranks gains ranks from an open lottery, and a group holding the higher ranks keeps them longer the stronger closure is.

\paragraph{Where does the lottery cost go?} The lottery cost stays among workers, in a closed economy in which all income passes between workers and the firm. Each entrant pays it to the job holders strictly above the entrant's former rank, in equal shares, and an entrant with nobody above pays all job holders equally. Such receipts do not depend on one's own route, so they change no result that rests on first-order dominance or on valuing income at its average.

\paragraph{Why does the model have only one social attribute?} The status group is the only order outside the market, and a separate set of brokers would have added a second one with its own rules and its own measurement. Paying the lottery cost to the job holders above the entrant uses rank, which the model already has, so the people whose favour the lottery stands for need no attribute of their own.

\paragraph{What is a status group, and how does it differ from rank?} Rank is the order of jobs on the ladder, set by the firm, whereas the status group is the order outside the market, inherited and not priced. Neither is a function of the other, and the status group acts only on access to partners in the lottery, the one allocation of jobs the firm does not make.

\paragraph{Why can closure not act with fewer than two entrants in each status group?} With two entrants the lottery is a sure exchange, and with two from one group and one from the other every reassignment moves two of the three jobs across the groups. Closure first acts with two entrants in each group, where one reassignment keeps every job within its group.
