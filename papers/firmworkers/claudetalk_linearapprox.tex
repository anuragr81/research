\large Working Paper}

\begin{abstract}
We develop a tractable three-player model connecting wage inequality,
worker upskilling decisions, and firm asset accumulation. A single
parameter $\mu \in (0,\tfrac{1}{2})$ --- the income share of the
lower-ranked worker group --- governs the entire wage distribution.
Workers allocate income between upskilling investment and participation
in a zero-sum displacement lottery. Risk preferences are captured through
a linearly approximated Friedman-Savage utility, preserving the sign
asymmetry between risk-seeking lower-income and risk-averse higher-income
workers while keeping all equilibrium objects in closed form. The
population mass ratio $W = i^*/(N-i^*)$ does not enter any individual
worker's budget or participation constraint, but enters the firm's asset
accumulation through the aggregate upskilling index
$\Phi(\mu) = (W+1)\kappa^{\min}(\mu)/R$, which is the population-weighted
workforce contribution to the firm's return on its wage bill. We address
two questions: how do workers respond to wage inequality $\mu$, and what
constraints does $\mu$ place on long-term growth? We derive fully explicit
steady-state equilibria and a complete set of signed comparative statics
for all three players.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Wage inequality is central to debates about human capital investment,
firm performance, and long-run economic growth. Standard growth models
treat income distribution as either exogenous or absent, leaving
unanswered the question of how a firm's wage policy feeds back into
the skill investments of its workers and ultimately into the firm's
own asset accumulation. This paper proposes a unified framework in
which a single wage inequality parameter $\mu$ connects all three
players --- a lower-income worker, a higher-income worker, and a firm
--- and delivers closed-form answers to two sharply posed questions.

The first question is how workers respond to wage inequality $\mu$.
When the firm changes the wage split, it simultaneously alters each
worker's income, upskilling investment, and lottery participation.
The model delivers signed predictions for upskilling fractions, lottery
stakes, net transfers, and risk premia as explicit functions of $\mu$.
A notable feature is the role of the population mass ratio $W$: it
does not enter any individual worker's budget constraint or participation
constraint, so individual upskilling fractions $\lambda_s^*(\mu)$ and
the minimum upskilling floor $\kappa^{\min}(\mu)$ are independent of
$W$. The population structure affects workers only through the lottery
pool size and the net transfer direction, both of which depend on $W$
through the prize mechanism. At the aggregate level, $W$ amplifies
the economy-wide human capital contribution through the upskilling
index $\Phi(\mu) = (W+1)\kappa^{\min}(\mu)/R$ --- the quantity the
firm internalises when setting wages.

The second question is what constraints $\mu$ places on long-term
growth. The firm's asset accumulation is driven by $\Phi(\mu)$, net
of depreciation and restructuring costs. Not all values of $\mu$
support positive growth: a viability condition must hold, depending
on $\mu$ through the upskilling floor $\kappa_0 = \kappa^{\min}(\mu_0)$
and on $W$ through $(W+1)$. Conditional on viability, the firm's
optimal $\mu^*$ is linear in the asset stock --- wealthier firms
enforce greater equality. Below the viability threshold, assets
collapse regardless of initial conditions, constituting a poverty trap.

The model uses a linear approximation of the second derivative of the
Friedman-Savage utility. This middle ground preserves the sign asymmetry
between workers' risk premia --- the lower-income worker has a negative
risk premium (risk-seeking) and the higher-income worker a positive one
(risk-averse) --- while keeping all equilibrium objects in closed form.
Crucially, risk preferences affect only the risk premia and not the
upskilling or accumulation equilibrium, a clean separation that follows
directly from the approximation structure.

\section{Model Setup}

\subsection{Players, Incomes, and the Role of $\mu$}

There are three strategic players: worker $L$, worker $H$, and a firm.
The firm holds asset stock $A_t$ and distributes total income $R$ to
workers each period. The firm chooses the income-split parameter
$\mu \in (0,\tfrac{1}{2})$:
\begin{equation}
  M_L = R\mu, \qquad M_H = R(1-\mu)
  \label{eq:incomes}
\end{equation}
Since $\mu < \tfrac{1}{2}$, worker $H$ always earns more than worker
$L$. Lower $\mu$ means greater wage inequality; higher $\mu$ means
greater equality. The income ratio $M_H/M_L = (1-\mu)/\mu$ is strictly
decreasing in $\mu$.

\begin{definition}[Wage inequality parameter]
$\mu \in (0,\tfrac{1}{2})$ is the income share of worker $L$, chosen
by the firm each period. Greater wage equality corresponds to higher $\mu$.
\end{definition}

In the underlying $N$-worker displacement model with Pareto income
ladder $m_i = x_0(1-i/(N+1))^{-1/\alpha}$ and Friedman-Savage class
barrier at rank $i^*$, the two-player reduction yields:
\begin{equation}
  \mu \approx \frac{\displaystyle\int_0^{i^*/N}
    x_0(1-s)^{-1/\alpha}\,ds}
  {\displaystyle\int_0^{1} x_0(1-s)^{-1/\alpha}\,ds}
  \label{eq:mu_micro}
\end{equation}
the lower hump's share of total representative income in the stationary
distribution. In the macroeconomic model $\mu$ is treated as the firm's
policy instrument.

\subsection{The Population Mass Ratio $W$}

The population mass ratio is:
\begin{equation}
  W = \frac{i^*}{N - i^*} \geq 1
  \label{eq:W_def}
\end{equation}
where $i^*$ is the class barrier rank. Under the Pareto distribution
the lower group is generically more numerous, so $W \geq 1$.

The parameter $W$ enters the model in two ways. First, it amplifies
worker $L$'s aggregate lottery stake in the prize pool, reflecting
that lower-rank workers generate more displacement events per period.
Second, it weights the lower group's upskilling investment in the
firm's aggregate upskilling index $\Phi(\mu) = (W+1)\kappa^{\min}(\mu)/R$.

Importantly, $W$ does \emph{not} enter any individual worker's budget
constraint or participation constraint. Each representative worker
invests $\kappa^{\min}(\mu)$ regardless of $W$. The population weighting
is an economy-level aggregation that the firm internalises, not an
individual incentive.

\subsection{Worker Budget and Allocation}

Each worker $s \in \{L, H\}$ allocates fraction $\lambda_s \in [0,1]$
of income $M_s$ to upskilling and the remainder to the lottery:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_s = \lambda_s M_s \quad \text{(upskilling)},
  \qquad
  \ell_s = (1-\lambda_s)M_s \quad \text{(lottery stake)}
  \label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
Upskilling produces output $f(\kappa_s) = \kappa_s^\gamma$,
$\gamma \in (0,1)$, with diminishing returns. The certain base income
after upskilling and human capital depreciation $\delta_w > 0$:
\begin{equation}
  b_s(\lambda_s) = \kappa_s + \kappa_s^\gamma - \delta_w
  \label{eq:base_income}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Zero-Sum Displacement Lottery}

The lottery represents the displacement mechanism from the $N$-worker
model. Workers contribute stakes to a common pool and one side wins the
entire pool each period. The pool is:
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda = W(1-\lambda_L)M_L + (1-\lambda_H)M_H = W\ell_L + \ell_H
  \label{eq:pool}
\end{equation}
The factor $W$ amplifies the lower group's contribution, consistent
with their greater population mass. Each worker wins the pool with
probability $\tfrac{1}{2}$. The outcome incomes are:
\begin{align}
  M_L^{\mathrm{win}}  &= b_L + (1-\lambda_H)M_H \label{eq:MLwin} \\
  M_L^{\mathrm{lose}} &= b_L - W(1-\lambda_L)M_L \label{eq:MLlose} \\
  M_H^{\mathrm{win}}  &= b_H + W(1-\lambda_L)M_L \label{eq:MHwin} \\
  M_H^{\mathrm{lose}} &= b_H - (1-\lambda_H)M_H \label{eq:MHlose}
\end{align}
The lottery is strictly zero-sum: only upskilling changes the aggregate
income.

\subsection{Participation Constraint and Upskilling Floor}

Each worker must maintain base income above a subsistence floor
$\bar{b}(\mu) = \bar{b}_0 R\mu$, proportional to the lower worker's
wage:
\begin{equation}
  b_s(\lambda_s) \geq \bar{b}(\mu)
  \iff
  \kappa_s + \kappa_s^\gamma \geq \bar{b}_0 R\mu + \delta_w
  \label{eq:pc}
\end{equation}
The minimum upskilling investment satisfying this constraint,
$\kappa^{\min}(\mu)$, solves:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa^{\min} + \left(\kappa^{\min}\right)^\gamma
  = \bar{b}_0 R\mu + \delta_w
  \label{eq:kappa_min}
\end{equation}
For $\gamma = 2$, adopted throughout for closed-form solutions:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa^{\min}(\mu) =
  \frac{-1 + \sqrt{1 + 4(\bar{b}_0 R\mu + \delta_w)}}{2}
  \label{eq:kappa_min_gamma2}
\end{equation}
This is strictly increasing and concave in $\mu$, with sensitivity:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\kappa^{\min}}{d\mu} \equiv \Psi(\mu)
  = \frac{\bar{b}_0 R}{1 + 2\kappa^{\min}(\mu)} > 0
  \label{eq:dkdmu}
\end{equation}
The same sensitivity $\Psi(\mu)$ appears in both the worker upskilling
response (Section~\ref{sec:workers}) and the firm's first-order condition
(Section~\ref{sec:firm}), linking the two sides of the model.

\subsection{The Aggregate Upskilling Index}

The firm internalises the population-weighted aggregate upskilling
contribution of its workforce through the upskilling index:
\begin{equation}
  \Phi(\mu) = \frac{(W+1)\kappa^{\min}(\mu)}{R}
  \label{eq:Phi}
\end{equation}
This is the aggregate human capital return per unit of total income.
The factor $(W+1)$ reflects that there are $W$ lower-rank workers
for every upper-rank worker, so the aggregate upskilling output
per representative pair is $(W+1)\kappa^{\min}(\mu)$ rather than
$2\kappa^{\min}(\mu)$. The derivative:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{d\Phi}{d\mu} = \frac{(W+1)\Psi(\mu)}{R} > 0,
  \qquad
  \frac{d^2\Phi}{d\mu^2} = \frac{(W+1)}{R}\frac{d\Psi}{d\mu} < 0
  \label{eq:Phi_derivs}
\end{equation}
The upskilling index is strictly increasing and strictly concave in $\mu$:
returns to equality are positive but diminishing. A larger $W$ raises
both the level of $\Phi$ and its sensitivity to $\mu$, but does not
affect the workers' individual $\kappa^{\min}(\mu)$ or $\lambda_s^*(\mu)$.

\section{Worker Preferences and Linearly Approximated Risk Premia}

\subsection{Friedman-Savage Utility}

Worker utility uses a cubic Friedman-Savage specification with
inflection point $m^*$:
\begin{equation}
  u(m) = m - \frac{\alpha_u}{2}(m-m^*)^2 + \frac{\beta_u}{3}(m-m^*)^3
  \label{eq:cubic_u}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_u, \beta_u > 0$. The second derivative
$u''(m) = -\alpha_u + 2\beta_u(m-m^*)$ is negative for $m > m^*$
(concave, risk-averse) and positive for $m < m^*$ (convex,
risk-seeking). Worker $L$ with $M_L = R\mu < m^*$ is risk-seeking;
worker $H$ with $M_H = R(1-\mu) > m^*$ is risk-averse.

\subsection{The Linear Approximation of $u''$}

We linearise the second derivative around the subsistence income
$\bar{b}(\mu)$:
\begin{equation}
  u''(m) \approx u''(\bar{b}) + 2\beta_u(m - \bar{b})
  \label{eq:linear_upp}
\end{equation}
Since $u'''(m) = 2\beta_u$ is constant under the cubic specification,
equation~(\ref{eq:linear_upp}) is exact for $u''$ itself. The
approximation enters when we replace $u'(m)$ by $u'(\bar{b})$ in
the Arrow-Pratt coefficient:
\begin{equation}
  A(m) \approx \tilde{A}(m)
  = \frac{-u''(m)}{u'(\bar{b})}
  = \frac{\alpha_u - 2\beta_u(m-m^*)}{u'(\bar{b})}
  \label{eq:AP_linear}
\end{equation}
This is valid when $u'$ does not vary substantially over the lottery
outcome range, holding when prizes are small relative to $|m^* - \bar{b}|$.

\subsection{Linearly Approximated Risk Premia}

We use the following compact notation throughout:
\begin{align}
  a(\mu) &= R\mu - \kappa^{\min}(\mu)
  && \text{(worker $L$ lottery stake)} \label{eq:a} \\
  c(\mu) &= R(1-\mu) - \kappa^{\min}(\mu)
  && \text{(worker $H$ lottery stake)} \label{eq:c} \\
  \Pi(\mu) &= Wa(\mu) + c(\mu) = \Lambda^*
  && \text{(total lottery spread)} \label{eq:Pi} \\
  \epsilon_L(\mu) &= \tfrac{c(\mu) - Wa(\mu)}{2}
  && \text{(net expected transfer to $L$)} \label{eq:epsilonL} \\
  \epsilon_H(\mu) &= -\epsilon_L(\mu)
  && \text{(net expected transfer to $H$)} \label{eq:epsilonH}
\end{align}
The linearly approximated risk premium for worker $s$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\rho}_s = \frac{\Pi(\mu)^2}{8} \cdot
  \frac{\alpha_u - 2\beta_u D_s}{u'(\bar{b})}
  \label{eq:rho_linear}
\end{equation}
where $D_s \equiv \bar{b}(\mu) - m^* + \epsilon_s$ is the deviation of
worker $s$'s expected lottery outcome from the inflection point.
Since $D_L < 0$ and $D_H > 0$:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\rho}_L < 0 \quad \text{(risk-seeking)},
  \qquad
  \tilde{\rho}_H > 0 \quad \text{(risk-averse)}
  \label{eq:rho_signs}
\end{equation}
The net social risk premium collapses to:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{\rho}_L + \tilde{\rho}_H
  = -\frac{\Pi(\mu)^2}{4} \cdot A(\bar{b})
  \label{eq:social_premium}
\end{equation}
The lottery is socially desirable if and only if $A(\bar{b}) < 0$,
i.e.\ $\bar{b}(\mu) < m^*$. Since $\bar{b}(\mu) = \bar{b}_0 R\mu$
rises with $\mu$, there exists a critical threshold:
\begin{equation}
  \mu^\dagger = \frac{m^*}{\bar{b}_0 R}
  \label{eq:mu_dagger}
\end{equation}
above which the lottery mechanism becomes socially costly. This
threshold is independent of $W$: population structure does not affect
the social desirability of the displacement mechanism.

\subsection{Equilibrium Utility}

The worker's expected utility, expanded to second order under the
linear approximation, is:
\begin{equation}
  V_s \approx
  \underbrace{u(\bar{b})}_{\text{subsistence value}}
  + \underbrace{\epsilon_s \cdot u'(\bar{b})}_{\text{expected transfer}}
  + \underbrace{\tilde{\rho}_s}_{\text{risk premium}}
  \label{eq:V_approx}
\end{equation}
The utility parameters $(\alpha_u, \beta_u, m^*)$ enter only through
$\tilde{\rho}_s$, not through the upskilling allocation or asset
accumulation. This clean separation is the key property of the linear
approximation and holds exactly at second order.

\section{Question 1: Worker Responses to Wage Inequality $\mu$}
\label{sec:workers}

\subsection{Optimal Worker Strategies}

Since the participation constraint $b_s \geq \bar{b}$ binds at
equilibrium, the optimal upskilling fractions are:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_L^*(\mu) = \frac{\kappa^{\min}(\mu)}{R\mu},
  \qquad
  \lambda_H^*(\mu) = \frac{\kappa^{\min}(\mu)}{R(1-\mu)}
  \label{eq:lambda_star}
\end{equation}
Both are explicit functions of $\mu$ through
equation~(\ref{eq:kappa_min_gamma2}), and neither depends on $W$. Since
$R\mu < R(1-\mu)$:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_L^*(\mu) > \lambda_H^*(\mu) \quad
  \forall\,\mu \in \left(0,\tfrac{1}{2}\right)
  \label{eq:lambda_ordering}
\end{equation}
The lower-income worker always invests a larger fraction of income in
upskilling because the same absolute floor $\kappa^{\min}$ represents a
larger share of their smaller budget. As $\mu \to \tfrac{1}{2}$ the
fractions converge; as $\mu \to 0$ the gap widens without bound.

\subsection{Upskilling Responses to a Change in $\mu$}

Differentiating equation~(\ref{eq:lambda_star}) with respect to $\mu$:
\begin{align}
  \frac{d\lambda_L^*}{d\mu}
  &= \frac{\Psi(\mu) - \kappa^{\min}(\mu)/\mu}{R\mu}
  \label{eq:dlL_dmu} \\[6pt]
  \frac{d\lambda_H^*}{d\mu}
  &= \frac{\Psi(\mu) + \kappa^{\min}(\mu)/(1-\mu)}{R(1-\mu)}
  > 0 \quad \text{always}
  \label{eq:dlH_dmu}
\end{align}
Worker $H$ always increases their upskilling fraction when equality
rises because both terms in the numerator are positive. For worker $L$,
the sign depends on whether $\Psi(\mu) > \kappa^{\min}(\mu)/\mu$, which
holds when the participation constraint is tight relative to current
upskilling --- the typical case for moderate inequality.

The total upskilling investment $K^*(\mu)$ and its response:
\begin{equation}
  K^*(\mu) = (W+1)\kappa^{\min}(\mu),
  \qquad
  \frac{dK^*}{d\mu} = (W+1)\Psi(\mu) > 0
  \label{eq:dK_dmu}
\end{equation}
Greater wage equality raises total upskilling investment. The response
is amplified by $(W+1)$: a larger lower-rank population magnifies the
economy-wide human capital response to any given change in wage policy.
This amplification is absent from the individual fractions
$\lambda_s^*(\mu)$, which do not depend on $W$, and emerges only at
the aggregate level when population weighting is applied.

\begin{proposition}[Upskilling and population structure]
\label{prop:upskilling}
At the constrained equilibrium:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Individual upskilling fractions $\lambda_L^*(\mu)$ and
        $\lambda_H^*(\mu)$, and the upskilling floor $\kappa^{\min}(\mu)$,
        are all independent of $W$. The population mass ratio does not
        affect individual worker investment decisions.
  \item Total upskilling $K^*(\mu) = (W+1)\kappa^{\min}(\mu)$ is
        increasing in both $\mu$ and $W$. A larger lower-rank population
        amplifies the economy-wide human capital base for any given
        wage policy.
  \item The same amplification factor $(W+1)$ appears in the upskilling
        index $\Phi(\mu) = K^*(\mu)/R$ and the firm's optimal
        $\mu^*$-coefficient $\Gamma \propto (W+1)$, ensuring that firm
        behaviour and aggregate upskilling are consistently linked.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Lottery Stakes and Net Transfer}

The lottery stakes and their responses to $\mu$:
\begin{equation}
  a(\mu) = R\mu - \kappa^{\min}(\mu), \qquad
  c(\mu) = R(1-\mu) - \kappa^{\min}(\mu)
  \label{eq:stakes}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \frac{da}{d\mu} = R - \Psi(\mu) > 0,
  \qquad
  \frac{dc}{d\mu} = -R - \Psi(\mu) < 0
  \label{eq:dstakes}
\end{equation}
As $\mu$ rises, worker $L$'s stake increases (income rises faster than
the upskilling floor) while worker $H$'s falls (income falls and floor
rises). The net expected transfer to worker $L$:
\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_L(\mu) = \frac{c(\mu) - Wa(\mu)}{2}
  = \frac{R(1-\mu) - WR\mu - (1-W)\kappa^{\min}(\mu)}{2}
  \label{eq:transfer_mu}
\end{equation}
is decreasing in $\mu$ when $W > 1$: higher equality reduces worker
$L$'s net lottery receipt because their growing stake means they
contribute proportionally more to the pool. The transfer reversal
threshold $W^\dagger = c(\mu)/a(\mu)$ gives the value of $W$ above
which worker $L$ becomes a net contributor to the pool despite being
the lower-income worker.

\subsection{Risk Premium Responses to $\mu$}

As $\mu$ rises, $\bar{b}(\mu) = \bar{b}_0 R\mu$ increases, moving
both workers toward the inflection $m^*$. Worker $L$'s $D_L$ moves
toward zero from below, so $|\tilde\rho_L|$ falls --- risk-seeking
diminishes as worker $L$ becomes richer. Worker $H$'s $D_H$ moves
further above zero, so $\tilde\rho_H$ rises --- risk-aversion
strengthens as worker $H$'s relative advantage grows. The net social
premium changes sign at $\mu = \mu^\dagger$, above which the
lottery displacement mechanism imposes a net social cost.

\begin{proposition}[Worker responses to wage equality]
\label{prop:worker_responses}
Under the linear approximation of $u''$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Worker $H$ always raises $\lambda_H^*$ when $\mu$ increases.
        Worker $L$ raises $\lambda_L^*$ when the participation
        constraint is sufficiently tight.
  \item Total upskilling $K^*(\mu) = (W+1)\kappa^{\min}(\mu)$ is
        increasing in $\mu$ and in $W$.
  \item Worker $L$'s risk-seeking premium magnitude $|\tilde\rho_L|$
        falls with $\mu$; worker $H$'s risk-aversion premium
        $\tilde\rho_H$ rises with $\mu$.
  \item A threshold $\mu^\dagger = m^*/(\bar{b}_0 R)$, independent of
        $W$, marks the point above which the lottery is socially costly.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\section{Question 2: $\mu$ Constraints on Long-Term Growth}
\label{sec:firm}

\subsection{The Firm's Asset Accumulation Problem}

The firm accumulates assets $A_t$ according to:
\begin{equation}
  A_{t+1} = \left[(1-\pi-\delta_f) + \pi\Phi(\mu_t)\right]A_t
             - K(\mu_t) - C(A_t)
  \label{eq:firm_lom}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{align}
  \Phi(\mu_t) &= \frac{(W+1)\kappa^{\min}(\mu_t)}{R}
  \label{eq:Phi_lom} \\[4pt]
  K(\mu_t) &= k|\mu_t - \mu_0|^\eta, \quad k,\eta > 0
  \label{eq:Kcost} \\[4pt]
  C(A_t) &= c_f A_t^\xi, \quad c_f,\xi > 0
  \label{eq:Ccost}
\end{align}
The wage share $\pi \in (0,1)$ determines what fraction of assets is
paid as wages. The upskilling index $\Phi(\mu_t)$ converts the wage
bill into a productive return: wages paid at split $\mu_t$ induce
upskilling investment $(W+1)\kappa^{\min}(\mu_t)$ across the workforce,
which feeds back into asset growth at rate $\pi$. The restructuring
cost $K(\mu_t) = k|\mu_t-\mu_0|^\eta$ is zero at the market reference
$\mu_0$ and rises convexly with deviation. The maintenance cost
$C(A_t) = c_f A_t^\xi$ is the running cost of a larger asset base.

\subsection{Stackelberg Timing}

The firm solves a Stackelberg problem within each period. It moves
first by choosing $\mu_t$, anticipating workers' best responses
$\lambda_s^*(\mu_t)$. By substituting these best responses into
$\Phi(\mu_t)$ before optimising, the problem reduces to a
single-variable maximisation with no circularity:
\begin{equation}
  \mu_t^* = \arg\max_{\mu_t} \left\{
    \left[(1-\pi-\delta_f) + \frac{\pi(W+1)\kappa^{\min}(\mu_t)}{R}\right]A_t
    - k|\mu_t-\mu_0|^\eta - c_f A_t^\xi
  \right\}
  \label{eq:firm_opt}
\end{equation}
The workers' choices are substituted out, leaving $\Phi(\mu_t)$ as a
known function of $\mu_t$ alone. Cross-period feedback ($A_{t+1}$
informing $\mu_{t+1}^*$) is handled by standard dynamic programming
and introduces no within-period circularity.

\subsection{Firm's First-Order Condition and Optimal $\mu^*$}

Taking the derivative of~(\ref{eq:firm_opt}) with respect to $\mu_t$
and setting to zero, with $\eta = 2$ for closed form and $\Psi$
evaluated at the reference $\Psi_0 \equiv \Psi(\mu_0)$:
\begin{equation}
  \underbrace{\frac{\pi(W+1)\Psi_0}{R} \cdot A_t}_{%
    \substack{\text{marginal gain:} \\ \text{aggregate upskilling return}}}
  =
  \underbrace{2k(\mu_t^* - \mu_0)}_{%
    \substack{\text{marginal cost:} \\ \text{restructuring}}}
  \label{eq:foc}
\end{equation}
The left-hand side is the marginal gain: raising $\mu_t^*$ increases
$\kappa^{\min}$ by $\Psi_0$, total upskilling by $(W+1)\Psi_0$, and
asset returns by $\pi(W+1)\Psi_0 A_t/R$. The right-hand side is the
marginal restructuring cost. Solving:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\mu_t^* = \mu_0 + \Gamma A_t,
  \qquad
  \Gamma \equiv \frac{\pi(W+1)\Psi_0}{2kR}}
  \label{eq:mu_opt}
\end{equation}
The coefficient $\Gamma$ is proportional to $(W+1)$: the firm's
incentive to equalise wages is driven by the population-weighted
aggregate upskilling return, not by any individual worker response.
A larger lower-rank group makes equality more valuable to the firm
because it amplifies the aggregate human capital payoff.

\begin{proposition}[Firm's optimal wage inequality]
\label{prop:firm_mu}
The optimal income split satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\mu_t^* = \mu_0 + \Gamma A_t$: optimal equality is linear in
        the asset stock.
  \item $\mu_t^* > \mu_0$ for all $A_t > 0$: the firm always chooses
        greater equality than the market reference.
  \item $\partial\mu_t^*/\partial A_t = \Gamma > 0$: wealthier firms
        enforce greater equality.
  \item $\partial\mu_t^*/\partial W > 0$: a larger lower-rank population
        pushes the firm toward equality through $(W+1)$ in $\Gamma$.
  \item $\partial\mu_t^*/\partial k < 0$: higher restructuring costs
        reduce optimal deviation from $\mu_0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\subsection{Three Constraints of $\mu$ on Long-Term Growth}

The asset growth rate is:
\begin{equation}
  g(\mu, A) = \pi\Phi(\mu) - \delta_f - c_f
              - \frac{k(\mu-\mu_0)^2}{A}
  \label{eq:growth_rate}
\end{equation}
Three distinct constraints on $\mu$ emerge.

\textbf{Constraint 1 --- Viability floor.}
Setting $\mu = \mu_0$ and $A \to \infty$ gives the maximum achievable
growth rate $g^{\max} = \pi\Phi(\mu_0) - \delta_f - c_f$. A positive
steady state requires:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\pi(W+1)\kappa_0 > R(\delta_f + c_f)}
  \label{eq:viability}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_0 = \kappa^{\min}(\mu_0)$. When this fails, no wage
policy can generate positive growth regardless of $\mu$. The condition
depends on $W$ through $(W+1)$: a larger lower-rank population makes
viability easier to satisfy. Below this threshold the economy has no
positive steady state and assets collapse --- a poverty trap.

\textbf{Constraint 2 --- Restructuring upper bound.}
For the firm to benefit from $\mu_t > \mu_0$:
\begin{equation}
  \pi[\Phi(\mu_t)-\Phi(\mu_0)]A_t > k(\mu_t-\mu_0)^2
  \label{eq:upper_bound}
\end{equation}
This is an asset-dependent upper bound on feasible $\mu_t$. Richer
firms can sustain more redistribution. As $A_t \to A^*$, this
constraint binds exactly at $\mu_t = \mu_t^*$ from
equation~(\ref{eq:mu_opt}), confirming the interior optimum.

\textbf{Constraint 3 --- Hard feasibility ceiling.}
Regardless of asset level, $\mu < \tfrac{1}{2}$: worker $H$ must
always earn more than worker $L$. As $\mu \to \tfrac{1}{2}$ the
upskilling gap $\lambda_L^* - \lambda_H^*$ closes and the lottery
spread $\Pi(\mu) \to 0$, eliminating the displacement mechanism.

\subsection{Closed-Form Steady-State Equilibrium}

Setting $A_{t+1} = A_t = A^*$ and using $\mu^*-\mu_0 = \Gamma A^*$:
\begin{equation}
  A^*[\pi\Phi^* - \delta_f - c_f] = k\Gamma^2 A^{*2}
  \label{eq:ss_eq}
\end{equation}
Dividing by $A^* > 0$ and evaluating $\Phi^*$ at reference $\kappa_0$:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{A^* = \frac{\pi(W+1)\kappa_0/R - \delta_f - c_f}{k\Gamma^2}}
  \label{eq:Astar}
\end{equation}
The full steady state is then computed sequentially. Define three
derived constants from primitives:
\begin{align}
  \kappa_0 &= \frac{-1+\sqrt{1+4(\bar{b}_0 R\mu_0+\delta_w)}}{2}
  \label{eq:k0} \\[4pt]
  \Psi_0 &= \frac{\bar{b}_0 R}{1+2\kappa_0}
  \label{eq:Psi0} \\[4pt]
  \Gamma &= \frac{\pi(W+1)\Psi_0}{2kR}
  \label{eq:Gamma}
\end{align}
Then all equilibrium objects follow without implicit equations:
\begin{alignat}{2}
  &\text{Firm:}\quad
  &A^* &= \frac{\pi(W+1)\kappa_0/R - \delta_f - c_f}{k\Gamma^2}
  \label{eq:Astar2} \\[4pt]
  &&\mu^* &= \mu_0 + \Gamma A^*
  \label{eq:mustar} \\[8pt]
  &\text{Workers:}\quad
  &\kappa^* &= \frac{-1+\sqrt{1+4(\bar{b}_0 R\mu^*+\delta_w)}}{2}
  \label{eq:kstar} \\[4pt]
  &&\lambda_L^* &= \frac{\kappa^*}{R\mu^*},\quad
  \lambda_H^* = \frac{\kappa^*}{R(1-\mu^*)}
  \label{eq:lambdas} \\[4pt]
  &&K^* &= (W+1)\kappa^*, \quad
  a^* = R\mu^*-\kappa^*, \quad
  c^* = R(1-\mu^*)-\kappa^*
  \label{eq:Kacss} \\[4pt]
  &&\Lambda^* &= Wa^*+c^*, \quad
  \epsilon_L^* = \tfrac{c^*-Wa^*}{2}
  \label{eq:Lamss} \\[8pt]
  &\text{Risk premia:}\quad
  &\tilde\rho_s^* &= \frac{\Lambda^{*2}}{8}\cdot
  \frac{\alpha_u - 2\beta_u D_s^*}{u'(\bar{b}^*)}
  \label{eq:rhostar}
\end{alignat}
Every object is an explicit function of the twelve primitives
$(R,\mu_0,W,\bar{b}_0,\delta_w,\pi,\delta_f,k,c_f,\alpha_u,\beta_u,m^*)$.

\subsection{The Endogenous Kuznets Relationship}

At the steady state, $\mu^* = \mu_0 + \Gamma A^*$, so wealthier
economies operate at a more equal wage split. Along the transitional
path, as $A_t$ grows toward $A^*$, $\mu_t^* = \mu_0 + \Gamma A_t$
rises monotonically. The Gini coefficient of income (proxied by
$1-2\mu^*$) falls as the economy grows: an endogenous Kuznets
relationship in which greater wealth generates greater equality through
the firm's optimal wage policy. This relationship is steeper when $W$
is larger, because the aggregate upskilling amplification makes equality
more valuable to richer firms.

\section{Conclusion}

We have developed a tractable three-player model in which wage inequality
$\mu$ is the central instrument connecting worker behaviour and firm
growth, and in which the population mass ratio $W$ enters aggregate
upskilling through the index $\Phi(\mu) = (W+1)\kappa^{\min}(\mu)/R$
without appearing in any individual worker's constraints.

On the first question --- worker responses to $\mu$ --- the main findings
are: worker $H$ always raises their upskilling fraction when equality
rises; total upskilling $K^*(\mu) = (W+1)\kappa^{\min}(\mu)$ rises with
both $\mu$ and $W$, with the amplification factor $(W+1)$ determined
entirely by population structure and absent from the individual fractions
$\lambda_s^*(\mu)$; worker $L$'s risk-seeking premium falls in magnitude
as $\mu$ rises; a threshold $\mu^\dagger = m^*/(\bar{b}_0 R)$ marks the
point above which the lottery displacement mechanism is socially costly.

On the second question --- $\mu$ constraints on growth --- the main
findings are: the viability condition $\pi(W+1)\kappa_0 > R(\delta_f+c_f)$
is the hard floor, with $W$ making viability easier to satisfy; the
optimal $\mu^* = \mu_0+\Gamma A^*$ is linear in asset wealth, generating
an endogenous Kuznets relationship; the steady-state asset level $A^*$
is fully explicit and rises with $W$, $\pi$, and $\delta_w$, and falls
with $k$ and $\delta_f$.

The key structural insight unifying both answers is that $W$ enters
the model at two different levels. At the individual level it is absent:
workers' upskilling investments and fractions are determined by the
participation constraint alone. At the aggregate level it amplifies:
the firm's return to raising $\mu$ is $(W+1)$ times the per-worker
upskilling sensitivity, so a more populous lower-rank group makes both
viability easier to satisfy and optimal equality more aggressive. This
two-level structure --- individual neutrality, aggregate amplification
--- is the model's central mechanism.

\section{Derivation of Optimal Upskilling Fractions $\lambda_L^*$ and $\lambda_H^*$}
\label{app:foc}

This appendix shows in full detail how the optimal upskilling fractions
$\lambda_L^*$ and $\lambda_H^*$ in equation~(\ref{eq:lambda_star}) are
derived. The result does not come from setting a first-order condition
equal to zero in the usual interior sense. Instead, the FOC reveals that
the objective is monotone in $\lambda_s$, which forces the solution to
the boundary defined by the participation constraint. We show this in
six steps.

\subsection*{A.1\quad The Worker's Problem}

Worker $s \in \{L, H\}$ with income $M_s$ chooses $\lambda_s \in [0,1]$
to maximise expected utility:
\begin{equation}
  V_s(\lambda_s;\lambda_{-s})
  = \frac{1}{2}\,u\!\left(b_s(\lambda_s) + \Pi_{-s}\right)
  + \frac{1}{2}\,u\!\left(b_s(\lambda_s) - W_s\ell_s(\lambda_s)\right)
  \tag{A.1}
  \label{eq:Vs_full}
\end{equation}
subject to the participation constraint:
\begin{equation}
  b_s(\lambda_s) \geq \bar{b}(\mu) = \bar{b}_0 R\mu
  \tag{A.2}
  \label{eq:pc_app}
\end{equation}
where the components are:
\begin{align}
  \kappa_s &= \lambda_s M_s
  && \text{(upskilling investment)} \tag{A.3} \\
  b_s(\lambda_s) &= \kappa_s + \kappa_s^\gamma - \delta_w
  = \lambda_s M_s + (\lambda_s M_s)^\gamma - \delta_w
  && \text{(base income after upskilling)} \tag{A.4} \\
  \ell_s(\lambda_s) &= (1-\lambda_s)M_s
  && \text{(lottery stake)} \tag{A.5}
\end{align}
The prize $\Pi_{-s}$ is the other worker's stake, which is fixed from
worker $s$'s perspective since $\lambda_{-s}$ is taken as given. The
lottery weight is $W_L = W \geq 1$ for worker $L$ and $W_H = 1$ for
worker $H$.

\subsection*{A.2\quad The Unconstrained First-Order Condition}

Differentiating $V_s$ with respect to $\lambda_s$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial V_s}{\partial\lambda_s}
  = \frac{1}{2}\,u'(b_s + \Pi_{-s})\cdot\frac{\partial b_s}{\partial\lambda_s}
  + \frac{1}{2}\,u'(b_s - W_s\ell_s)
    \cdot\frac{\partial(b_s - W_s\ell_s)}{\partial\lambda_s}
  \tag{A.6}
  \label{eq:dV_dlambda}
\end{equation}
Computing each derivative in turn. Since $b_s = \lambda_s M_s +
(\lambda_s M_s)^\gamma - \delta_w$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial b_s}{\partial\lambda_s}
  = M_s\left(1 + \gamma\kappa_s^{\gamma-1}\right) > 0
  \tag{A.7}
  \label{eq:dbs_dlambda}
\end{equation}
Since $W_s\ell_s = W_s(1-\lambda_s)M_s$, we have
$\partial(W_s\ell_s)/\partial\lambda_s = -W_s M_s$, so:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial(b_s - W_s\ell_s)}{\partial\lambda_s}
  = \frac{\partial b_s}{\partial\lambda_s} + W_s M_s
  = M_s\left(1 + W_s + \gamma\kappa_s^{\gamma-1}\right) > 0
  \tag{A.8}
  \label{eq:dlose_dlambda}
\end{equation}
Substituting back into equation~(\ref{eq:dV_dlambda}):
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial V_s}{\partial\lambda_s}
  = \frac{M_s(1+\gamma\kappa_s^{\gamma-1})}{2}\,u'(b_s+\Pi_{-s})
  + \frac{M_s(1+W_s+\gamma\kappa_s^{\gamma-1})}{2}\,u'(b_s-W_s\ell_s)
  \tag{A.9}
  \label{eq:dV_full}
\end{equation}
Since $u'(\cdot) > 0$ everywhere, $M_s > 0$, $W_s \geq 1$, and
$\gamma\kappa_s^{\gamma-1} > 0$, every term in equation~(\ref{eq:dV_full})
is strictly positive. Therefore:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial V_s}{\partial\lambda_s} > 0
  \quad \text{for all } \lambda_s \in [0,1)
  \tag{A.10}
  \label{eq:monotone}
\end{equation}
The objective $V_s$ is strictly increasing in $\lambda_s$. There is no
interior critical point. The unconstrained optimum is the corner
$\lambda_s = 1$.

\subsection*{A.3\quad Why Both Lottery Outcomes Improve with $\lambda_s$}

The monotonicity in~(\ref{eq:monotone}) has a clear economic
interpretation. As $\lambda_s$ rises (more income to upskilling):
\begin{itemize}
  \item The \textbf{win outcome} $M_s^{\mathrm{win}} = b_s + \Pi_{-s}$
        improves because $b_s$ rises via
        equation~(\ref{eq:dbs_dlambda}) while $\Pi_{-s}$ is fixed.
  \item The \textbf{lose outcome} $M_s^{\mathrm{lose}} = b_s - W_s\ell_s$
        improves by even more: $b_s$ rises and simultaneously
        $W_s\ell_s = W_s(1-\lambda_s)M_s$ falls, so both additive
        components move favourably.
\end{itemize}
There is no upskilling-lottery tradeoff in terms of final outcomes.
Raising $\lambda_s$ strictly dominates lowering it in both states of the
world. The only force that prevents $\lambda_s = 1$ is the participation
constraint, to which we now turn.

\subsection*{A.4\quad The Constrained KKT Conditions}

Introduce a Lagrange multiplier $\xi_s \geq 0$ on the participation
constraint~(\ref{eq:pc_app}). The Lagrangian is:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_s
  = V_s(\lambda_s;\lambda_{-s})
  + \xi_s\!\left[b_s(\lambda_s) - \bar{b}\right]
  \tag{A.11}
  \label{eq:lagrangian}
\end{equation}
The KKT stationarity condition in $\lambda_s$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial V_s}{\partial\lambda_s}
  + \xi_s\,\frac{\partial b_s}{\partial\lambda_s} = 0
  \tag{A.12}
  \label{eq:kkt_stat}
\end{equation}
Substituting equations~(\ref{eq:dV_full}) and~(\ref{eq:dbs_dlambda}),
and factoring out $M_s(1+\gamma\kappa_s^{\gamma-1}) > 0$:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{u'(b_s+\Pi_{-s}) + u'(b_s - W_s\ell_s)}{2} + \xi_s = 0
  \tag{A.13}
  \label{eq:kkt_simplified}
\end{equation}
Solving for the multiplier:
\begin{equation}
  \xi_s = -\frac{u'(b_s+\Pi_{-s}) + u'(b_s - W_s\ell_s)}{2}
  \tag{A.14}
  \label{eq:multiplier}
\end{equation}
Since $u'(\cdot) > 0$ always, the right-hand side is strictly negative:
$\xi_s < 0$. But the KKT conditions require $\xi_s \geq 0$ for a
$\geq$ constraint. This is a contradiction. The contradiction means there
is no feasible interior point satisfying the KKT conditions --- the
participation constraint must bind with equality.

\subsection*{A.5\quad The Binding Constraint Determines $\lambda_s^*$}

Since $V_s$ is strictly increasing in $\lambda_s$ and the participation
constraint prevents $\lambda_s = 1$, the worker pushes $\lambda_s$
as high as feasible. The constraint binds at equality:
\begin{equation}
  b_s(\lambda_s^*) = \bar{b}
  \iff
  \kappa_s^* + (\kappa_s^*)^\gamma - \delta_w = \bar{b}
  \iff
  \kappa_s^* + (\kappa_s^*)^\gamma = \bar{b} + \delta_w
  \tag{A.15}
  \label{eq:binding_pc}
\end{equation}
where $\kappa_s^* = \lambda_s^* M_s$. This is the equation defining
$\kappa^{\min}$, the minimum upskilling investment required to reach
subsistence. For the closed-form case $\gamma = 2$:
\begin{equation}
  (\kappa^{\min})^2 + \kappa^{\min} = \bar{b} + \delta_w
  \implies
  \kappa^{\min} = \frac{-1 + \sqrt{1 + 4(\bar{b} + \delta_w)}}{2}
  \tag{A.16}
  \label{eq:kmin_solve}
\end{equation}
Since $\kappa_s^* = \lambda_s^* M_s = \lambda_s^* R M_s^0$, the
optimal upskilling fraction is:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_s^* = \frac{\kappa^{\min}}{R M_s^0}
  \tag{A.17}
  \label{eq:lambda_star_app}
\end{equation}
For worker $L$ with $M_L^0 = \mu$ and worker $H$ with $M_H^0 = 1-\mu$:
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \lambda_L^* = \frac{\kappa^{\min}}{R\mu},
  \qquad
  \lambda_H^* = \frac{\kappa^{\min}}{R(1-\mu)}
  }
  \tag{A.18}
  \label{eq:lambda_star_final}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{A.6\quad Why $\lambda_H^* > \lambda_L^*$ Always}

Since $\mu < \tfrac{1}{2}$ we have $R\mu < R(1-\mu)$, so the denominator
of $\lambda_L^*$ is smaller than the denominator of $\lambda_H^*$:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_L^* = \frac{\kappa^{\min}}{R\mu}
  > \frac{\kappa^{\min}}{R(1-\mu)} = \lambda_H^*
  \tag{A.19}
  \label{eq:lambda_ordering_app}
\end{equation}
The lower-income worker always invests a larger \emph{fraction} of
income in upskilling. This is not a preference difference --- both
workers solve the same binding constraint --- but an income-scaling
effect: the same absolute floor $\kappa^{\min}$ represents a larger
share of a smaller income.

\subsection*{A.7\quad Where Risk Preferences Enter}

A natural question is: if both workers have the same binding constraint
and the same $\kappa^{\min}$, where does the Friedman-Savage risk
asymmetry between them appear? The answer is that risk preferences
do not determine $\lambda_s^*$ --- they determine the value of the
lottery participation, captured by the risk premium $\tilde\rho_s$ in
equation~(\ref{eq:rho_linear}).

Specifically, worker $L$ participates in the lottery mechanism (at the
stake level $\ell_L^* = (1-\lambda_L^*)M_L$) because the convex
curvature of utility at $b_L < m^*$ makes the gamble attractive:
$\tilde\rho_L < 0$ means worker $L$ receives a premium for bearing
risk. Worker $H$ participates because displacement is not fully
avoidable, and their concave utility at $b_H > m^*$ means $\tilde\rho_H > 0$:
they pay a premium to participate.

Neither premium affects the optimal $\lambda_s^*$ because the risk
premium correction $\tilde\rho_s$ is additive in $V_s$ (see
equation~(\ref{eq:V_approx})) and does not feed back into the
participation constraint. This is the key property of the linear
approximation of $u''$: it preserves the sign asymmetry of risk
preferences while keeping the allocation problem separable from the
preference problem.

\subsection*{A.8\quad Summary of the Derivation Chain}

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.4}
\begin{tabular}{cl}
\toprule
Step & Result \\
\midrule
A.2 & $\partial V_s/\partial\lambda_s > 0$ always: no interior optimum \\
A.3 & Both win and lose outcomes improve with $\lambda_s$ \\
A.4 & KKT multiplier $\xi_s < 0$: constraint must bind \\
A.5 & $b_s(\lambda_s^*) = \bar{b}$ gives $\kappa^{\min}$;
      closed form for $\gamma = 2$ \\
A.6 & $\lambda_s^* = \kappa^{\min}/RM_s^0$;
      ordering $\lambda_L^* > \lambda_H^*$ from income scaling \\
A.7 & Risk preferences enter via $\tilde\rho_s$,
      not via $\lambda_s^*$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

\noindent The optimal $\lambda_s^*$ is determined entirely by the
binding participation constraint, not by equating marginal upskilling
benefit to marginal lottery benefit. The FOC's role is to confirm
there is no interior solution, thereby directing the solution to the
constraint boundary.
